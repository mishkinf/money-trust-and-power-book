# Glossary of Key Terms

This glossary provides quick definitions for technical and historical terms used throughout the book. Terms are organized alphabetically for easy reference.

---

## A

**Aerarium**: The Roman state treasury. Maintained detailed accounts of tax revenues and public expenditures, demonstrating continuity of credit-based accounting from ancient temples through classical civilization.

**Agio**: Premium or fee charged for exchanging one currency for another. The Amsterdam Wisselbank's banco guilder traded at 4-5% agio over physical coins, reflecting its superior reliability and convenience.

**Amargi**: Ancient Sumerian term meaning "return to mother" or "freedom." Debt cancellation proclamations issued periodically by Mesopotamian rulers to prevent debt bondage and social collapse. Early evidence that money was a political technology requiring periodic resets to maintain stability.

**Arbitrage**: Profiting from price differences in different markets. Under bimetallic systems, merchants could exploit differences in gold-silver ratios between countries, driving undervalued metal out of circulation (Gresham's Law).

**Assignat**: Paper currency issued by revolutionary France (1789–1797), initially backed by confiscated church lands. Massive overissuance led to hyperinflation; replaced at 30:1 ratio. Cautionary example of fiat money without institutional discipline.

**Austrian School**: School of economic thought emphasizing individual choice, spontaneous market order, and commodity origins of money. Founded by Carl Menger (1871); Ludwig von Mises's Regression Theorem (1912) argued money must originate from commodity with prior use-value, contradicting chartalist state-creation theory.

## B

**Bancor**: Supranational currency proposed by John Maynard Keynes at Bretton Woods (1944). Would have been issued by International Clearing Union, defined in gold but not convertible to it. Rejected by U.S. in favor of dollar-based system. Name from French "banque or" (bank gold).

**Bank Charter Act of 1844**: British legislation that divided the Bank of England into two departments (Issue and Banking), mandated that new note issuance be fully backed by gold, and prohibited new banks from issuing notes. Intended to control money creation but failed by not recognizing deposits as money.

**Bank Restriction Act (1797)**: British law suspending the Bank of England's obligation to redeem notes in gold during Napoleonic Wars. Lasted 24 years (1797–1821), proving paper money could function successfully without metal backing when institutions were credible.

**Banking School**: Nineteenth-century British monetary doctrine opposing the Currency School. Held that bank notes did not need rigid gold backing because convertibility-on-demand and the "real bills" doctrine would automatically limit issuance to the legitimate needs of trade. Key figures: Thomas Tooke, John Fullarton, James Wilson (founder of *The Economist*), and the parliamentary opposition led by Benjamin Hawes. Lost the Bank Charter Act fight in 1844 despite intellectual sophistication; vindicated when the Act had to be suspended during crises in 1847, 1857, and 1866.

**Bimetallism**: Monetary system where both gold and silver serve as legal tender at a government-set exchange ratio. Britain operated on bimetallism before Newton's 1717 adjustment accidentally created a de facto gold standard.

**Bills of Exchange**: Credit instruments used by merchants to settle debts in distant cities without moving gold. Pioneered by Italian banking families in medieval period; became backbone of 18th-19th century international trade when centered in London.

**Bretton Woods System**: International monetary regime (1944–1971) that fixed exchange rates by pegging currencies to U.S. dollar (±1%) and dollar to gold ($35/oz). Collapsed when U.S. abandoned gold convertibility in 1971.

**Bullion**: Precious metal in bulk form (bars or ingots), not yet coined. Amsterdam Wisselbank accepted bullion deposits and credited accounts in standardized banco guilders, creating early form of bank money independent of physical coins.

**Buchanan-Wagner Critique / *Democracy in Deficit* (1977)**: James Buchanan and Richard Wagner's institutional argument that once the technical solvency constraint on a sovereign government is removed, democratic political incentives push toward chronic deficits. Voters underestimate the future tax and inflation costs of deficit finance; politicians prefer salient tax cuts and spending increases over diffuse-cost surpluses; the old "fiscal constitution" of balanced peacetime budgets gets replaced by technocratic discretion that systematically biases toward overspending. Strongest version of the case against discretionary fiscal policy that does not depend on metallism. Engaged seriously in Chapter 12B alongside the modern Modern Monetary Theory tradition's response, which proposes the federal job guarantee as a built-in countercyclical mechanism that addresses the same political-economy worry through stabilizers rather than rules.

## C

**Canon Law**: Church legal system governing religious and moral matters. Canon lawyers eventually accepted bills of exchange as legitimate despite usury prohibitions, enabling medieval merchant finance.

**Central Bank**: Institution that manages a nation's currency, money supply, and monetary policy. Early examples include Sweden's Riksbank (1668), Bank of England (1694), and Banque de France (1800). Modern central banks typically have monopoly on currency issuance and act as lender of last resort.

**Chartalism / State Theory of Money**: Theory that money's value derives from state's power to impose taxes payable in that currency, not from intrinsic commodity value. Articulated by Georg Friedrich Knapp (1905), adopted by Keynes (1930), and forms foundation of Modern Monetary Theory. From Latin "charta" (token).

**Clearing**: Process of settling mutual debts by offsetting claims rather than moving actual money. Antwerp and Amsterdam merchant bankers met weekly to "strike the books," canceling debts and paying only residual differences—ancestors of modern clearinghouses.

**Commodity Money**: Currency whose value derives from the material it contains (gold, silver, etc.). Contrasts with credit money or fiat money, whose value depends on institutional backing and acceptance.

**Consols**: British government perpetual bonds (consolidated annuities) paying fixed interest indefinitely with no maturity date. Became benchmark for British government debt in 18th-19th centuries; low rates demonstrated Parliament's credibility as borrower.

**Convertibility**: Ability to exchange paper money for commodity (usually gold) at fixed rate. Bank Restriction Act (1797-1821) suspended convertibility, proving paper could function without metal backing. Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility in 1971, completing transition to pure fiat.

**Corvée Labor**: Unpaid labor owed to lord or state as form of taxation. Medieval peasants paid dues "in kind"—grain, wool, or corvée days worked on lord's fields—demonstrating money's role as unit of account even when physical currency scarce.

**Country Banks**: Private partnerships (1700-1844) that issued their own banknotes across England. Over 700 existed by 1810, creating most of Britain's money supply and financing Industrial Revolution. Prohibited from issuing new notes by Bank Charter Act 1844; last private note issued 1921.

**Currency School**: Nineteenth-century British monetary doctrine that won the fight over the Bank Charter Act of 1844. Held that bank-note issue should be tied mechanically to gold reserves to prevent over-issue, removing discretion from both Bank and Treasury. Defensible response to the genuine threat of unconstrained inconvertible paper that France's assignats had demonstrated. Key figures: David Ricardo, Samuel Jones Loyd (Lord Overstone), Robert Torrens, Sir Robert Peel. Their decisive error, invisible until the financial crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866, was equating control of the note issue with control of the money supply — bank deposits, which expanded on top of any given note base, lay outside their model. The orthodoxy they consolidated outlived the metallic constraint that originally justified it.

**Creditor-Debtor Dynamics**: The systematic wealth transfer that occurs during deflation (benefits creditors) or inflation (benefits debtors). During deflation, fixed debts become harder to repay as money becomes more valuable; during inflation, debts become easier to repay as money becomes less valuable.

**Currency Sovereignty**: Ability of a government to issue and control its own currency, denominate debts in that currency, and maintain independent monetary policy. Japan possesses full currency sovereignty (yen-denominated debt, floating exchange rate); Argentina lacks it (dollar-denominated external debt). Countries with sovereignty cannot involuntarily default on own-currency obligations; those without face hard external constraints.

## D

**Darien Scheme / Company of Scotland (1695–1700)**: Scottish colonization venture targeting the Isthmus of Darien (modern Panama) to short-circuit the East Indies trade. The Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies raised £400,000 from Scottish subscribers — roughly a quarter of all liquid capital in the kingdom. William Paterson, founder of the Bank of England, was its principal projector and sailed with the second expedition; his wife and son died there along with nearly two thousand other colonists. The catastrophic failure helped push Scotland toward the 1707 Act of Union. The companion case study to the Bank of England in Chapter 5: same projector, same speculative engine, opposite institutional foundations.

**Debasement**: Reducing precious metal content in coins while maintaining face value. Common practice of medieval and early modern monarchs facing fiscal pressure; typically destroyed currency credibility and led to inflation.

**Deflation**: A sustained decrease in the general price level. Under deflation, the real burden of debt increases (it takes more goods/labor to repay the same nominal debt), systematically transferring wealth from debtors to creditors.

**Deposit Creation**: Process by which commercial banks create money when making loans. Bank credits borrower's account with new deposit without transferring existing funds—"loans create deposits." Confirmed empirically by Richard Werner (2014) and Bank of England (2014), refuting money multiplier theory.

## E

**Endogenous Money**: Theory that money supply is determined by demand for credit, not by central bank control of reserves. Banks create deposits when making loans; central banks accommodate by supplying reserves at target interest rate. Articulated by Post-Keynesian economists (Kaldor, Moore, Minsky); confirmed by Bank of England 2014.

**Equivalent (1707)**: England's £398,085 payment to Scotland under the Act of Union, ostensibly to compensate Scottish creditors for assuming a share of English public debt. In practice it covered most of the Darien Scheme losses and was prominent among the financial inducements that delivered Scottish parliamentary support for the Union. William Paterson, who had sailed with the Darien expedition and lost his wife and son there, was prominent among the Scottish negotiators who pressed for it.

**Exchequer**: Royal treasury and accounting office in medieval England. Used tally sticks to record tax payments and loans; name derives from checkered cloth on which accounts were calculated with counters.

**Exorbitant Privilege**: Term coined by French Finance Minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing (1965) for advantages U.S. enjoys from dollar's reserve currency status. Includes ability to sustain persistent current account deficits ($819 billion in 2023), borrow at artificially low rates (46-50 basis points suppression per Federal Reserve research), and print currency accepted globally. Barry Eichengreen's research shows net benefit approximately 1% of U.S. GDP.

## F

**Fiat Money**: Currency that derives value from government decree and taxation authority rather than commodity backing. The term comes from Latin "fiat" (let it be done). All major currencies have been pure fiat since 1971.

**Financial Revolution**: Term for England's late 17th-century transformation of public finance following Glorious Revolution (1688). Parliament gained control of taxation and borrowing, created Bank of England (1694), and established funded public debt backed by parliamentary taxes.

**Fiscal Capacity**: A government's ability to tax its population and collect revenue. Strong fiscal capacity—backed by effective administration and rule of law—enables governments to create credible currency. Parliamentary control of taxation after 1688 gave England superior fiscal capacity compared to absolutist France, enabling cheaper borrowing despite smaller economy.

**Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (FTPL)**: Theoretical framework, sharpened by Sargent and Wallace's "unpleasant monetarist arithmetic" (1981) and generalized by John Cochrane (*The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level*, 2023), holding that the price level is determined by the present value of expected future government surpluses, not by money supply alone. Implies that large deficits unbacked by credible expected surpluses must eventually be offset by either explicit default or inflation, regardless of central-bank independence. Engaged seriously alongside the public-choice critique in Chapter 12B as the strongest non-metallist case against unconstrained discretionary fiscal policy.

**Florin**: Gold coin first minted by Florence in 1252 at 3.5 grams pure gold. Never debased; became trusted international currency alongside Venetian ducat. Example of how stable currencies spread through market competition.

**Foreign Currency Trap**: Situation where countries borrow in currencies they cannot print (typically dollars), creating hard external constraints. When export revenue falls or currency devalues, real debt burden increases while government cannot create the foreign currency needed for service. Argentina's nine crises since 1980 demonstrate this pattern.

**Fractional Reserve Banking**: System where banks hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves while lending out the rest. Discovered accidentally by English goldsmith-bankers (1640s-1670s). Modern banks create deposits directly through lending, not by "multiplying" reserves.

**Functional Finance**: Economic doctrine (Abba Lerner, 1943) that government should judge fiscal policy by economic effects, not budget balance. Government should spend enough to buy all producible goods, can create money to meet obligations, and taxes control inflation rather than "fund" spending. Briefly influential 1940s-1960s; marginalized after stagflation.

## G

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**: Total value of goods and services produced in economy. Used to measure economic size and compare debt burdens across time.

**Giro Transfer**: Direct transfer between accounts without physical money moving. Medieval merchant families balanced debts through giro in common ledgers, transforming money from matter into information transmitted by courier.

**Glorious Revolution (1688)**: Overthrow of English King James II by Parliament, installing William III and Mary II under constitutional constraints. Shifted sovereignty from monarch to Parliament, transformed public finance by tying royal borrowing to parliamentary taxation.

**Gold Convertibility**: Ability to exchange paper money for fixed amount of gold. Bank of England suspended in 1797 to finance Napoleonic Wars; restored 1821. U.S. ended foreign official convertibility in 1971, completing global transition to fiat money.

**Gold Points**: The upper and lower limits on exchange rate movements determined by the cost of physically shipping gold between countries. Under classical gold standard (1870-1914), gold points typically ranged 0.4-1.4% of par value. Within gold points, exchange rates fluctuated; outside them, gold flowed automatically to correct imbalances.

**Gold Standard**: Monetary system where currency is convertible to a fixed amount of gold on demand. Classical gold standard (1870–1914) featured automatic adjustment of trade imbalances through specie flows. Britain operated on a gold standard from 1717 (de facto) or 1821 (de jure) until 1914, and briefly from 1925-1931. Provided long-run price stability but limited domestic policy flexibility and imposed deflationary bias.

**Gold Window**: System where foreign governments could exchange dollars for gold at fixed rate ($35/oz). Core of Bretton Woods (1944-1971). Nixon closed gold window August 15, 1971, when U.S. gold reserves insufficient to meet conversion demands.

**Gresham's Law**: Principle that overvalued money drives undervalued money out of circulation. When two forms of money circulate at fixed ratio differing from market value, arbitrage drives out the undervalued form. Named for Thomas Gresham (1519-1579); mechanism explains how Newton's 1717 guinea revaluation drove silver from England.

## H

**Hierarchy of Money**: Framework (Stephanie Kelton, 2001) showing state-issued currency sits atop pyramid, with bank deposits second tier, and private credit instruments below. Position in hierarchy determined by acceptability—state money most acceptable because required for tax payments. Explains why private money creation depends on state foundation.

**Household Budget Metaphor**: Misleading analogy treating government finances like household budgets. Popularized by Margaret Thatcher ("housewife's purse") and Ronald Reagan. Obscures that sovereign currency issuers face different constraints than currency users—governments that issue own money cannot "run out" but face inflation and real resource limits instead.

## I

**Impossible Trinity (Trilemma)**: Fundamental constraint in international finance: a country can have any TWO of the following, but not all THREE: (1) Fixed exchange rate, (2) Independent monetary policy, (3) Free capital flows. Also called the Mundell-Fleming framework. Explains why Bretton Woods collapsed (countries chose policy flexibility over fixed rates) and why eurozone design created structural problems (gave up monetary independence for currency union).

**In Kind**: Payment with goods or services rather than money. Medieval peasants paid taxes in kind (grain, wool, labor). Demonstrates money's function as unit of account can exist independently of physical currency.

## J

**Jiaozi**: World's first government-issued paper currency, introduced by Song Dynasty China (1023 CE). Government mandated use in tax payments—early fiat system operating centuries before European paper money. By 1204, 4.14 million notes circulated backed by only 700,000 coins.

**Job Guarantee**: Policy proposal central to the modern Modern Monetary Theory tradition (developed most fully by Pavlina Tcherneva, building on work by Hyman Minsky, L. Randall Wray, and Warren Mosler) for a federally funded employment program that offers a fixed-wage job to anyone willing to work. Functions as a built-in countercyclical stabilizer: outlays automatically expand in slumps as private demand contracts and workers take the guaranteed-employment wage; outlays automatically contract in booms as workers leave for higher-paying private work. The MMT tradition's affirmative answer to the Buchanan-Wagner public-choice critique — addressing inflation-control through structural mechanism rather than through binding fiscal rules. Engaged in Chapter 12B as the stabilizer-based answer to the institutional question the framework owes its critics.

## L

**Lender of Last Resort**: Central bank's function to provide emergency liquidity to financial institutions during crises. Prevents solvent but illiquid banks from failing due to panics. The Bank of England pioneered this role; the Federal Reserve was created in 1913 partly to serve this function.

**Liquidity**: Readily available money or assets quickly convertible to cash. Central banks act as lender of last resort, providing emergency liquidity during crises to prevent bank runs from becoming systemic failures.

**London Gold Pool**: Arrangement among eight central banks (1961-1968) to defend gold's $35/ounce official price by coordinating sales. U.S. contributed 50%. Accumulated $1.4 billion surplus 1962-1965, then suffered $3.7 billion deficit 1965-1968 as speculators tested the system. Disbanded March 1968 after hemorrhaging reserves, replaced by two-tier gold market. Collapse foreshadowed Bretton Woods' end three years later.

## M

**Metallism / Commodity Theory**: The view that money must be backed by precious metals (gold or silver) to have value. Economists believing money must originate from commodity value. Argues that money is valuable because of what it *is* (commodity with intrinsic value) rather than the relationship it represents. Contradicts chartalist view that state authority creates money through taxation.

**Mint Ratio**: Official price at which mint exchanges gold for silver (or vice versa). Isaac Newton's 1717 undervaluation of silver relative to gold accidentally drove silver from circulation and placed Britain on gold standard.

**Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)**: Contemporary economic framework synthesizing chartalist tradition (Knapp, Mitchell-Innes, Keynes) with functional finance (Lerner) and endogenous money (Minsky, Moore). Core insights: taxation drives currency demand, sovereign issuers can't run out of own money, real resources (not finance) constrain economies. Founded by Warren Mosler (1993); developed by Wray, Kelton, Mitchell. Governments that issue their own fiat currency face different constraints than households—cannot "run out of money" but are constrained by real resources (productive capacity) rather than finance. Inflation, not insolvency, is the risk of excessive spending.

**Monetary Sovereignty**: Condition where government issues its own currency, sets tax obligations in that currency, and faces no external convertibility requirement. Sovereign issuers (U.S., Japan, UK) face different constraints than currency users or countries with foreign-currency debt. Central to MMT framework.

**Money Multiplier**: Discredited theory that banks "multiply" central bank reserves through sequential lending. Taught in textbooks for decades despite being empirically false. Bank of England (2014) confirmed banks create deposits directly when lending, not by multiplying reserves. Reserves supplied by central bank to accommodate lending, not constrain it.

## N

**Niskanen / Budget-Maximizing Bureaucrats**: William Niskanen's analysis of bureaucratic incentives (*Bureaucracy and Representative Government*, 1971), arguing that public agencies have structural reasons to expand budgets regardless of macroeconomic conditions because information asymmetries between agencies and legislators allow them to do so. A central plank of the public-choice critique of discretionary fiscal policy: even setting aside deficit politics, the supply side of government spending biases upward. Engaged in Chapter 12B alongside Buchanan-Wagner as part of the rules-based tradition's case for institutional pre-commitment; the chapter presents the rules-based response (sunset clauses, performance-based budgeting) and the stabilizer-based response (the job guarantee as automatic countercyclical mechanism) as the two main traditions answering the public-choice critique.

**Nixon Shock**: President Richard Nixon's August 15, 1971 announcement suspending dollar convertibility to gold, ending Bretton Woods system. Presented as "temporary" measure but became permanent. Triggered by Britain's August 11 request to convert $3 billion in reserves. U.S. gold reserves had fallen to $10 billion while foreign dollar liabilities reached $80 billion (12.5% backing). Marked final break from gold standard and beginning of pure fiat era.

## O

**Open Market Operations**: Central bank buying/selling of government securities to influence money supply and interest rates. Primary tool of modern monetary policy under fiat currency regime.

**Output Gap**: The difference between an economy's actual GDP and potential GDP at full capacity. Central to understanding when money creation causes inflation. When output gap is negative (recession, unemployment, idle factories), money creation mobilizes unused resources without inflation—as occurred during 2008 crisis when Fed created $3.6 trillion with minimal price increases. When output gap is positive (economy at/above capacity), additional money creation bids up prices for scarce goods, causing inflation. The constraint on government spending is productive capacity and inflation, not nominal budget balance.

## P

**Parliamentary Sovereignty**: Principle that Parliament is supreme lawmaking authority. In British context, established by Glorious Revolution; crucial for credible public debt because lenders knew Parliament controlled taxation.

**Procyclical Policy**: Policy amplifying economic cycles rather than dampening them. Real Bills Doctrine prescribed procyclical monetary policy: as production fell during Great Depression, "needs of trade" legitimately declined, so Fed contracted money supply—deepening the crisis catastrophically.

**Productive Capacity**: Economy's ability to produce real goods and services given available labor, capital, technology, and resources. The real constraint on money creation—creating money beyond productive capacity causes inflation; creating money to mobilize unused capacity does not. Hyperinflation results from destruction of productive capacity (Venezuela oil -77%, Weimar coal/steel -80%, Zimbabwe agriculture -50%), not money creation per se. When capacity intact, massive monetary expansion produces minimal inflation (Japan, US 2008-2020, UK 1797-1821).

**Projector**: Late-Stuart and Williamite English term for someone who proposed grand financial or commercial schemes — joint-stock companies, public banks, life-insurance arrangements, river-navigation projects, lotteries, friendly societies. The 1690s in particular were saturated with projectors of widely varying competence and honesty. Daniel Defoe's *An Essay upon Projects* (1697) distinguished the "honest projector" (real innovation under real risk) from the "mere projector" (predatory speculation living on others' gullibility). William Paterson, founder of the Bank of England and principal projector of the Darien Scheme, worked at the boundary between the two.

**Public Choice**: School of economic analysis applying standard rationality assumptions to political behavior. Treats voters, politicians, and bureaucrats as self-interested actors operating within institutional constraints rather than benevolent maximizers of public welfare. Founded by James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock (*The Calculus of Consent*, 1962); central insight that the gold standard's defenders had partly right — that you cannot trust office-holders to stop printing when inflation arrives — survives the move to fiat. The discipline must come from rules and institutions rather than from a metal that imposes its own painful costs whether warranted or not. Engaged in Chapter 12B.

## Q

**Quantitative Easing (QE)**: Central bank purchases of government bonds and other assets to expand money supply when interest rates near zero. Fed's balance sheet grew from $870 billion (2007) to $4.5 trillion (2015). Despite massive expansion, core inflation remained below 2%—demonstrating productive capacity, not money quantity, constrains inflation. QE increases central bank reserves but doesn't automatically create bank credit to real economy. Asset purchases can inflate financial asset prices without causing consumer price inflation if money circulates within financial markets rather than financing productive investment or household consumption.

**Quantity Theory of Money**: The theory that money supply growth directly determines inflation, often expressed as MV = PY (Money × Velocity = Price Level × Output). Assumes velocity is stable and output is fixed at full employment. David Ricardo championed this theory to support gold standard orthodoxy.

## R

**Real Bills Doctrine**: Theory that banks should only lend against "real goods" in production or transit, not for speculation. Argues that such lending is "self-liquidating" and cannot cause inflation. Adam Smith's "water-pond" metaphor (1776) suggested self-liquidating trade bills automatically regulate currency. Henry Thornton (1802) demolished doctrine by showing interest rates matter. Thoroughly discredited after the Great Depression, when Federal Reserve adherence to this doctrine deepened the crisis.

**Regression Theorem**: Ludwig von Mises's 1912 argument that money's value today depends on yesterday's value, traced back to original commodity use. Claims purely fiat money is logically impossible because people need a reason to initially accept it. Presents logical challenge to chartalist theory: how does purely abstract money acquire initial value? Chartalist answer: state power to tax creates value from start.

**Reserve Ratio**: Fraction of deposits that banks must hold as reserves (cash or central bank deposits). Tool for controlling money supply; higher ratios constrain credit creation.

## S

**Sargent-Wallace Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic (1981)**: Result, due to Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace, showing that if a fiscal authority runs persistent primary deficits without credible future surpluses, monetary restraint cannot ultimately prevent inflation: the central bank must either monetize the debt eventually or watch real debt service costs rise until fiscal collapse forces the issue. Central-bank independence becomes nominal under that constraint. Foundational to the modern Fiscal Theory of the Price Level. The strongest pure-rationality argument that fiscal discipline matters even under fiat; engaged in Chapter 12B.

**Seigniorage**: The profit government makes from creating money—difference between face value and production cost. For example, if it costs $0.05 to produce a $100 bill, seigniorage is $99.95. Historically, monarchs debased coins to increase seigniorage. Modern governments earn seigniorage by creating currency and deposits. Represents government's ability to create purchasing power through money creation.

**Shadow Banking**: Financial institutions and instruments that perform bank-like functions (credit creation, maturity transformation) outside traditional banking regulation. Includes money market funds, repo markets, asset-backed commercial paper, securitization. Reached $20 trillion by 2007, exceeding traditional banking. The 2008 crisis demonstrated that shadow banking could create systemic risk comparable to traditional banking.

**Specie**: Physical coins made of precious metal (gold or silver). Distinguished from paper currency or electronic money. The term comes from Latin "species" (kind, type). Under gold standard, "specie flows" between countries automatically adjusted trade imbalances as metal moved to settle accounts.

**Sticky Wages**: Wages that don't adjust quickly to economic changes. Henry Thornton (1802) warned that rapid monetary contraction creates "unusual and temporary distress" because prices and wages don't adjust instantly—deflation causes real economic pain.

## T

**Tally Sticks**: Wooden sticks used as receipts for tax payments in medieval England (circa 1100-1826). Split lengthwise with notches indicating amounts—one half (stock) kept by taxpayer, other half (foil) kept by Exchequer. Could circulate as state-backed credit instrument. Demonstrated that taxation authority creates currency demand without commodity backing.

**Tax-Farming**: Selling right to collect taxes to private contractors who keep portion as profit. French monarchy relied on tax farmers (*fermiers généraux*) who kept 30-40% of gross receipts. England's parliamentary system collected taxes through salaried officials at 3-5% cost—superior fiscal capacity despite smaller economy.

**Taxation-Driven Money**: Concept (prominent in Modern Monetary Theory) that fiat currency derives value from government's requirement that taxes be paid in it. Creates baseline demand independent of currency's intrinsic worth; explains how domestic credit instruments like tally sticks maintained value.

**Temple Ledgers**: Clay tablets used by ancient Mesopotamian temples and palaces to record grain debts and credits. Earliest form of money-as-accounting; demonstrates credit preceded commodity money by millennia.

**Triffin Dilemma / Triffin's Impossible Position**: The contradiction inherent in a national currency serving as global reserve currency, identified by Robert Triffin (1959). The country must run persistent deficits to supply global liquidity to the world, but persistent deficits undermine confidence in the currency. Named for economist Robert Triffin, who predicted in 1960 that this would doom the Bretton Woods system. U.S. dollar liabilities exceeded gold reserves by 1960s, leading to Nixon's 1971 decision.

**Two-Tier Gold Market**: System created March 1968 after London Gold Pool collapse. Official tier: central banks traded gold among themselves at $35/ounce, agreed not to supply private markets from official stocks. Private tier: market forces determined price (opened April 1 at $38/ounce, reached $42.60 by May). Tacit admission that Bretton Woods' $35 price was unsustainable. System limped along until Nixon closed gold window August 1971, ending pretense of convertibility.

## U

**Unit of Account**: Function of money as standard measure of value. Can exist independently of physical medium; ancient societies measured value in silver shekels by weight long before coinage.

**Usury**: Charging interest on loans, forbidden by medieval Church law as sinful. Merchants circumvented prohibition through bills of exchange that disguised interest as "exchange fees." Protestant Reformation (especially Calvin) reinterpreted interest as legitimate "rent on capital," enabling modern finance.
