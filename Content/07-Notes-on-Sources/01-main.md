# Notes on Sources

These notes describe where the book's claims come from, where contested questions sit in the historiography, and where the chapter has chosen one defensible interpretation over another. They are organized by chapter and, within each chapter, by topic. The discursive form follows Liaquat Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* and Adam Tooze's *Crashed* rather than the numbered-footnote style: a reader who wants to verify any specific claim can find the source, and a reader who wants to know how the historiographic debate sits can find that too.

## Chapter 1 — The Birth of Credit

### The Mesopotamian credit-theory tradition

The chapter's framing of Mesopotamian temple economies as "palatial balance-sheet economies" follows Michael Hudson's work, particularly *...and Forgive Them Their Debts: Lending, Foreclosure and Redemption from Bronze Age Finance to the Jubilee Year* (Dresden: ISLET, 2018) and the volumes of the International Scholars' Conference on Ancient Near Eastern Economies (ISCANEE) edited by Hudson and others. Hudson's interpretation reads the surviving cuneiform record as evidence of recurring, systematic balance-sheet stabilization through royal debt-cancellation edicts (*amargi*, *andurarum*, *misharum*). Marc Van De Mieroop's *The Business of Business* and *Society and Enterprise in Old Babylonia* offer a more cautious reading, in which these edicts were episodic political acts responding to specific royal crises rather than a standing institutional mechanism. Both readings have substantial scholarly support; the chapter follows Hudson's framing because it captures what the surviving record best supports about *recurrence* of the practice while flagging the interpretive choice in the body text.

### Ur III administrative scale

The figures cited for the Ur III *bala* taxation system — roughly 188 million liters of grain mobilized annually, roughly 500 administrators coordinating Puzrish-Dagan, redistribution to a substantial non-food-producing workforce — derive from Piotr Steinkeller's prosopographic reconstruction of the surviving administrative tablets. See Steinkeller's contributions to *Labor in the Ancient World* and his work on Ur III administration. These are reconstructed estimates from sampled records, not census certainties, and the chapter presents them as such.

### The Larsa loan tablet (Year 21 of Rīm-Sîn I, c. 1801 BCE)

The narrative of Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn's barley loan reconstructs the transaction recorded on tablet Kress 83 / P370944 (Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative), preserved in a private collection in Bochum, Germany. The market-acquisition origin makes the tablet's exact findspot within Larsa unknown. Partial damage to the reverse leaves some witness names incomplete; the reconstruction follows the conventional reading of the surviving cuneiform, with some elements restored from contextual parallels with other Old Babylonian loan contracts of the period. The 33⅓ percent annual interest rate for barley loans is the standard rate codified in Old Babylonian legal practice, including the Code of Hammurabi.

### Song dynasty *jiaozi* circulation, 1204

The figures of roughly 4 million notes in circulation against approximately 700,000 iron coins in reserve at the height of *jiaozi* depreciation in 1204 trace through Richard von Glahn, *Fountain of Fortune: Money and Monetary Policy in China, 1000–1700* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1996), p. 49. Von Glahn's discussion connects to the earlier Chinese-language scholarship of Peng Xinwei, *Zhongguo Huobi Shi (A Monetary History of China)*, p. 393, and the standard English-language reference of Yang Lien-sheng, *Money and Credit in China: A Short History* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard-Yenching Institute, 1952), p. 32. The figures are widely cited in the modern monetary-history literature; the chapter follows the consensus.

## Chapter 2 — Merchants of Trust

### *Cambium non est mutuum* and the canon-law accommodation of the bill of exchange

The legal fiction by which medieval merchant bankers profited on bills of exchange while remaining technically within the Church's prohibition on usury is described in Raymond de Roover, *Money, Banking and Credit in Mediaeval Bruges: Italian Merchant-Bankers, Lombards and Money-Changers — A Study in the Origins of Banking* (Cambridge, MA: Mediaeval Academy of America, 1948). The technical mechanics of paired *cambium* and *recambium* contracts — and of the *cambium siccum* or "dry exchange" that contemporaries openly understood as a loan — are most fully laid out in Peter Spufford, *Handbook of Medieval Exchange* (London: Royal Historical Society, 1986). The exchange-rate mechanics that produced the lender's profit on the round trip are documented across the Datini archive (Archivio di Stato di Prato, Fondo Datini), which preserves roughly 150,000 letters and 500 account books from Francesco di Marco Datini's merchant-banking operations.

### Italian banking institutional evolution: super-companies to Medici to Fugger

The structural distinction between the fourteenth-century Florentine super-companies (Peruzzi, Bardi) and the later Medici Bank — extended partnerships of family capital giving way to limited-liability branch structures with separate *libri segreti* — is the central argument of Raymond de Roover, *The Rise and Decline of the Medici Bank, 1397–1494* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1963). Richard Goldthwaite, *The Economy of Renaissance Florence* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 2009), offers the most recent comprehensive treatment of Florence's financial development. The Fugger of Augsburg's later vertical integration into mining and metals — and their financing of the Habsburgs in exchange for mining concessions — is treated in Mark Häberlein, *The Fuggers of Augsburg: Pursuing Wealth and Honor in Renaissance Germany* (Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2012).

### The Bardi/Peruzzi collapse, 1340s

The chapter's account of the Peruzzi and Bardi failures in the 1340s follows the traditional narrative in which Edward III of England's default on his foreign debts triggered the cascade. Peruzzi exposure to the English crown is conventionally placed at roughly 400,000 gold florins and Bardi exposure at roughly 600,000 — sums many times the annual revenue of the English crown. These figures derive from Edwin S. Hunt, *The Medieval Super-Companies: A Study of the Peruzzi Company of Florence* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1994), and have been refined in subsequent revisionist work, including Armando Sapori's earlier scholarship on the Italian super-companies. More recent scholarship has questioned whether Edward's default was the *cause* of the houses' collapse or the most visible *trigger* of a deterioration already underway across the 1340s — driven by Florentine political instability, declining Mediterranean trade, and competitive pressure from rising rivals. The chapter's narrative frames Edward's default as the immediate trigger while acknowledging that the underlying weaknesses had been accumulating.

## Chapter 3 — The Sovereign's Dilemma

### Tally sticks and the medieval English Exchequer

The chapter's account of the tally-stick system — notched hazel rods split lengthwise so that debtor and creditor each held a matching record, with redeemed tallies cancelled at final reckoning — follows the standard treatment in Hilary Jenkinson, "An Exchequer Tally," *Archaeologia* 62 (1911): 367–380, and the more recent reconstruction in James Macdonald, *A Free Nation Deep in Debt: The Financial Roots of Democracy* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2003). The "spend, tax, destroy" sequence — that the crown issued tallies in advance of revenue, accepted them back in payment of taxes, and cancelled the matched halves on redemption — is documented across the medieval Exchequer literature. The 1834 fire that destroyed the medieval Palace of Westminster, in which the accumulated tally archive was incinerated, is treated in Caroline Shenton, *The Day Parliament Burned Down* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2012). Charles Dickens's well-known 1855 mockery of the tally bureaucracy ("the official routine") appears in his speech to the Administrative Reform Association on June 27, 1855.

### The Sign of the Unicorn: Backwell pre-1672

The chapter's pre-1672 reconstruction of Edward Backwell's banking operations at the Sign of the Unicorn — the Lombard Street geography, the shop interior, the 1671 customer ledger, his exposure to the Crown — draws on D.M. Mitchell, "To Alderman Backwells for the candlsticks," *Silver Society Journal* 12 (2000): 111–124; Stephen Quinn's 1994 University of Illinois doctoral dissertation on London goldsmith banking; the NatWest Group Heritage Hub's Edward Backwell archives (Archives Hub reference GB1502-EB); John Strype's 1720 *Survey of the Cities of London and Westminster*; and the Pepys diary entries (Robert Latham and William Matthews, eds., *The Diary of Samuel Pepys*, London: Bell, 1971–1983). The chapter draws particularly on Pepys's entries of June 17, 1662 (the chest of crusados, Don Duarte de Silva's perfumed comfits), April 12, 1669 (Backwell's "little town" of Lombard Street holdings), July 23, 1666 (the "good master, the King" line that the next decade would render tragic), and November 15, 1667 (the Sheerness "vain glory" remark in the aftermath of the Dutch raid on the Medway). The figure of approximately £365,000 in Crown debt accumulated by 1670 derives from Backwell's surviving ledgers as analyzed in Quinn (1994) and the NatWest archive material. The 1666 Great Fire descriptions are drawn from Thomas Vincent's *God's Terrible Voice in the City* (1667) and from Pepys's contemporaneous diary entries.

### Greece 2010 as Edward III's dilemma

The chapter's closing parallel — that Greece in 2010 ran into the same domestic-versus-foreign-credit asymmetry Edward III had run into in 1340, because Greece had surrendered its currency-issuing power to the eurozone — follows Adam Tooze, *Crashed: How a Decade of Financial Crises Changed the World* (New York: Viking, 2018), Part III. The yield-spread arithmetic (Germany at roughly 2 percent, Greece at roughly 30 percent in summer 2015) is documented in eurozone bond-market data of the period.

## Chapter 4 — When Private Money Failed

### Charles I's 1640 mint seizure and the goldsmith opportunity

The chapter's account of Charles I's 1640 seizure of £200,000 from the Royal Mint — and of the subsequent emergence of Lombard Street goldsmiths as an alternative depository for wealthy Londoners — follows Robert Ashton, *The Crown and the Money Market, 1603–1640* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1960). The mechanics of goldsmith banking that emerged in the 1640s and 1650s, including the development of bearer receipts during the Civil War, are most fully treated in Stephen Quinn, "Goldsmith-Banking: Mutual Acceptance and Interbank Clearing in Restoration London," *Explorations in Economic History* 34, no. 4 (1997): 411–432, and in the same author's 1994 PhD dissertation. Henry Roseveare, *The Financial Revolution, 1660–1760* (London: Longman, 1991), provides the institutional context for the Restoration-era development.

### The 1672 Stop of the Exchequer: documentary record

The detailed Treasury minute-book material on the January 1672 emergency meetings — the Friday January 5 attendance list, the Sunday January 7 four-o'clock meeting, the bankers' January 12 plea for quarterly interest payments and protection from creditor arrests, the January 19 stripping of Backwell's appointment as receiver of the Queen's Portuguese revenues — is drawn from the *Calendar of Treasury Books*, Volume III: 1669–1672 (HMSO). Bishop Gilbert Burnet's contemporary judgment ("the bankers were broken, and multitudes who had put their money in their hands were ruined by this dishonourable and perfidious action") appears in Burnet, *History of My Own Time*, ed. Osmund Airy (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1897). The narrative of Backwell's life in the decade after the Stop — the continued operation of the Unicorn, the Treasury work that took him to the Netherlands in 1674 and 1680, the partial payments arriving in 1675 and across 1680–85, the 1682 bankruptcy declaration, the June 13, 1683 death in the Netherlands, the 1685 family reburial at Tyringham, and the 1698 private Act of Parliament settling the estate — is reconstructed from the NatWest Group Heritage Hub's Backwell archives (GB1502-EB), the parish records of St. Mary Woolnoth, and the relevant entries in the *Calendar of Treasury Books* for the period.

### The 1701 Appropriation of Revenue Act and the legal aftermath

The legal-settlement arithmetic — interest reduced from 6 percent to 3 percent in 1701, further reduced to 2.5 percent in 1716, and the modern reconstruction yielding the 0.01 percent effective annual return calculation — follows P.G.M. Dickson, *The Financial Revolution in England: A Study in the Development of Public Credit, 1688–1756* (London: Macmillan, 1967), and the modern reconstruction in Quinn (1994). The Court of Exchequer's 1692 ruling, the 1696 reversal on technical grounds, and the House of Lords' January 1700 final judgment are documented in J. Keith Horsefield, *British Monetary Experiments, 1650–1710* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1960).

### Lehman 2008 as 1672 in modern dress

The chapter's parallel between the 1672 collapse and the September 2008 failure of Lehman Brothers — both as cases of private credit creation operating without a public-backstop institution — follows Tooze, *Crashed*, particularly chapters 5–7. The Federal Reserve's October 2008 emergency lending facilities and money-market backstops are documented in Ben S. Bernanke, *The Federal Reserve and the Financial Crisis* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2013).

## Chapter 5 — The Bank of England Solution

### William Paterson: primary writings and the Darien arc

The chapter's reconstruction of William Paterson — his Cameronian background, his Caribbean years, his 1691 Privy Council bank proposal, his role in the 1694 founding, his post-Bank involvement with the Company of Scotland, and the Darien expedition that killed his wife and son — draws on *The Writings of William Paterson, Founder of the Bank of England, and of the Darien Colony*, edited by Saxe Bannister (3 vols., London: Effingham Wilson, 1858–1859), which remains the principal collection of his primary materials. The institutional history of the Bank's founding follows John Clapham, *The Bank of England: A History* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1944), Volume I, and the UK Debt Management Office's official history of the Bank's establishment. The Darien material draws on Douglas Watt, *The Price of Scotland: Darien, Union and the Wealth of Nations* (Edinburgh: Luath Press, 2007), and on the contemporary records of the Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies preserved at the National Records of Scotland.

### The "money out of nothing" attribution

The widely quoted line that the Bank "hath benefit of interest on all moneys which it creates out of nothing," conventionally attributed to Paterson, has a contested transmission history. The phrase appears in pamphlet form in defenses of the Bank published in the late 1690s and is widely cited across the subsequent literature, but the attribution to Paterson personally — as opposed to one of the Bank's anonymous defenders writing in his idiom — has been questioned at least since the nineteenth century. The chapter follows the conventional attribution while flagging in the body that "the precise attribution has been contested in the historiography since the nineteenth century." The historiographic caution is reinforced by the standard advice that the most-cited Paterson lines should be checked against the Bannister collected writings before being used as primary citations.

### The Backwell payoff: the Bank as the architecture Backwell tried to build

The chapter's closing reflection — that the Bank of England's 1694 charter delivered the architecture Backwell had tried to build privately and could not, eleven years after his 1683 death in Netherlands exile — uses the same documentary base as the Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 entries above. The institutional contrast between the Bank's parliamentary backing and the goldsmith system's reliance on personal Crown credit is the central structural argument running through Dickson (1967) and the modern Bank of England historical literature.

### Two-tier banking and the country banks

The chapter's account of the two-tier system — Bank of England notes as base money, country banks pyramiding deposits and notes on top — follows L.S. Pressnell, *Country Banking in the Industrial Revolution* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1956), the standard treatment of the eighteenth-century English country banking system. The 1810 figure of approximately 700 country banks circulating roughly £22 million in notes is widely cited and traces through Pressnell and the Bank of England's own institutional records. The eventual 1844 Bank Charter Act consolidation of the framework is treated in greater detail in the Chapter 8 sources below.

## Chapter 6 — Newton's Accident

### The 1717 mint ratio adjustment: "accident" or deliberate?

The chapter follows the scholarly consensus that Isaac Newton's 1717 mint ratio adjustment was intended to maintain a working bimetallic standard rather than to establish a gold-monometallic regime. The conventional account — that the actual outcome (silver's drainage from circulation, the de facto gold standard that emerged over the following decades) was an unintended consequence of a technical mint report — is supported across the modern monetary-history literature. Some monetary historians, including the older institutional-history tradition represented by Sir Albert Feavearyear's *The Pound Sterling* (2nd ed., Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963) and Sir John Craig's *Newton at the Mint* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1946), argue for a more deliberate reading: that Newton's adjustment was an informed response to a known global silver-gold price differential, and that contemporaries understood the likely consequences better than the "accident" framing suggests. The recent work of Richard Kleer on Newton's monetary administration has refined the technical detail. The chapter's framing distinguishes intent from outcome: the mint adjustment was technically informed and the recommendation was deliberate, but the regime that emerged — gold's dominance of British circulation, the eventual nineteenth-century gold standard — was not what Newton proposed or what contemporaries publicly anticipated.

### The classical era's achievements

The "What the classical era delivered" section's three claims — multi-decade price-level stability, capital exports averaging 4 to 5 percent of GDP and peaking near 9 percent in 1913, and roughly 60 percent of world trade settled in sterling by 1913 — are drawn respectively from B.R. Mitchell, *British Historical Statistics* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1988), and the Sauerbeck-Statist wholesale price index for the price-level claim; from A.G. Ford and the Imlah-Cain-Hopkins literature on British capital exports, with the modern reconstruction in Maurice Obstfeld and Alan M. Taylor, *Global Capital Markets: Integration, Crisis, and Growth* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2004), Chapter 2; and from Marc Flandreau and Clemens Jobst's work on the international currency hierarchy in the late nineteenth century, plus the standard treatment in Catherine Schenk, *The Decline of Sterling: Managing the Retreat of an International Currency, 1945–1992* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2010). The metallist tradition the section credits — Bordo and Schwartz on the central-banking side, Selgin and White on the free-banking side, Saifedean Ammous in the cryptocurrency adaptation — is most fully developed in Michael D. Bordo and Anna J. Schwartz, eds., *A Retrospective on the Classical Gold Standard, 1821–1931* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984); George Selgin, *Less Than Zero: The Case for a Falling Price Level in a Growing Economy* (London: Institute of Economic Affairs, 1997); and Saifedean Ammous, *The Bitcoin Standard: The Decentralized Alternative to Central Banking* (Hoboken: Wiley, 2018).

## Chapter 7 — Paper Proves Itself

### The Bank Restriction Act, 1797–1821

The chapter's reconstruction of the February 1797 suspension of gold convertibility — the French Fishguard landing, the Privy Council's emergency session, the Bank's depleted reserves (£1 million against £10.86 million in notes outstanding by February 1797) — draws on Frank Whitson Fetter, *Development of British Monetary Orthodoxy, 1797–1875* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1965), the standard institutional history. The merchants' February 28, 1797 declaration of confidence in Bank notes is preserved in the Bank of England archives. The figure of £10.9 million in note circulation in 1797 rising to £28.4 million by 1814, against the inflation that ran roughly 30–40 percent across the period, is documented in John Clapham, *The Bank of England: A History*, Volume II.

### Henry Thornton and the Bullionist controversy

The treatment of Thornton draws principally on Henry Thornton, *An Enquiry into the Nature and Effects of the Paper Credit of Great Britain* (London: Hatchard, 1802), available in the standard Friedrich Hayek edition (London: George Allen & Unwin, 1939; reprinted 1991), with Hayek's introduction explicitly framing the work as the foundational text of modern central-banking theory. The chapter's reading of Thornton's two-interest-rate model as the source from which Knut Wicksell's "cumulative process" account (in *Geldzins und Güterpreise*, 1898; English translation as *Interest and Prices*, 1936) derives, and through which Keynes's *Treatise on Money* (1930) and the *General Theory* (1936) inherit a fundamentally Thornton-derived monetary architecture, follows Robert Skidelsky, *John Maynard Keynes, Volume Two: The Economist as Saviour, 1920–1937* (London: Macmillan, 1992), and the standard secondary literature on Thornton, including Lloyd Mints, *A History of Banking Theory in Great Britain and the United States* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1945). The 1810 Bullion Report's findings are reproduced in Edwin Cannan, ed., *The Paper Pound of 1797–1821* (London: P.S. King, 1925).

### Attwood and the post-1815 deflation

The chapter's framing of Thomas Attwood as the industrial witness whose Birmingham manufactory experience between 1817 and 1822 produced *Prosperity Restored* (1817) and *The Currency Question* (1819) follows the historiographic literature catalogued in the Chapter 8 sources below — particularly Asa Briggs's 1948 article and David Moss's 1990 biography — applied to the earlier period when Attwood was first formulating his critique of the resumption-era monetary contraction. The detailed accounts of post-1815 farmer insolvencies (Samuel Gigney of Latchington, the Devon farmers, Robert Leach of Halberton) are drawn from the *London Gazette*'s insolvency notices for 1822–1824 and from Boyd Hilton, *Corn, Cash, Commerce: The Economic Policies of the Tory Governments, 1815–1830* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1977). William Cobbett's 1822 Southern England tour is documented in Cobbett, *Rural Rides* (London: William Cobbett, 1830).

### The Real Bills Doctrine and its Federal Reserve afterlife

The Real Bills Doctrine's intellectual genealogy from Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) through its embedding in the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 to its role in the 1929–33 monetary contraction follows Mints (1945) and, on the Federal Reserve afterlife, Allan H. Meltzer, *A History of the Federal Reserve, Volume 1: 1913–1951* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2003). Bernanke's 2002 remarks at the conference honoring Milton Friedman's ninetieth birthday are at the Federal Reserve Board's archived speeches site (November 8, 2002).

### The 2020 Federal Reserve mobilization

The chapter's closing parallel — between the Bank Restriction's twenty-four years of fiat money and the Federal Reserve's March 2020 emergency response, in which the balance sheet expanded from $4 trillion to $7 trillion within months without producing the predicted hyperinflation — follows Tooze, *Crashed* (for the 2008 baseline) and the Federal Reserve's own *2020 Annual Report* for the COVID-era operations. The H.4.1 statistical release (Factors Affecting Reserve Balances of Depository Institutions) provides the weekly balance-sheet record.

## Chapter 8 — The Great Forgetting

### Attwood and the Birmingham School: the proto-Keynesian question

The chapter's qualified treatment of Thomas Attwood — directionally right about money's non-neutrality, but not the worked-out theorist of demand management that the "proto-Keynesian" laurel sometimes implies — follows the historiographic caution running from Asa Briggs, "Thomas Attwood and the Economic Background of the Birmingham Political Union," *Cambridge Historical Journal* 9, no. 2 (1948): 190–216, through Henry Miller, "Radicals, Tories, or Monomaniacs? The Birmingham Currency Reformers in the House of Commons, 1832–67," *Parliamentary History* 31, no. 3 (2012): 354–377, and James A. Moss, *Thomas Attwood: The Biography of a Radical* (Montreal & Kingston: McGill-Queen's University Press, 1990). Briggs's note 73 contains the locus classicus: "It would be unhistorical to call Attwood's analysis 'quasi-Keynesian'... but it is interesting to note that there are undoubted similarities of approach." Miller's reassessment frames the Birmingham currency men as "popular radicals" whose monetary program was a political project rather than a theoretical breakthrough. Moss reads Attwood's later work as a "rationalization designed to hide a sense of desperation" rather than a worked-out theoretical advance. The chapter's framing tries to capture what is genuinely vindicated about Attwood — that money quantity matters for employment, that monetary contraction causes real suffering, that paper money can function without gold backing — without overclaiming the analytical machinery that came later.

### Currency School institutional capture

The institutional-capture narrative in Chapter 8 — Lord Overstone's £2 million fortune, George Warde Norman's 51-year directorship, the Norman family's 123-year continuity at the Bank of England, Overstone's advisory relationships with successive Chancellors, his control of Political Economy Club membership — rests on the project's research file *Currency School Institutional Capture in 1820s–1850s Britain*. The file includes the relevant primary correspondence (Overstone-Norman letters; Charles Wood's 1847 letter crediting Overstone with "the Act of 1844"; Gladstone's "most Socratic document" praise; Lord John Russell's consultations) along with the contextual qualifier that the Currency School "captured Parliament but lost the press" — James Wilson's *The Economist*, founded 1843, opposed the 1844 Act and continued to advocate Banking School positions. The chapter frames the institutional-capture argument honestly within these limits: the Currency School won the political contest decisively, but the contest was not silenced, and the historiographic literature continues to debate how much of the alignment between Currency School theory and bondholder interests was design and how much was the ordinary working of an institutional consensus.

### "Was the worry justified?": the Currency School's vindication on its own terms

The new section's twentieth- and twenty-first-century cases — Weimar Germany 1921–23, Hungary 1945–46, Argentina 1975–91, Brazil through 1994, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela 2017, Turkey 2021–24 — are documented in standard monetary-history reference works. The Weimar narrative follows Adam Fergusson, *When Money Dies: The Nightmare of Deficit Spending, Devaluation, and Hyperinflation in Weimar Germany* (London: William Kimber, 1975); the Hungary case is treated in Michael Hudson and Cornelia Wunsch, *Creating Economic Order: Record-Keeping, Standardization, and the Development of Accounting in the Ancient Near East* (Bethesda: CDL Press, 2004), and in the standard Eastern European monetary-history literature; the South American cases in Carlos Diaz-Alejandro, "Stories of the 1930s for the 1980s," in *Financial Policies and the World Capital Market: The Problem of Latin American Countries*, ed. Pedro Aspe Armella, Rudiger Dornbusch, and Maurice Obstfeld (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983); the Turkish case in current IMF and Brookings analytical work.

## Chapter 9 — The Interwar Catastrophe

### Churchill's 1925 return to gold and *Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill*

The reconstruction of the March 17, 1925 dinner at 11 Downing Street and Churchill's April 28, 1925 budget announcement follows Robert Skidelsky, *John Maynard Keynes, Volume 2: The Economist as Saviour, 1920–1937* (London: Macmillan, 1992), Chapter 7, and Robert Boyce, *British Capitalism at the Crossroads, 1919–1932* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1987). The exchange-rate arithmetic ($4.86 official parity against the market rate of $4.77, the implied 10 percent overvaluation) is documented across the Bank of England's archived monetary-policy materials. Reginald McKenna's "There is no escape... You have to go back. But it will be hell" remark is reported in P.J. Grigg, *Prejudice and Judgement* (London: Jonathan Cape, 1948), the memoir by Churchill's private secretary at the time. Keynes's *The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* (London: Hogarth Press, 1925) is the primary text; the three Evening Standard articles published July 22–24, 1925, are collected in *The Collected Writings of John Maynard Keynes*, Volume 9 (Royal Economic Society / Cambridge University Press), pp. 207–230. The "Why should coal-miners suffer a lower standard of life?" passage appears in the same volume at p. 223.

### The 1926 General Strike and the Todd family

The chapter's reconstruction of the Todd family's experience at Boldon Colliery during the 1926 lockout draws on documentary materials from the Durham Mining Museum, the Beamish Museum's coalfield social-history archives, and the family-memoir literature on the strike preserved in the People's History Museum collection, Manchester. The contemporary newspaper record of the seventeen-month lockout is preserved in *The Times* (the Conservative-aligned coverage), the *Manchester Guardian* (the Liberal coverage), and the *Daily Herald* (the Labour coverage). The Durham County Council's free-school-meal program (£250,000 expenditure) and the Wesleyan Chapel soup-kitchen operations are documented in W.H. Crook, *The General Strike* (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1931), and in subsequent Durham social-history scholarship. The strike's national arc — the May 3–12, 1926 General Strike, the TUC's May 12 capitulation, the November 1926 miners' return at the lower wage — is treated in G.A. Phillips, *The General Strike: The Politics of Industrial Conflict* (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1976), and Patrick Renshaw, *Nine Days in May: The General Strike* (London: Eyre Methuen, 1975).

### Gold sinks: France, the United States, and the Great Depression's monetary mechanism

The chapter's gold-hoarding narrative — France's share of world gold reserves rising from 7 percent in 1926 to 27 percent by 1932, U.S. gold sterilization 1928–30, the price-level decline of 34 percent between 1928 and 1932 with roughly 40 percent attributed to the two countries' hoarding — follows Barry Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters: The Gold Standard and the Great Depression, 1919–1939* (New York: Oxford University Press, 1992), and Douglas Irwin, "Did France Cause the Great Depression?" *NBER Working Paper No. 16350* (September 2010). The 5 billion gold francs Franco-Prussian indemnity that funded Germany's 1871 transition to gold (which the chapter cross-references to its Chapter 6 treatment) is documented in Marc Flandreau, *The Glitter of Gold: France, Bimetallism, and the Emergence of the International Gold Standard, 1848–1873* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004).

### Iowa farm material: Farmer A and the foreclosure record

The narrative of "Farmer A" — the 311-acre Story County, Iowa operator who borrowed $90,000 in March 1920 and lost both farms to foreclosure by 1928 — is drawn from William G. Murray, *An Economic Analysis of Farm Mortgages in Story County, Iowa, 1854–1931* (Ames: Iowa Agricultural Experiment Station Bulletin 156, March 1933). Murray anonymized the family but documented the case down to the dollar from contemporary courthouse records. The aggregate national farm-foreclosure data — the rise from 3.2 per 1,000 farms in 1913–1920 to 17.4 per 1,000 by 1926 to 38.8 per 1,000 by 1933, with more than 200,000 farms foreclosed in 1933 alone — is from Lee J. Alston, "Farm Foreclosure Moratorium Legislation: A Lesson from the Past," *American Economic Review* 74, no. 3 (June 1984): 445–457.

### Lebesque's winter, 1932–33

Morvan Lebesque's Métro-bench experience in the winter of 1932–33 is drawn from Lebesque's own *Comment peut-on être Breton? Essai sur la démocratie française* (Paris: Éditions du Seuil, 1970), particularly the autobiographical passages in which he reconstructs his early years in Paris. The biographical chronology — *Loire Echo* editor at nineteen, the 1932 move to Paris, the 1932–33 winter on Métro benches, the eventual reestablishment in journalism, the long *Le Canard Enchaîné* career — is documented across the standard French biographical material on Lebesque.

### Roosevelt's 1933 break from gold

The March 1933 bank holiday, Executive Order 6102 (the April 5, 1933 ban on private gold ownership), and the January 30, 1934 Gold Reserve Act devaluing the dollar to $35 per ounce of gold are documented in Friedman and Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1963), Chapter 8, and in Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters*, Chapter 12. The recovery arithmetic — money supply growth of 10 percent annually 1933–1937, GDP growth averaging over 8 percent annually, unemployment falling from 25 percent to 14.3 percent in Roosevelt's first year — follows Christina D. Romer, "What Ended the Great Depression?" *Journal of Economic History* 52, no. 4 (December 1992): 757–784.

## Chapter 10 — Bretton Woods and the Bancor That Wasn't

### Interpretive choice: Steil over Helleiner

The interpretation in this chapter — that the bancor's defeat at Bretton Woods reflected procedural and power-political control by the United States rather than the institutional superiority of White's design — follows Benn Steil, *The Battle of Bretton Woods: John Maynard Keynes, Harry Dexter White, and the Making of a New World Order* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2013) more closely than it follows Eric Helleiner, *States and the Reemergence of Global Finance: From Bretton Woods to the 1990s* (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1994). Helleiner's account emphasizes the administrative coherence of the dollar standard (its operational simplicity, its compatibility with existing banking networks, and the technical advantages of denominating IMF obligations in a single major currency) alongside its power-political origins. Robert Skidelsky's *John Maynard Keynes, Volume 3: Fighting for Britain, 1937–1946* (London: Macmillan, 2000) occupies the middle ground, treating both dimensions as load-bearing. The chapter has chosen the Steil framing because the documentary record on White's procedural strategy and Morgenthau's "seize the hotel" pressure is unambiguous, and because the chapter's emphasis on what the bancor's defeat *cost* the postwar international monetary system tracks the power-political reading more naturally than the institutional-design reading. Readers should know that competing scholarly accounts give the institutional-design dimension of the dollar standard more independent weight than the chapter does.

### Volcker's pre-1971 Treasury and Federal Reserve career

The chapter's reconstruction of Paul Volcker's career inside the Bretton Woods system — the 1949 Princeton senior thesis on Federal Reserve policy, the 1951 Harvard MA, the 1951–52 Rotary fellowship at the London School of Economics, the New York Fed years 1952–57, the Chase Manhattan tenure 1957–62 under David Rockefeller, the Treasury Deputy Under Secretary appointment under Robert Roosa in 1963, the London Gold Pool meetings he was inside the design of — draws on William L. Silber, *Volcker: The Triumph of Persistence* (New York: Bloomsbury Press, 2012), Chapters 1–4, and Paul A. Volcker (with Christine Harper), *Keeping At It: The Quest for Sound Money and Good Government* (New York: PublicAffairs, 2018), Chapters 1–4. Silber is the more thoroughly documented chronological reconstruction; Volcker's own memoir is the source for his interpretive framing of the period.

## Chapter 11 — The Breaking of Bretton Woods

### The Triffin dilemma and its unfolding

Robert Triffin, *Gold and the Dollar Crisis: The Future of Convertibility* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1960), is the foundational text. The chapter's tracking of U.S. gold reserves against foreign claims — the 1949–50 peak at $24.6 billion, the 1962 crossover when foreign official liabilities first exceeded reserves, the March 1968 reserve level of $10.7 billion after the London Gold Pool's $3 billion drain, the August 1971 ratio of $10 billion in reserves against $80 billion in foreign claims — follows Federal Reserve archival data and the standard treatment in Barry Eichengreen, *Globalizing Capital: A History of the International Monetary System* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2008, 2nd ed.), Chapter 4.

### De Gaulle's gold offensive and Britain's 1967 crisis

De Gaulle's February 4, 1965 press conference and the French gold-conversion campaign through 1968 are documented in Alec Cairncross and Barry Eichengreen, *Sterling in Decline: The Devaluations of 1931, 1949 and 1967* (2nd ed., Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2003). The chapter's tracking of French gold conversions accounting for $2.8 billion of the $3.3 billion total decline in U.S. gold stocks 1962–1966 is drawn from the same source. The November 18, 1967 sterling devaluation from $2.80 to $2.40, James Callaghan's resignation as Chancellor, and the IMF's £1.4 billion rescue loan with its conditional austerity terms are treated in Cairncross-Eichengreen and in Robin Stewart, *Britain's Reserve Currency Role* (London: Macmillan, 1983).

### The London Gold Pool

The 1961 founding of the London Gold Pool with eight central banks contributing 240 metric tons (initial value $270 million), the 1962–65 operating surplus, the 1965–68 cumulative deficit of $3.7 billion, France's June 1967 withdrawal, the November 1967–March 1968 collapse losing $3 billion in five months, and the March 14–17, 1968 Washington meetings that produced the two-tier gold market follow Gianni Toniolo, *Central Bank Cooperation at the Bank for International Settlements, 1930–1973* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2005), and Michael D. Bordo, "The Operation and Demise of the Bretton Woods System, 1958–1971," *NBER Working Paper No. 23189* (February 2017).

### Camp David, August 13–15, 1971

The reconstruction of the Camp David weekend draws principally on Volcker, *Keeping At It*, Chapter 5, supplemented by Silber, *Volcker: The Triumph of Persistence*, Chapters 4–5; Jeffrey E. Garten, *Three Days at Camp David: How a Secret Meeting in 1971 Transformed the Global Economy* (New York: Harper, 2021); and William Safire, *Before the Fall: An Inside View of the Pre-Watergate White House* (Garden City: Doubleday, 1975), the contemporaneous account from the speechwriter who attended. The list of attendees — Connally, Burns, Shultz, McCracken, Stein, Peterson, Safire, Volcker — and the substantive disagreement (Burns opposing closure, Connally driving the unilateral package over Volcker's preferred coordinated multilateral approach) is documented across all four sources. Volcker's earlier contingency plan for an orderly multilateral devaluation, drafted at Treasury in spring 1971, is described in *Keeping At It*, pp. 71–74, and in Silber, Chapter 4. The "If the British were going to take gold for their dollars, it was clear the game was indeed over" line appears in *Keeping At It* and is widely quoted across the secondary literature.

### The Smithsonian Agreement and the float

The December 1971 Smithsonian Agreement, its fifteen-month duration, and the March 1973 transition to floating exchange rates among major currencies are treated in Eichengreen, *Globalizing Capital*, Chapter 4, and in Robert Solomon, *The International Monetary System, 1945–1981* (New York: Harper & Row, 1982), the contemporary insider account by the Federal Reserve official who participated in the negotiations.

### Post-hoc inflation decompositions

The post-hoc empirical estimates of supply versus demand contributions to U.S. inflation cited in the chapter are drawn from:

- **Adam Hale Shapiro, "Decomposing Supply and Demand Driven Inflation,"** Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Working Paper 2022-18, October 12, 2022 — sectoral PCE decomposition with monthly supply- and demand-driven contributions.
- **Mary Amiti, Sebastian Heise, Fatih Karahan, and Ayşegül Şahin, "How Much Did Supply Constraints Boost U.S. Inflation?"** *Liberty Street Economics*, August 23, 2022 — the structural multi-sector model with the 60/40 demand/supply attribution for 2019–21.
- **Kyle Cox, Marco Del Negro, Marc Giannoni, et al., "Quantifying the Inflationary Impact of Fiscal Stimulus under Supply Constraints,"** Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report No. 1050 — the calibration attributing roughly two-thirds of total model-based inflation to aggregate demand shocks, with fiscal stimulus contributing half or more of the demand effect.
- **Rudolfs Bems, Francesca Caselli, Francesco Grigoli, and Damiano Sandri, "Demand vs. Supply Decomposition of Inflation,"** IMF Working Paper, 2023 — the 32-country extension of Shapiro's framework.
- **Ben S. Bernanke and Olivier Blanchard, "What caused the U.S. pandemic-era inflation?"** Hutchins Center / Brookings, 2023 — dynamic model placing most of the surge in price shocks while flagging the demand component.

### COVID labor-supply shock

The labor-force participation analysis follows U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data (LFPR series, February 2020 baseline at 63.4 percent through April 2020 trough at 60.1–60.2 percent and partial recovery to 62.0 percent by December 2021). The "missing workers" framing draws on Katharine G. Abraham and Lea E. Rendell, "Where Are the Missing Workers? Anticipated and Unanticipated Labor Supply Changes in the Pandemic's Aftermath," Brookings Papers on Economic Activity (working paper, 2023), which places the truly pandemic-attributable shortfall at several hundred thousand to roughly 1.5 million workers. The long-COVID labor-force estimate of 2 to 4 million working-age Americans out of work is from Katie Bach, "New data shows long Covid is keeping as many as 4 million people out of work," Brookings Institution, August 23, 2022.

### The "great inflation debate" framing

The chapter's editorial position that no single framework called the 2021–22 episode cleanly draws on Adam Tooze, "The Great Inflation Debate," *Chartbook* No. 42, October 4, 2021, and "Chartbook #152: The anti-inflation pivot of 2022," September 17, 2022. The IMF's October 2022 *World Economic Outlook* analytical chapters and the Bems et al. cross-country decomposition above support the broader judgment that the U.S. experience was one instance of a layered global episode rather than a clean test of any one theory.

## Chapter 12B — The Tests

### The 1979–82 disinflation and the South American counterfactual

The chapter's treatment of Volcker's 1979–82 disinflation, the South American hyperinflation counterfactual, and the Hagedorn family Iowa farm material draws on Volcker, *Keeping At It*, and Silber, *Volcker: The Triumph of Persistence*, with the South American comparative framework set by Ben S. Bernanke, "The Great Moderation," remarks at the Eastern Economic Association, February 20, 2004, and Bernanke's 2005 essay on Latin American very-high inflation. Harl, *The Farm Debt Crisis of the 1980s* (Ames: Iowa State University Press, 1990).

### The public-choice critique: Buchanan, Wagner, Niskanen

The chapter's treatment of the public-choice critique of discretionary fiscal policy follows James M. Buchanan and Richard E. Wagner, *Democracy in Deficit: The Political Legacy of Lord Keynes* (New York: Academic Press, 1977; Liberty Fund reprint, 2000); James M. Buchanan, "The Constitution of Economic Policy," Nobel Prize Lecture, December 8, 1986; William A. Niskanen Jr., *Bureaucracy and Representative Government* (Chicago: Aldine-Atherton, 1971); and James M. Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, *The Calculus of Consent: Logical Foundations of Constitutional Democracy* (Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 1962).

### Sargent-Wallace and the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level

The chapter's treatment of fiscal dominance follows Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace, "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic," *Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review* 5 (Fall 1981): 1–17, and John H. Cochrane, *The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2023), especially chapter 2.

### Hayek's knowledge problem

The discussion of Hayek's epistemic critique follows F.A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society," *American Economic Review* 35, no. 4 (September 1945): 519–530, and *Denationalisation of Money: An Analysis of the Theory and Practice of Concurrent Currencies* (London: Institute of Economic Affairs, 1976).

### The rules-based tradition: contemporary cases

The chapter's treatment of the rules-based answer to the public-choice critique draws on three contemporary cases. The European Union's revised fiscal framework follows the European Commission's economic-governance review (2024), with the Excessive Deficit Procedure documented in the consolidated EU treaties (Article 126 TFEU) and the medium-term net-expenditure plans set out in the 2024 reform of the Stability and Growth Pact. The United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility was established by the Budget Responsibility and National Audit Act 2011; its mandate, forecasting methodology, and assessment role are documented in the OBR's Charter for Budget Responsibility and its Economic and Fiscal Outlook publications. The Chilean structural balance rule, adopted in 2001 and revised across multiple business cycles, is documented in the Chilean Ministry of Finance's structural-balance methodology papers and in Mauricio Larraín, Christian Larraín, and Daniel Smith, "Chile's Fiscal Policy and the Cycle," and the Inter-American Development Bank's working-paper literature on Latin American fiscal rules. The Kydland–Prescott "rules vs. discretion" framework underlying the rules-based design follows Finn E. Kydland and Edward C. Prescott, "Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans," *Journal of Political Economy* 85, no. 3 (June 1977): 473–492. The eurozone case for the rules-based tradition's documented vulnerabilities follows the standard literature on the Greek, Spanish, Portuguese, and Irish crises and their handling under Maastricht-era fiscal architecture, including the post-2010 record assembled in Adam Tooze, *Crashed*, and Mark Blyth, *Austerity: The History of a Dangerous Idea* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2013).

### The stabilizer-based tradition: the modern MMT canon

The chapter's treatment of the stabilizer-based answer follows the modern Modern Monetary Theory canon: L. Randall Wray, *Modern Money Theory: A Primer on Macroeconomics for Sovereign Monetary Systems* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2nd ed., 2015), for the chartalist-tradition reconstruction; Stephanie Kelton, *The Deficit Myth: Modern Monetary Theory and the Birth of the People's Economy* (PublicAffairs, 2020), for the popular argument and the political-economy critique of the household-budget metaphor; Pavlina R. Tcherneva, *The Case for a Job Guarantee* (Polity, 2020), and the Levy Economics Institute working-paper literature on the federal job guarantee as automatic stabilizer; Warren Mosler, *Soft Currency Economics II: The Origin of Modern Monetary Theory* (US Virgin Islands: Valance Co., 2012), for the operational-monetary-economics framing; and Perry Mehrling, *The New Lombard Street: How the Fed Became the Dealer of Last Resort* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2011), for the hierarchical-money perspective that connects the chartalist tradition to the contemporary plumbing of central banking.
