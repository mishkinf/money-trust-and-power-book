# Chapter 3 Sources & Summary

## **Chapter Summary**

Early modern monarchs faced an impossible dilemma: war required credit, but credit required trust, and royal defaults had destroyed that trust. Domestic instruments like tally sticks worked within borders because tax authority backed them and because Exchequer assignments could move claims on future taxation through English law. Foreign wars required settlement assets, bills, or institutions that outsiders could enforce without relying on the king's mercy. The chapter now gives fuller treatment to the goldsmith-bankers as a private payments system born from mistrust of Crown custody, then destroyed when Charles II's Stop of the Exchequer froze the Crown debt that backed their circulating promises. The solution emerged not from economics alone but from constitutional revolution: parliamentary control of taxation after 1688 made England's debt credible for the first time. Lenders could trust institutional commitment even if they distrusted a king. The Bank of England would turn that parliamentary credibility into liquid money in 1694.
