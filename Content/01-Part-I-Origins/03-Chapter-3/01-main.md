# PART I: THE ORIGINS OF CREDIT MONEY

## Chapter 3: The Sovereign's Dilemma

The royal treasury at Westminster was nearly empty. Outside, in the muddy streets of London, soldiers waited for their pay. Some had marched from Yorkshire; others had sailed from Portsmouth. All had fought for King Edward III in his war against France, and all expected coin—silver pennies stamped with the royal seal. But the Exchequer's coffers held only tally sticks and promises. The year was 1340, and England's king faced a problem that would haunt many European monarchs for the next three centuries: how to pay for war when the realm's usable wealth existed mostly in claims on future revenue.

Edward turned to the only people with liquid capital—Italian merchant-bankers. The Bardi and Peruzzi families of Florence had grown rich financing Mediterranean trade, and they understood the new instruments of credit. They advanced Edward gold florins and silver marks, recorded the debt in their ledgers, and waited for repayment. When the king suspended payment in the mid-1340s, unable to extract enough taxes from his subjects to satisfy foreign creditors, the default became the traditional trigger in later accounts of both houses' collapse, though the decline had wider causes. For a generation afterward, English royal borrowing carried the memory of that failure.

This chapter follows that dilemma from Edward III's medieval default to Edward Backwell's goldsmith bank on Lombard Street. The distinction is simple, and it would shape monetary history for centuries: domestic credit could rest on a monarch's power to tax his own subjects, but international credit required assets or institutions that strangers would trust. A king could make promises circulate inside his realm. Across borders, his word became just another risk.

---

### The paradox of sovereignty

By the fourteenth century, European monarchs were trying to finance territories, wars, and administrative ambitions that medieval fiscal forms could not comfortably support. Yet they were poorer in disposable wealth than a Mesopotamian temple or a Chinese provincial governor. The reason was structural: medieval kingship rested on feudal obligation—duties owed by vassals to lords in exchange for land—not bureaucratic extraction. A king could summon his vassals to war for forty days a year; he could collect customary dues—traditional fees and payments required by law or custom—on trade and justice; he could claim rents from royal lands. But he lacked fiscal capacity—the government's ability to collect taxes reliably and in sufficient quantity. He could not simply decree a tax and expect silver to flow into his treasury.

War changed everything. The Hundred Years' War, the Italian Wars, the Wars of Religion—each conflict demanded resources on a scale that feudal dues could never provide. Cannons cost more than castles. Professional soldiers expected regular wages. Naval expeditions required not just ships but provisions, gunpowder, and foreign supplies. A single campaign could consume a decade's ordinary revenue.

To finance these wars, kings needed to borrow. But what they borrowed, and from whom, determined whether they could ever repay.

---

### Domestic credit: the limits of tally sticks

Within England, the crown had long relied on a simple instrument: the tally stick. When a taxpayer delivered grain or coin to the Exchequer—the royal treasury and accounting office that managed all government revenues and expenditures—a clerk notched a hazel rod to record the amount, split it lengthwise, and gave half to the payer. The stick itself became a receipt—proof of payment and a claim on future tax obligations. Because the crown accepted tallies in lieu of coin, they circulated as a kind of money. A merchant who held a tally for one hundred shillings could use it to pay his own taxes or sell it to someone who owed the king.

This system worked brilliantly within the realm. Tallies were backed by the king's power to tax, and anyone who lived under English law might need to satisfy the Exchequer. A tally was as good as silver to the right holder because it canceled a debt the state could enforce. The crown could, in effect, spend against future taxes - issuing tallies to pay soldiers or suppliers, confident that those instruments would return through the tax system.

The physical simplicity disguised an advanced fiscal machine. The Exchequer did not merely record money already collected; by the fourteenth century it routinely assigned future revenue before the sheriff or tax farmer had brought it in. A contractor who supplied cloth to the army might receive a tally drawn on a named stream of future customs or excise. He could wait, sell the tally at a discount, pledge it as collateral, or pass it to someone with taxes to settle. The Crown had spent before collecting, and the tax system gave the wooden claim its domestic value.

That made tallies more like public credit than primitive barter. Their value came from a chain of enforceable relationships: the Crown's claim on the taxpayer, the Exchequer's record of the assignment, and the holder's ability to use the instrument inside English law. The stick itself was not magic. Split wood did not circulate because people mistook hazel for silver. It circulated because it was a legal technology for moving claims on future taxation from one balance sheet to another.

But tallies had a fatal limitation: they could not cross borders. A Flemish merchant selling wool to England might accept payment in tallies if he planned to buy English goods or pay English agents. But an Italian banker financing a war in France, or a German arms dealer supplying cannons, had no use for a stick of wood redeemable only in English taxes. They demanded coin—gold or silver that held value anywhere.

This distinction between domestic credit (borrowing from subjects in instruments the state can accept back) and foreign credit (borrowing from outsiders in assets or currencies the state must acquire) defined the fiscal crisis of early modern monarchy. At home, a king could create credit by issuing promises backed by his authority to tax. Abroad, he was just another debtor, and his word alone meant much less.

---

### What kings borrowed and why

When Edward III borrowed from the Bardi, he borrowed gold florins - Florentine coins that circulated across Europe as a trusted standard. When he suspended payment in the mid-1340s, the shock damaged the Florentine houses and became the most visible symbol of royal credit failure. When Henry VIII financed his wars against France in the 1540s, he borrowed from Antwerp bankers through gold ducats and bills of exchange - merchant credit instruments allowing payment in distant cities. When Philip II of Spain funded his armies in the Netherlands, he pledged future revenues from American silver mines: metal that had not yet been dug from the ground.

What were these kings buying? Not abstractions, but tangible goods and services that could only be acquired abroad:

- Mercenaries: Swiss pikemen, German landsknechts—professional infantry soldiers—and Italian condottieri—mercenary commanders and their troops—fought for cash, not feudal loyalty. They demanded payment in coin they could spend anywhere.
- Warships and naval stores: Timber from the Baltic, tar from Scandinavia, sailcloth from the Low Countries—all had to be purchased in international markets.
- Gunpowder and weapons: Sulfur from Sicily, saltpeter from India, bronze for cannons from German foundries.
- Provisions for armies: When an English army campaigned in France, it could not live off tallies. It needed bread, beer, and beef purchased locally—in French coin.

Every foreign purchase required exportable value. A king could not pay a Venetian shipbuilder with a promise redeemable only in London. He needed metal, goods, or credit instruments drawn on houses foreign merchants already trusted.

This is why monarchs obsessed over their realms' supply of precious metal. Not because gold and silver magically solved the problem of value, but because they reduced the problem of trust. A gold coin minted in Florence could pay a soldier in Bruges, a merchant in Lisbon, or a banker in Augsburg. It was collateral that traveled more easily than law.

The same logic shaped the great continental banking houses. The Fuggers did not lend to Habsburg princes because royal promises were sacred; they lent against mining rights, silver flows, tax farms, and political access. Genoese bankers financing Spain's armies built syndicates around the *asiento* system, converting royal promises into claims on future American silver and other assigned revenues. Bills of exchange could settle most trade without chests of coin, but only because the bills rested on merchant networks with reputation, courts, and reciprocal enforcement. When those networks doubted repayment, metal and collateral reappeared as the settlement asset of last resort.

Information traveled almost as fast as money. Printed gazettes, scribal newsletters, merchant letters, and banking correspondents carried news of defaults across Europe. A sovereign who stopped payment in Madrid, Paris, or London did not merely inconvenience one lender. He altered the terms on which every future lender priced his word. The international creditor's question was therefore brutally practical: if the king changes his mind, what can I seize, sell, ship, or enforce without depending on that same king's mercy?

Today's equivalent is the hard-currency reserve. Countries that borrow in dollars they cannot create face a modern version of the old royal problem: domestic authority does not automatically become international liquidity.

---

### The alchemy of coinage

If foreign creditors demanded metal, kings needed a way to concentrate it in their hands. The solution was coinage mandates—laws requiring that taxes be paid in the king's own coin. By monopolizing minting and requiring its use for crown obligations, monarchs created demand for their currency (a principle we'll examine fully in Chapter 5).

The logic was elegant: the state spent first, issuing coins to pay soldiers and suppliers. Those coins entered circulation, changing hands in markets and taverns. Eventually, they returned to the crown through taxes. The cycle created a perpetual demand for the king's money, because everyone who owed taxes needed to acquire it. As long as the king could enforce tax collection, his coins held value—not because of the metal content alone, but because of the obligation they satisfied.

This was the same principle that had made Mesopotamian grain credits and Chinese jiaozi notes valuable: the issuing authority's power to demand them back. Coinage added metal, portability, and recognizability; tax acceptance added a guaranteed domestic use. The coin's value came from both its substance and the obligations it discharged.

Yet this domestic alchemy had no power abroad. A French merchant did not owe English taxes, so he had no reason to accept English coin at face value. He would take it only if the metal content justified the exchange rate—and kings, desperate for revenue, routinely debased their coinage, reducing the silver or gold and pocketing the difference. By the sixteenth century, Europe's monetary system was a chaos of clipped (edges cut off for metal), sweated (shaken to remove metal dust), and counterfeited (fake) coins, each trading at a discount to its nominal value.

---

### The crisis of trust

The real problem was not metal but credibility. When a king borrowed, he promised to repay from future tax revenues. But could he? Medieval and early modern monarchs had weak bureaucracies for tax collection, poor knowledge of their subjects' wealth, and no reliable way to predict next year's harvest or trade flows. They borrowed optimistically and defaulted routinely.

The English crown's record was particularly dismal. Edward III's default in 1345 damaged the Italian houses that had financed him. Elizabeth I borrowed cautiously, but her successors - James I and Charles I - treated credit as an entitlement. Charles resorted to forced loans, demanded from merchants under threat of imprisonment, and in 1640 interfered with bullion deposited at the Tower Mint. When Parliament resisted his demands for new taxes, he dissolved it and ruled alone.

The Tower Mint episode mattered because it taught London's merchants where not to store their money. Gold and silver had been lodged there for safety and recoining. Charles I, desperate for funds, stopped the flow of metal out of the Mint and treated private bullion as an emergency fiscal resource. He later released much of it, but the political lesson had already been learned. A vault controlled by the Crown was not a neutral safe. If the king could not tax openly, he might borrow forcibly, seize temporarily, or redefine custody as credit. The goldsmiths' future banking business began partly as an act of avoidance: private vaults became more attractive because royal vaults had proved politically exposed.

The English Civil War (1642-1651) was many things - a religious conflict, a constitutional struggle, a clash of classes - but it was also a fiscal reckoning. Charles I's inability to finance his government without parliamentary consent revealed the bankruptcy of absolutism. His execution in 1649 symbolized not just the death of a king but the death of an idea: that personal sovereignty could sustain public credit.

### The Sign of the Unicorn

Lombard Street ran two hundred and sixty meters from Bank junction east to Gracechurch Street through London's financial heart. Its eastern half retained the narrow medieval line; its western half, near St Mary Woolnoth church, had widened into a row of, as John Strype described it fifty years later, "good and lofty buildings… generally inhabited by goldsmiths, bankers, merchants, and other eminent tradesmen." The signs hanging over their doors — the Grasshopper at Thomas Gresham's, the Vine at the Vyners', the Unicorn at Edward Backwell's — were landmarks in an era of limited literacy. By the 1660s, the people who passed beneath them were doing something for which there was no English public institution at all: they were banking.

Inside Backwell's shop, the ground floor opened to the street through arched windows whose horizontal shutters lowered at night for security. The lower shutter folded down to form a counter; customers stood outside while the shopkeeper served from within, the goods and ledgers behind him. On June 17, 1662, Samuel Pepys delivered the last chest of "crusados" — Portuguese gold coins — for his patron, the Earl of Sandwich, to be weighed at the shop. He found Backwell's wife in the shop preparing to take a coach; a Portuguese merchant called Don Duarte de Silva was offering her a gilded glass of perfumed comfits, and he poured some into Pepys's hand as well. Pepys, who would appear in Backwell's ledger nearly sixty times, watched three thousand crusados weighed out to roughly five hundred and thirty pounds. The shop was a counting house, a vault, and a parlor at once.

What Backwell and his neighbors had built in the absence of any English public bank was, in form, astonishingly modern. A merchant who deposited gold or silver received a written receipt - payable to bearer, on demand - that could be passed from hand to hand as money. The receipts circulated; the metal sat in the vault; and Backwell, observing that depositors rarely all asked for their gold at once, kept perhaps a tenth in reserve and lent the rest. He paid six per cent on deposits; he earned eight, ten, sometimes more on Crown debt purchased at a discount. Nineteen other goldsmith-bankers maintained clearing accounts with him to settle each other's notes at par - a private network that performed, in miniature, some of the work a formal banking system would later do. By 1671, his ledger ran to six hundred and sixty-six folios, recording roughly four hundred customers: Charles II's accounts in his own name; Prince Rupert; the Prince of Orange; the Countess of Castlemaine, the King's mistress; the Earl of Sandwich; Pepys; the East India Company; and hundreds of merchants and what one historian has called "relatively humble doctors, lawyers, and military officers." The Little London Directory of 1677 listed about eighteen hundred merchants in the City; a significant fraction held accounts at the Unicorn.

There were three banking inventions here, each hiding in plain sight. First, the receipt payable to bearer could circulate beyond the original depositor; it was a promise that had become transferable money. Second, the drawn note - a written instruction to pay a third party - let depositors make payments without moving coin at all. Third, the interbank clearing account allowed one goldsmith's promises to settle against another's. London had not yet created a central bank, but it had already created the habits of deposit banking: ledger transfer, reserve management, note circulation, and par clearing among trusted houses.

None of this was designed by statute. Goldsmith banking grew in a legal gray area, tolerated because it was useful and trusted because the leading houses had names, vaults, ledgers, and relationships. That made the system flexible, entrepreneurial, and fragile. Its liabilities were payable on demand; its assets were increasingly long-dated claims on the Crown. A maturity mismatch had become a political mismatch. The goldsmiths promised instant redemption to depositors while lending to a sovereign who could delay repayment by proclamation.

Backwell's prosperity was visible. In April 1669 he showed Pepys "the model of his houses that he is going to build in Cornhill and Lumbard Street; but he hath purchased so much there, that it looks like a little town, and must have cost him a great deal of money." By December 1670 he had taken a fifty-one-year lease on six and a half thousand square feet of London ground and enlarged his own house from thirteen hearths to twenty-one. He had survived the Great Fire of 1666 — at four o'clock in the afternoon of Monday September 3, the fire had reached "the wealthiest part of the City, the area around Lombard Street and Gracechurch Street," and Pepys had recorded the houses tumbling — but Backwell had got his papers and plate out, and within a few years he was building.

What kept the system aloft was Backwell's principal debtor, who was also his sovereign. By 1670, by the surviving estimates, he had advanced something on the order of £365,000 to Charles II's Treasury, secured against assignments on future tax revenue. The Crown was at once his largest customer, his largest counterparty, and his largest concentration of risk. He understood the position. So, periodically, did Pepys. In November 1667, in the aftermath of the disastrous Dutch raid on the Medway, Pepys recorded Backwell saying, "with some vain glory," that the Sheerness business had nearly undone him. The previous July, Pepys had written down a different remark in Backwell's voice — a sentence whose full weight only the next decade would reveal: "he says he has a good master, the King, who will not suffer him to be undone, as otherwise he must have been, and I believe him."

Backwell's confidence was not foolish. He had been managing the Crown's finances on this scale since the Restoration, and the King had, on his record, paid. The architecture of his business was perhaps the most sophisticated private credit system in seventeenth-century Europe. What he could not see, and what no goldsmith could insure against, was the structural fact this chapter has already named: in the absence of an institution that constrained the sovereign, the sovereign could simply choose not to pay. The goldsmith system rested entirely on a foundation it did not control.

---

On January 2, 1672, Charles II chose the one option the goldsmiths could not survive. Preparing for war with the Dutch and short of ready cash, he declared the Stop of the Exchequer, suspending payments on Exchequer orders for one year. The total later calculated as suspended was £1,328,526 - approximately 2.2-3.3 percent of English GDP and more than the crown's entire annual peacetime revenue. The proclamation promised interest. What it withheld was the thing a banker needs in a panic: cash now.

The reaction was immediate. On January 5, Treasury officials summoned the Duke of York, Prince Rupert, Robert Vyner, Alderman Backwell, and other bankers to emergency talks. On January 6, Richard Langhorne wrote that "the trade of bankers is totally destroyed by this accident." On January 12, Backwell and others asked for quarterly interest and protection from arrests by their own creditors. A week later, the Crown moved to strip Backwell of his role receiving the Queen's Portuguese portion. The king had not merely stopped paying. He had turned his banker into a shock absorber for the state's own crisis.

The Stop was announced as a temporary measure, but temporary was exactly what a banker in a run could not rely on. A merchant holding Backwell's note did not need to solve the kingdom's fiscal problem. He needed to meet payroll, pay suppliers, settle a bill, or reassure his own creditors. Once the Crown froze Backwell's assets, every private promise Backwell had issued became suspect. The public default propagated through private balance sheets because the goldsmiths had transformed royal debt into circulating money.

That is the crucial monetary point. If Backwell had merely been a wealthy lender, the Stop would have been an elite creditor dispute. Because Backwell's receipts and drawn notes circulated through London commerce, the Stop attacked the payments system. The King's delayed payment became a merchant's failed withdrawal, then a supplier's missing settlement, then a creditor's demand for cash. A sovereign accounting decision had become a private liquidity crisis.

The chain snapped. Backwell, eventually recorded as owed £295,994 by the king, suddenly could not collect; his depositors still held notes promising gold on demand. He could not pay them. He struggled on for a decade, was declared bankrupt in 1682, and died in the Netherlands the following year. Sir Robert Vyner, owed more than £400,000, was reduced to petitioning Parliament for relief while serving as Lord Mayor of London. Five major goldsmith houses failed; nine more were severely undermined; over ten thousand wealthy families were said to have suffered losses.

The archive preserves the great names better than the smaller losses. We can follow Pepys into Backwell's shop; we can trace the King's account and the Duke of York's summons; we can name Vyner and Backwell because Parliament and the courts had to reckon with them. The ordinary depositor appears mostly as an aggregate: families embarrassed, houses lost, capital locked away, creditors pressing claims against people whose own money had disappeared into a frozen Exchequer order. That absence matters. The Stop was most legible to posterity where elite paper survived, but its economic force ran through less visible balance sheets.

For a London merchant, the disaster did not require bankruptcy on the first day. It could begin as illiquidity: a receipt that could not be cashed, a supplier who demanded coin, a bill coming due, a creditor who no longer trusted goldsmith paper. What had been treated as money yesterday became a lawsuit tomorrow. The transformation was psychological as well as legal. Once people learned that the Crown's promise to Backwell could be interrupted, they had reason to doubt Backwell's promise to them.

The legal battles dragged for nearly three decades. The Court of Exchequer ruled for the bankers in 1692 and was reversed on technical grounds in 1696. The House of Lords found for them in 1700, but Parliament's settlement the following year limited interest to 3 percent from December 1705 only - no compensation for the missing twenty-four years. By the time the dust settled, the effective return on the goldsmiths' loan to the king worked out to roughly 0.01 percent annually. The pattern was clear: sovereign default on elite creditors produced decades of litigation and partial compensation. Ordinary depositors had no direct claim on the Crown. And the City of London learned a lesson it would not forget: the king's word, by itself, was not a foundation strong enough to build a banking system on.

### The circular trap

By the 1680s, England's monarchy faced a paradox that defined the age: war required credit, but credit required trust, and trust required institutions the Crown did not control. A king could raise an army, but he could not raise the money to pay it cheaply. He could declare war, but he could not compel lenders to finance it on his terms. And he could not borrow abroad without offering collateral - metal, goods, or credible promises - that his domestic authority could not guarantee.

France faced the same problem. Louis XIV waged nearly continuous war from 1667 to 1714, draining the kingdom's wealth and driving it into debt. French kings borrowed from tax farmers—private financiers who paid the crown upfront for the right to collect taxes—but the system was corrupt and inefficient. Lenders demanded exorbitant interest because they knew the king might default. And when he did, as in the repudiation of debts in 1648 and 1661, it only made future borrowing more expensive.

Spain's monarchy, despite the wealth of its American empire, declared bankruptcy in 1557, 1560, 1575, 1596, 1607, 1627, and 1647. Each default destroyed the fortunes of Genoese and German bankers who had financed Habsburg wars. Yet each time, the cycle repeated: war, borrowing, default, and a new generation of creditors willing to gamble on royal promises.

The pattern was not identical everywhere, but the pressure was common. Monarchs could not wage modern war without credit. They could not sustain credit without making repayment more credible than royal convenience. The solution, when it came in England, would not be a cleverer private bank. It would be a political settlement that made the state's promises harder for the monarch to break.

### The border problem

The distinction between domestic and foreign borrowing was not merely technical. It reflected what creditors could enforce. English tallies worked inside England because they connected their holder to English tax machinery. A domestic merchant could present them to Exchequer officials, verify their place in the revenue system, and use them against obligations owed to the Crown.

A Dutch investor or Hamburg merchant faced a different world. He could not easily collect from English tax receivers without agents. He could not use an English tally to settle a debt in Amsterdam. He could rely on bills of exchange when the underlying banking network was trusted, and most cross-border trade did settle through bills rather than through chests of metal. But when trust thinned or balances had to be closed, gold and silver still mattered because they traveled without requiring access to the debtor's courts, tax offices, or political bargains.

Foreign creditors' preference for metal was therefore practical rather than superstitious. The Genoese bankers who financed Spanish wars through the *asiento* system structured contracts around American silver because silver gave them a claim outside the king's promises. Later Dutch investors would hold English debt without gold collateral when parliamentary guarantees made repayment credible. The lesson was not that gold created trust by itself. It was that, in the absence of institutions strong enough to cross borders, metal was the least political settlement asset available.

---

### The invention of the fiscal state

What Europe needed was a way to make royal debt credible without relying on royal virtue. The answer emerged not from economic theory but from constitutional struggle: parliamentary control of taxation. If a representative body, rather than the king alone, authorized taxes and guaranteed their use for debt repayment, then creditors could trust the nation even if they distrusted the monarch.

The Dutch Republic pioneered this model in the late sixteenth century. After rebelling against Spanish rule, the United Provinces created a federal system where provincial assemblies controlled taxation and borrowing. Because creditors knew that taxes were legally committed to debt service, they lent to the Dutch at interest rates far below those charged to absolute monarchs. The Dutch could borrow more, at lower cost, than any kingdom in Europe—and they used that advantage to build a commercial empire.

Foreign investors understood the difference. The question was not whether the Dutch or English were morally superior to French or Spanish kings; it was whether repayment depended on one ruler's convenience or on institutions with longer time horizons. Representative bodies, political parties, funded taxes, and tradable debt all changed the creditor's calculation. A Parliament could be corrupt, factional, and self-interested, but it was harder for it to vanish into one man's appetite. Its members included many of the people who lent to the state, traded with it, or depended on London's credit. That alignment created a coalition for repayment.

England would follow the same path, but only after a revolution. In 1688, when Parliament invited William of Orange to take the throne, it imposed a condition: the king would govern with Parliament, not above it. The Bill of Rights (1689) made explicit what the Civil War had implied—the crown could not raise taxes or maintain an army without parliamentary consent. That constitutional settlement transformed England's credit overnight.

William's invasion also revealed how quickly credible institutions could mobilize money. Dutch financiers raised millions of guilders for the expedition because William's political project looked familiar: a Protestant coalition, parliamentary taxation, and war finance disciplined by representative institutions. After 1688, English debt was no longer simply the king's debt. It became national debt - authorized by Parliament, serviced by named taxes, and protected by a political class whose own fortunes were tied to the credit of the state.

For the first time, lenders could trust that their loans were backed not by a king's mood but by a nation's legal machinery. Parliament could commit future tax revenues to debt repayment, and no monarch could unilaterally repudiate that commitment. This birthed the fiscal-military state - a government capable of mobilizing resources on a scale that would have astonished Edward III or Charles II.

But the machinery of that state - the institution that would turn parliamentary promises into liquid credit - had yet to be invented. That innovation would come in 1694, when a group of London merchants proposed a radical solution: a bank that would lend to the government by creating money. The Bank of England would complete the revolution of 1688, transforming not just England's finances but the nature of money itself.

The solution to the sovereign's dilemma - how to create credible credit both domestically and internationally - would require institutional innovation on an unprecedented scale. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 made England's debt more credible by transferring fiscal power to Parliament. The Bank of England's founding in 1694 completed the transformation by creating a mechanism to turn that credibility into immediately spendable credit.

The asymmetry that trapped Edward III did not vanish with medieval monarchy. A government that owes in money it can issue faces one problem; a government that owes in money it must acquire faces another. Greece would rediscover that distinction inside the eurozone in 2010, when debts denominated in a shared currency behaved less like domestic promises and more like obligations to outside creditors. Currency sovereignty did not guarantee wisdom or prosperity. But losing it changed the state's room to maneuver.

Backwell's ruined ledgers show why the next step had to be institutional. The goldsmith-bankers had created credit through fractional-reserve lending, issuing receipts that circulated as currency. For three decades the system worked - until 1672 revealed its flaw. Private money could expand trust, but it could not discipline the sovereign on whom its largest assets depended. What England needed was not simply more inventive bankers. It needed a public credit machine, backed by parliamentary taxation, that could make state promises more reliable than the personal word of a king and liquid enough to circulate as money.
