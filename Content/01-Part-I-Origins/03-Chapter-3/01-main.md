# PART I: THE ORIGINS OF CREDIT MONEY

## Chapter 3: The Sovereign's Dilemma

The royal treasury at Westminster was nearly empty. Outside, in the muddy streets of London, soldiers waited for their pay. Some had marched from Yorkshire; others had sailed from Portsmouth. All had fought for King Edward III in his war against France, and all expected coin—silver pennies stamped with the royal seal. But the Exchequer's coffers held only tally sticks and promises. The year was 1340, and England's king faced a problem that would haunt every European monarch for the next three centuries: how to pay for war when the realm's wealth existed mostly on paper.

Edward turned to the only people with liquid capital—Italian merchant-bankers. The Bardi and Peruzzi families of Florence had grown rich financing Mediterranean trade, and they understood the new instruments of credit. They advanced Edward gold florins and silver marks, recorded the debt in their ledgers, and waited for repayment. But when the king defaulted in 1345, unable to extract enough taxes from his subjects to satisfy foreign creditors, both houses collapsed. The shock rippled across Europe's financial networks. For a generation afterward, no banker would lend to an English king without collateral in hand.

This chapter reveals a fundamental distinction that would shape monetary history for centuries: the difference between domestic credit, backed by a monarch's power to tax his subjects, and international credit, which required assets foreigners would accept. A king could create money within his realm by issuing promises redeemable in taxes. But beyond his borders, those promises meant nothing. Foreign wars required foreign credit, and foreign credit required gold, silver, or goods that could cross boundaries. The fiscal crisis of early modern monarchy was born from this asymmetry.

---

### The paradox of sovereignty

By the fourteenth century, European monarchs ruled territories larger and more populous than any ancient empire. Yet they were poorer in disposable wealth than a Mesopotamian temple or a Chinese provincial governor. The reason was structural: medieval kingship rested on feudal obligation—duties owed by vassals to lords in exchange for land—not bureaucratic extraction. A king could summon his vassals to war for forty days a year; he could collect customary dues—traditional fees and payments required by law or custom—on trade and justice; he could claim rents from royal lands. But he lacked fiscal capacity—the government's ability to collect taxes reliably and in sufficient quantity. He could not simply decree a tax and expect silver to flow into his treasury.

War changed everything. The Hundred Years' War, the Italian Wars, the Wars of Religion—each conflict demanded resources on a scale that feudal dues could never provide. Cannons cost more than castles. Professional soldiers expected regular wages. Naval expeditions required not just ships but provisions, gunpowder, and foreign supplies. A single campaign could consume a decade's ordinary revenue.

To finance these wars, kings needed to borrow. But what they borrowed, and from whom, determined whether they could ever repay.

---

### Domestic credit: the limits of tally sticks

Within England, the crown had long relied on a simple instrument: the tally stick. When a taxpayer delivered grain or coin to the Exchequer—the royal treasury and accounting office that managed all government revenues and expenditures—a clerk notched a hazel rod to record the amount, split it lengthwise, and gave half to the payer. The stick itself became a receipt—proof of payment and a claim on future tax obligations. Because the crown accepted tallies in lieu of coin, they circulated as a kind of money. A merchant who held a tally for one hundred shillings could use it to pay his own taxes or sell it to someone who owed the king.

This system worked brilliantly within the realm. Tallies were backed by the king's power to tax, and anyone who lived under English law eventually needed to satisfy the Exchequer. A tally was as good as silver because it canceled a debt that every subject would someday owe. The crown could, in effect, spend before it taxed—issuing tallies to pay soldiers or suppliers, confident that those instruments would return through the tax system.

But tallies had a fatal limitation: they could not cross borders. A Flemish merchant selling wool to England might accept payment in tallies if he planned to buy English goods or pay English agents. But an Italian banker financing a war in France, or a German arms dealer supplying cannons, had no use for a stick of wood redeemable only in English taxes. They demanded coin—gold or silver that held value anywhere.

This distinction between domestic credit (borrowing from your own citizens in currency you control, backed by your power to tax them) and foreign credit (borrowing from foreigners in currencies or assets you must acquire from others) defined the fiscal crisis of early modern monarchy. At home, a king could create credit by fiat, issuing promises backed by his authority to tax. Abroad, he was just another debtor, and his word alone meant nothing.

---

### What kings borrowed and why

When Edward III borrowed from the Bardi, he borrowed gold florins—Florentine gold coins that circulated across Europe as a trusted standard. When he defaulted in 1345, unable to extract enough taxes to repay foreign creditors, both banking houses collapsed—a sovereign default where a government refuses or is unable to pay its debts. When Henry VIII financed his wars against France in the 1540s, he borrowed from Antwerp bankers who advanced him silver ducats—Venetian gold coins used in international trade—and bills of exchange—merchant credit instruments allowing payment in distant cities—payable in multiple currencies. When Philip II of Spain funded his armies in the Netherlands, he pledged future revenues from American silver mines — metal that had not yet been dug from the ground.

What were these kings buying? Not abstractions, but tangible goods and services that could only be acquired abroad:

- Mercenaries: Swiss pikemen, German landsknechts—professional infantry soldiers—and Italian condottieri—mercenary commanders and their troops—fought for cash, not feudal loyalty. They demanded payment in coin they could spend anywhere.
- Warships and naval stores: Timber from the Baltic, tar from Scandinavia, sailcloth from the Low Countries—all had to be purchased in international markets.
- Gunpowder and weapons: Sulfur from Sicily, saltpeter from India, bronze for cannons from German foundries.
- Provisions for armies: When an English army campaigned in France, it could not live off tallies. It needed bread, beer, and beef purchased locally—in French coin.

Every foreign purchase required exportable value. A king could not pay a Venetian shipbuilder with a promise redeemable in London. He needed either metal (gold or silver coin), goods (wool, grain, or other exports), or credit instruments that foreign merchants would accept (bills of exchange drawn on trusted banking houses).

This is why monarchs obsessed over their realms' supply of precious metal. Not because gold and silver were intrinsically valuable, but because they were the universal language of international debt. A gold coin minted in Florence could pay a soldier in Bruges, a merchant in Lisbon, or a banker in Augsburg. It was neutral collateral—a store of value that transcended political boundaries.

Today's equivalent is dollar reserves. Emerging market countries hoard dollars not because they love American currency but because international debts require payment in currencies creditors accept. Turkey's 2018 crisis, Argentina's repeated defaults—all stem from borrowing in dollars they cannot create, exactly as medieval kings borrowed gold they could not mint.

---

### The alchemy of coinage

If foreign creditors demanded metal, kings needed a way to concentrate it in their hands. The solution was coinage mandates—laws requiring that taxes be paid in the king's own coin. By monopolizing minting and requiring its use for crown obligations, monarchs created demand for their currency (a principle we'll examine fully in Chapter 5).

The logic was elegant: the state spent first, issuing coins to pay soldiers and suppliers. Those coins entered circulation, changing hands in markets and taverns. Eventually, they returned to the crown through taxes. The cycle created a perpetual demand for the king's money, because everyone who owed taxes needed to acquire it. As long as the king could enforce tax collection, his coins held value—not because of the metal content alone, but because of the obligation they satisfied.

This was the same principle that had made Mesopotamian grain credits and Chinese jiaozi notes valuable: the issuing authority's power to demand them back. A coin was a portable tally stick, a tax credit stamped in metal. Its value derived not from the gold or silver itself but from the legal requirement to use it in dealings with the state.

Yet this domestic alchemy had no power abroad. A French merchant did not owe English taxes, so he had no reason to accept English coin at face value. He would take it only if the metal content justified the exchange rate—and kings, desperate for revenue, routinely debased their coinage, reducing the silver or gold and pocketing the difference. By the sixteenth century, Europe's monetary system was a chaos of clipped (edges cut off for metal), sweated (shaken to remove metal dust), and counterfeited (fake) coins, each trading at a discount to its nominal value.

---

### The crisis of trust

The real problem was not metal but credibility. When a king borrowed, he promised to repay from future tax revenues. But could he? Medieval and early modern monarchs had no reliable bureaucracy for tax collection, no accurate census of their subjects' wealth, and no way to predict next year's harvest or trade flows. They borrowed optimistically and defaulted routinely.

The English crown's record was particularly dismal. Edward III's default in 1345 destroyed the Bardi and Peruzzi. Henry VIII seized monastic lands to pay his debts, destabilizing the entire economy. Elizabeth I borrowed cautiously, but her successors—James I and Charles I—treated credit as an entitlement. Charles resorted to forced loans—loans the king demanded from merchants under threat of imprisonment—and arbitrary seizures of gold deposited at the Tower of London. When Parliament resisted his demands for new taxes, he dissolved it and ruled alone.

The English Civil War (1642–1651) was many things—a religious conflict, a constitutional struggle, a clash of classes—but it was also a fiscal reckoning. Charles I's inability to finance his government without parliamentary consent revealed the bankruptcy of absolutism. His execution in 1649 symbolized not just the death of a king but the death of an idea: that personal sovereignty could sustain public credit.

Modern bond markets enforce the same discipline. When investors doubt a government's ability to collect taxes—whether Charles I's England or contemporary Argentina—they demand higher interest rates or refuse to lend entirely. Fiscal credibility requires institutional capacity, not just sovereign authority.

### The Sign of the Unicorn

Lombard Street ran two hundred and sixty meters from Bank junction east to Gracechurch Street through London's financial heart. Its eastern half retained the narrow medieval line; its western half, near St Mary Woolnoth church, had widened into a row of, as John Strype described it fifty years later, "good and lofty buildings… generally inhabited by goldsmiths, bankers, merchants, and other eminent tradesmen." The signs hanging over their doors — the Grasshopper at Thomas Gresham's, the Vine at the Vyners', the Unicorn at Edward Backwell's — were landmarks in an era of limited literacy. By the 1660s, the people who passed beneath them were doing something for which there was no English public institution at all: they were banking.

Inside Backwell's shop, the ground floor opened to the street through arched windows whose horizontal shutters lowered at night for security. The lower shutter folded down to form a counter; customers stood outside while the shopkeeper served from within, the goods and ledgers behind him. On June 17, 1662, Samuel Pepys delivered the last chest of "crusados" — Portuguese gold coins — for his patron, the Earl of Sandwich, to be weighed at the shop. He found Backwell's wife in the shop preparing to take a coach; a Portuguese merchant called Don Duarte de Silva was offering her a gilded glass of perfumed comfits, and he poured some into Pepys's hand as well. Pepys, who would appear in Backwell's ledger nearly sixty times, watched three thousand crusados weighed out to roughly five hundred and thirty pounds. The shop was a counting house, a vault, and a parlor at once.

What Backwell and his neighbors had built in the absence of any English public bank was, in form, astonishingly modern. A merchant who deposited gold or silver received a written receipt — payable to bearer, on demand — that could be passed from hand to hand as money. The receipts circulated; the metal sat in the vault; and Backwell, observing that depositors rarely all asked for their gold at once, kept perhaps a tenth in reserve and lent the rest. He paid six per cent on deposits; he earned eight, ten, sometimes more on Crown debt purchased at a discount. Nineteen other goldsmith-bankers maintained clearing accounts with him to settle each other's notes at par — a private network that, on a good day, looked like the modern interbank system five centuries early. By 1671, his ledger ran to six hundred and sixty-six folios, recording roughly four hundred customers: Charles II's accounts in his own name; Prince Rupert; the Prince of Orange; the Countess of Castlemaine, the King's mistress; the Earl of Sandwich; Pepys; the East India Company; and hundreds of merchants and what one historian has called "relatively humble doctors, lawyers, and military officers." The Little London Directory of 1677 listed about eighteen hundred merchants in the City; a significant fraction held accounts at the Unicorn.

Backwell's prosperity was visible. In April 1669 he showed Pepys "the model of his houses that he is going to build in Cornhill and Lumbard Street; but he hath purchased so much there, that it looks like a little town, and must have cost him a great deal of money." By December 1670 he had taken a fifty-one-year lease on six and a half thousand square feet of London ground and enlarged his own house from thirteen hearths to twenty-one. He had survived the Great Fire of 1666 — at four o'clock in the afternoon of Monday September 3, the fire had reached "the wealthiest part of the City, the area around Lombard Street and Gracechurch Street," and Pepys had recorded the houses tumbling — but Backwell had got his papers and plate out, and within a few years he was building.

What kept the system aloft was Backwell's principal debtor, who was also his sovereign. By 1670, by the surviving estimates, he had advanced something on the order of £365,000 to Charles II's Treasury, secured against assignments on future tax revenue. The Crown was at once his largest customer, his largest counterparty, and his largest concentration of risk. He understood the position. So, periodically, did Pepys. In November 1667, in the aftermath of the disastrous Dutch raid on the Medway, Pepys recorded Backwell saying, "with some vain glory," that the Sheerness business had nearly undone him. The previous July, Pepys had written down a different remark in Backwell's voice — a sentence whose full weight only the next decade would reveal: "he says he has a good master, the King, who will not suffer him to be undone, as otherwise he must have been, and I believe him."

Backwell's confidence was not foolish. He had been managing the Crown's finances on this scale since the Restoration, and the King had, on his record, paid. The architecture of his business was perhaps the most sophisticated private credit system in seventeenth-century Europe. What he could not see, and what no goldsmith could insure against, was the structural fact this chapter has already named: in the absence of an institution that constrained the sovereign, the sovereign could simply choose not to pay. The goldsmith system rested entirely on a foundation it did not control.

---

In 1672, Charles II faced a liquidity crisis — a shortage of readily available cash. When the Exchequer could not meet its obligations, he declared the **Stop of the Exchequer**, suspending all debt payments for one year. The total suspended: £1,328,526 — approximately 2.2–3.3 percent of English GDP and more than the crown's entire annual peacetime revenue. The goldsmiths were ruined. Some never recovered; others passed losses to their depositors. The City of London's financial community resolved that the crown would never again borrow on its word alone.

The chain snapped. Backwell, owed £295,994 by the king, suddenly could not collect; his depositors, owed roughly the same, still held notes promising gold on demand. He could not pay them. He was declared bankrupt in 1682, ten years after that January morning, and died in the Netherlands the following year. Sir Robert Vyner, owed over £400,000, was reduced to petitioning Parliament for relief while serving as Lord Mayor of London. Five major goldsmith houses failed; nine more were severely undermined; over ten thousand wealthy families lost their deposits.

The legal battles dragged for nearly three decades. The Court of Exchequer ruled for the bankers in 1692 and was reversed on technical grounds in 1696. The House of Lords found for them in 1700, but Parliament's settlement the following year limited interest to 3 percent from December 1705 only — no compensation for the missing twenty-four years. By the time the dust settled the effective return on the goldsmiths' loan to the king worked out to roughly 0.01 percent annually. The pattern was clear: sovereign default on elite creditors produced decades of litigation and eventual partial compensation. Ordinary depositors got nothing. And the City of London learned a lesson it would not forget: the king's word, by itself, was not a foundation strong enough to build a banking system on. The same problem would emerge again in 2008, when Lehman's collapse revealed that modern shadow banking rested on the same private-credit-without-public-backstop logic. The Federal Reserve had to step in for the same reason that, in 1694, England would have to invent something new.

---

### The circular trap

By the 1680s, England's monarchy faced a paradox that defined the age: war required credit, but credit required trust, and trust required institutions the crown did not control. A king could raise an army, but he could not raise the money to pay it. He could declare war, but he could not compel his subjects to finance it. And he could not borrow abroad without offering collateral—metal, goods, or credible promises—that his domestic authority could not guarantee.

France faced the same problem. Louis XIV waged nearly continuous war from 1667 to 1714, draining the kingdom's wealth and driving it into debt. French kings borrowed from tax farmers—private financiers who paid the crown upfront for the right to collect taxes—but the system was corrupt and inefficient. Lenders demanded exorbitant interest because they knew the king might default. And when he did, as in the repudiation of debts in 1648 and 1661, it only made future borrowing more expensive.

Spain's monarchy, despite the wealth of its American empire, declared bankruptcy in 1557, 1560, 1575, 1596, 1607, 1627, and 1647. Each default destroyed the fortunes of Genoese and German bankers who had financed Habsburg wars. Yet each time, the cycle repeated: war, borrowing, default, and a new generation of creditors willing to gamble on royal promises.

The pattern was universal. Monarchs could not wage modern war without credit. But they could not sustain credit without surrendering control over the very power—taxation—that made them sovereign. The solution, when it came, would not be a financial innovation but a political revolution.

### Why foreign creditors demanded gold

The distinction between domestic and foreign borrowing was not merely technical—it reflected fundamental differences in what creditors could enforce. English tally sticks worked brilliantly within England because they represented claims on specific tax revenues that domestic holders could collect. A tally reading "£9 4s 4d from Fulk Basset for the farm of Wycombe" identified both the revenue source and the tax collector. Domestic merchants could present these tallies to Exchequer officials, verify their authenticity, and receive payment. The system required access to English institutions but no commodity backing.

Foreign creditors faced entirely different constraints. A Dutch investor holding English tally sticks could not easily collect from English tax receivers without domestic agents. He could not verify the creditworthiness of specific revenue streams. Most critically, he could not use tally sticks as payment in Amsterdam—they were worthless outside England's borders. While tallies circulated domestically as a form of money, they embodied information asymmetries that made them unsuitable for international finance.

This explains why foreign wars required gold and silver in ways domestic finance did not. When England hired German mercenaries or purchased Dutch ships, foreign suppliers demanded payment in universally accepted media.

Gold and silver settled across borders without requiring trust in foreign legal systems or knowledge of domestic revenue collection. They avoided currency conversion risks. They provided liquid collateral that could be shipped or pledged internationally. Most importantly, they required no institutional access—a Hamburg merchant holding gold coins could spend them anywhere without navigating English Exchequer procedures.

The mechanism of international settlement reinforced this pattern. Most cross-border trade settled through bills of exchange rather than physical gold shipments. A Virginia tobacco producer could draw a bill on his London merchant account, sell it to a local merchant, who transmitted it to London to pay for imported goods. But bills of exchange worked only when both parties trusted the underlying banking networks. For final settlement of imbalances—when one country owed another more than it was owed—gold provided the universal medium that required no trust.

The Amsterdam Exchange Bank exemplified this system. Its "bank money" traded at a premium over circulating currency and was required by law for settling international bills of exchange. This created a two-tier system: international bills settled in Amsterdam bank money (ultimately backed by precious metal), while domestic transactions used various currencies. During the classical gold standard era (1870-1914), gold points—the exchange rate boundaries where physical gold shipment became profitable—typically ranged from 0.4% to 1.4% of par value, depending on shipping costs, insurance, and interest forgone. Most transactions settled within these bands through bills, but gold flowed at the margins to correct persistent imbalances.

Foreign creditors' preference for gold thus reflected practical realities rather than irrational commodity fetishism. Dutch investors who held 30.3% of Bank of England stock by 1750 did so because parliamentary guarantees made English debt credible even without gold backing. But those same investors, when lending to Spain or France, demanded either gold collateral or interest rates high enough to compensate for default risk. The Genoese bankers who financed Spanish wars through the *asiento* system structured contracts to receive American silver directly, bypassing the Spanish crown's unreliable promises. When Spain defaulted in 1557, 1575, 1596, 1607, and 1627, the Genoese lost fortunes—but they kept lending because silver collateral made partial recovery possible.

The fundamental problem was institutional. Domestic money worked when backed by domestic tax authority. International money required either commodity backing or institutional credibility that transcended borders. England's tally sticks, France's tax receipts, Spain's promises—all were domestic instruments that foreign creditors could not enforce. Gold and silver were the only media that settled internationally without requiring trust in foreign institutions. This asymmetry would drive the creation of the Bank of England and shape monetary architecture for centuries.

### The invention of the fiscal state

What Europe needed was a way to make royal debt credible without relying on royal virtue. The answer emerged not from economic theory but from constitutional struggle: parliamentary control of taxation. If a representative body, rather than the king alone, authorized taxes and guaranteed their use for debt repayment, then creditors could trust the nation even if they distrusted the monarch.

The Dutch Republic pioneered this model in the late sixteenth century. After rebelling against Spanish rule, the United Provinces created a federal system where provincial assemblies controlled taxation and borrowing. Because creditors knew that taxes were legally committed to debt service, they lent to the Dutch at interest rates far below those charged to absolute monarchs. The Dutch could borrow more, at lower cost, than any kingdom in Europe—and they used that advantage to build a commercial empire.

England would follow the same path, but only after a revolution. In 1688, when Parliament invited William of Orange to take the throne, it imposed a condition: the king would govern with Parliament, not above it. The Bill of Rights (1689) made explicit what the Civil War had implied—the crown could not raise taxes or maintain an army without parliamentary consent. That constitutional settlement transformed England's credit overnight.

For the first time, lenders could trust that their loans were backed not by a king's promise but by a nation's legal obligation. Parliament could commit future tax revenues to debt repayment, and no monarch could unilaterally repudiate that commitment. This birthed the fiscal-military state—a government capable of mobilizing resources on a scale that would have astonished Edward III or Charles II.

But the machinery of that state—the institution that would turn parliamentary promises into liquid credit—had yet to be invented. That innovation would come in 1694, when a group of London merchants proposed a radical solution: a bank that would lend to the government by creating money. The Bank of England would complete the revolution of 1688, transforming not just England's finances but the nature of money itself.

The solution to the sovereign's dilemma—how to create credible credit both domestically and internationally—would require institutional innovation on an unprecedented scale. As Part II will reveal, the answer emerged not from private ingenuity alone but from the fusion of constitutional revolution and financial engineering. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 made England's debt credible by transferring fiscal power to Parliament. The Bank of England's founding in 1694 completed this transformation by creating a mechanism to manufacture credit on demand. Together, these innovations would solve the problem that had plagued European monarchies for centuries: how to wage war without gold.

The asymmetry that trapped Edward III did not vanish with the medieval monarchies. In May 2010, Greece stood on the edge of default with €330 billion in debts it could not repay, and Greek citizens shouting "Print euros!" in the streets discovered what English kings had discovered seven centuries earlier: their government did not control its own currency. Greece had surrendered the drachma in 2001 in exchange for membership in a monetary union whose central bank sat in Frankfurt. Germany, using the same euro, could borrow at 2 percent; Greece paid 30. Both used the currency; only one issued it. Germany's debts were denominated in a currency its central bank could create. Greece's debts were denominated in a currency Greece had to acquire from others. The categories Edward III had run into in 1340 were precisely the categories the eurozone had recreated in 2010 — domestic credit you can issue, and foreign credit you must obtain — and the consequences ran the same way they had run for the Bardi and the Peruzzi. Currency sovereignty determined fiscal capacity then. It still does.

But the path to that solution would first require witnessing private money's spectacular failure. The goldsmith-bankers had created credit through fractional reserve lending, issuing receipts that circulated as currency. For three decades the system worked — until 1672 revealed its flaw. When the crown suspended debt payments, the entire network collapsed. What monarchies needed was not more private bankers but a public institution backed by the full fiscal power of the state, capable of making its promises believable to foreign creditors who would never accept a goldsmith's note.
