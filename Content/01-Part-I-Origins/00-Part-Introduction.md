# PART I: THE ORIGINS OF CREDIT MONEY

Money did not begin with coins or precious metals. It began with promises—recorded obligations managed by institutions powerful enough to enforce them. From the clay tablets of Mesopotamian temples to the bills of exchange circulating through medieval Europe, credit preceded commodity by millennia. The medium mattered less than the institutional capacity to impose and enforce obligations.

But every early credit system shared a fatal limit. It worked within a zone of trust—a temple, a city, a kingdom—and stopped at the border. A clay tablet meant nothing in another empire. A merchant's bill of exchange meant nothing to a creditor who did not know his name. A king's tally stick meant nothing in a country where his tax collectors could not reach.

That limit would shape three thousand years of monetary history, and would eventually force the invention of something new.
