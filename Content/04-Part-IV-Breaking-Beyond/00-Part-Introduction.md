# PART IV: THE BREAKING AND BEYOND

The gold standard collapsed across five decades, not in a single moment. Churchill's 1925 return to pre-war parity triggered the General Strike. The Great Depression proved that gold's "automatic adjustment" was a myth — countries that left gold earlier recovered faster, those that clung longer suffered worse. Roosevelt broke the dollar from gold in 1933 and recovery began the same year. Bretton Woods in 1944 tried to preserve gold's discipline while allowing fiat's flexibility, and embedded a contradiction Robert Triffin would identify in 1959: the world needed dollars for trade, but providing enough dollars eventually had to undermine the gold backing. By August 1971 the math had become impossible. Nixon closed the gold window and called it temporary. It was permanent.

What emerged after 1971 was money in its honest form: a human institution, created by law and sustained by trust, serving the economy rather than constraining it. The age of pure fiat had begun — and with it, a new question. Gold had been a constraint on government power; without it, the only constraint left was institutional discipline. The 2008 and 2020 crises would test whether central banks could create trillions in new money without losing control of inflation. They could, when productive capacity remained. They could not, when supply chains broke. Liberation from gold brought new challenges of power and distribution. Fiat money's flexibility serves whoever designs the transmission mechanism — bondholders if you create money through asset purchases, households if you create it through direct fiscal transfers. The question is no longer whether we have enough money. The question is whose needs the system serves.
