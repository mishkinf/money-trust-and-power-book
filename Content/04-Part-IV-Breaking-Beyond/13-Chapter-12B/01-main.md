# PART IV: THE BREAKING AND BEYOND

## Chapter 12B: The Tests

The 2008 Federal Reserve response, the 2020 COVID mobilization, and the Bank of Japan's three-decade balance-sheet expansion validated the framework Chapter 12A laid out. Money created against abundant productive capacity does not inflate. Money created against destroyed productive capacity does. The constraint is real resources, not metal. That much, the post-1971 record settles.

What the validation does not settle is the harder set of questions the framework's critics have raised. Three of them, in particular, name limits the productive-capacity argument does not by itself address.

The first is the Buchanan–Wagner public-choice critique: even if productive capacity is the real constraint, what stops a democratic government, especially in election years, from spending past it? The second is the Sargent–Wallace and Cochrane fiscal-dominance argument: monetary discipline cannot anchor the price level on its own when fiscal policy is set independently and deficit-biased. The third is Hayek's knowledge problem: anyone trying to read sectoral capacity from the center is doing a computation Hayek argued is impossible in principle. Each of these objections names a real limit the productive-capacity framework needs an institutional answer to.

This chapter takes them in turn. It begins with the 1970s and Volcker's recession — the previous episode in which capacity was binding and inflation arrived, and the case that most directly tests what fiat money does when its institutions are stressed. It then walks through the three critiques on their own terms. It closes with the two traditions that have proposed institutional answers to those critiques — the rules-based response from the public-choice school and the stabilizer-based response from the modern Modern Monetary Theory tradition — and what the historical record this book has traced suggests about how the choice between them has tested in practice.

### The 1970s Inflation Crisis: When Real Resources Became the Constraint

The 1970s inflation crisis—when U.S. inflation reached 13.5% in 1980—is often cited as proof that fiat money inevitably fails. The real story reveals what happens when supply shocks reduce productive capacity beyond monetary policy's control.

OPEC's oil embargo of October 1973, followed by the 1973 and 1979 price increases, did not just raise prices. It reduced the economy's productive capacity. Oil rose from three dollars a barrel in 1972 to twelve dollars in 1974 and thirty-nine dollars by 1980 — a thirteen-fold increase in eight years. This was not too much money chasing too few goods. It was fewer goods available for any amount of money. Less energy meant factories could not run at full capacity, trucks could not deliver goods, and the economy could literally produce less, regardless of monetary policy.

Central banks faced what was, in effect, a binary decision. They could accommodate the shock and let prices rise to maintain employment, accepting the resulting inflation. Or they could suppress demand by raising rates, forcing unemployment to reduce inflation through recession. Neither option addressed the root problem of insufficient energy production. Monetary policy could only determine who bore the cost of the shortage — consumers through inflation, or workers through unemployment. This was structurally distinct from the 1960s episode in which excess money creation at full capacity had bid up prices and tightening could solve the problem; in the 1970s, reduced productive capacity caused inflation regardless of money supply.

Arthur Burns (Fed Chair 1970-1978) correctly diagnosed "cost-push" inflation and recognized that monetary policy could not create real resources. Better tools existed in principle—Strategic Petroleum Reserve, coordinated energy policy, temporary incomes policy. Whether any was politically achievable when wage-price expectations were already spiraling, and Carter's voluntary guidelines had failed, is the harder question. Instead the U.S. chose Volcker's recession (1979–1982), tightening so aggressively that 10.8 percent unemployment was the predictable cost of breaking wage-price dynamics. The recession "worked" by redistributing the cost of the supply shock from price-paying creditors to wage-earning workers, not by solving the energy shortage.

### Volcker's recession: Breaking inflation through mass unemployment

Paul Volcker's appointment as Fed Chair in August 1979 marked a turning point. The man who had drafted the contingency plans at Treasury in 1971 and watched Connally overrule him at Camp David was now the man Carter had asked to defend the dollar that the Camp David weekend had set adrift. Volcker himself was an austere figure — six feet seven inches tall, prone to a cigar, anti-romantic in temperament, and preoccupied throughout his career with credibility, expectations, and the institutional discipline he believed central banking required. The voice that runs through his memoir and his FOMC remarks is institutional rather than ideological: he was not arguing that gold should return or that fiat had failed, but that what he repeatedly called "inflationary psychology" had become entrenched, and that the longer the regime lasted the worse the eventual reckoning would be. He understood, too, that aggressive U.S. tightening would amplify debt vulnerability in major borrowing countries — Argentina, Brazil, Mexico — and proceeded anyway.

The conventional account — that Volcker broke the Great Inflation through sustained monetary tightening, reset expectations, and laid the foundation for the Great Moderation — captures most of what happened, and the chapter does not dispute it. What the conventional account tends to elide is the distributional pattern in which the disinflation arrived, and the specific households and communities that paid for it. The chapter takes both halves of the record seriously: what Volcker prevented and what he imposed. The mechanism Volcker used was demand suppression: rates high enough to force the business failure and unemployment that would break the wage-price dynamic that had become entrenched. He understood the cost. He chose to pay it because he believed the alternative — letting inflation expectations de-anchor permanently into an indexation regime that would have tracked the South American pattern of the 1970s and 1980s — was worse. The case for that judgment, on the evidence available to him in 1979 and visible to us in retrospect, is the subject of the section after the human-cost vignettes that follow.

The Fed funds rate reached 20 percent in June 1981—the highest in U.S. history. Unemployment peaked at 10.8 percent in November 1982—the highest since the Great Depression. The recession was devastating: GDP contracted 2.9% in the year ending Q4 1982. But these aggregate numbers obscured the concentrated devastation in specific industries and regions.

#### The human wreckage

The aggregate numbers obscured concentrated devastation in specific industries and regions. Youngstown, Ohio, lost forty thousand steelworkers between 1977 and 1983 as the Campbell Works, U.S. Steel, and Republic Steel all closed; with twenty-percent interest rates, laid-off workers could neither buy nor refinance nor sell. Farmland values fell sixty percent between 1981 and 1986; Iowa farm foreclosures doubled, and rural Main Streets emptied across the Midwest. Housing starts fell forty-five percent — from 2.0 million to 1.1 million units — and construction lost eight hundred thousand jobs. Michigan's unemployment hit 16.9 percent; Flint's exceeded twenty. The auto industry lost three hundred thousand jobs. Detroit, Cleveland, and Pittsburgh entered long-term decline from which many neighborhoods never recovered. The Rust Belt was born.

Dean and Kaye Hagedorn's hundred-year-old family farm near Spencer, Iowa, exemplified the human cost. The farm had passed through three generations: 560 acres of corn and beans on flat black-soil prairie, the kind of land Iowa farms had been built on since homesteading. Dean's father had paid down the original mortgage during the New Deal years; Dean himself had taken over in the late 1960s and farmed it through the boom decade that followed. The 1970s commodity boom — driven partly by the 1972 Soviet wheat purchase, partly by inflation that pushed food prices up faster than nominal interest rates — looked like a permanent shift to Iowa farmers and their bankers alike. Dean borrowed to expand. He bought additional acreage, upgraded the equipment, took out a Production Credit Association line of credit at the rates of the time. His banker called it good farming. His debt was within what the cash flow could service when interest rates were six or seven percent and corn was four dollars a bushel.

Volcker's tightening killed both halves of that math at once. By 1981 the prime rate was above twenty percent and the PCA rate had moved with it; by 1982 corn had fallen below three dollars and Iowa farmland values had begun the slide that would take them down sixty percent before it ended. Dean's September 1982 diary entry, written on lined notebook paper that survives in the family's records, says it as plainly as anyone has said it: "I've never been so terrified in my life. We owe over $18,000 just on interest for PCA for one year! What do you do?" He did what farmers in his position did: he borrowed against next year's crop, refinanced where he could, sold off equipment to pay the interest, watched his banker grow gradually less friendly through 1983 and 1984. Kaye took a job in town. The children — there were three — understood that something was wrong without being told what.

The end came in the fall of 1985. The corn harvest that year was a hundred and forty-six thousand bushels, a strong year by historical standards, worth ninety-four thousand dollars on the local elevator. The bank took the entire check. Against the accumulated debt — more than three hundred thousand dollars after four years of compound interest on rolled-over notes — it barely covered a year's interest. The Hagedorns filed Chapter 12 bankruptcy in early 1986 and lost the farm at auction that summer. The buyer was a corporate operation out of Des Moines that had done its arithmetic on land prices that had now fallen to a level Dean's grandfather had paid in the 1920s. The Hagedorn name came off the mailbox. The diary stopped soon after. They had done nothing wrong. The PCA loan had been prudent at the rates it was contracted at. The expansion had been responsible against the cash flows of the time. What had changed was none of those things. What had changed was the federal funds rate, set in Washington by a Federal Reserve that had concluded — facing what it took to be a worse alternative — that the distributional cost of breaking the wage-price dynamic was the lesser evil. The cost turned out to be the Hagedorn farm, along with roughly two hundred thousand others between 1981 and 1986. Whether the Fed was right about the alternative is the question the next section takes up.

The Hagedorns' tragedy was replicated nationwide. The recession's costs fell on workers (12 million unemployed at the peak), farmers (more than 200,000 foreclosures), and industrial communities (Youngstown lost 40,000 steel jobs). The disinflation's benefits — a permanent reset of wage-price expectations, headline inflation falling from 13.5 percent to 3.2 percent in three years, and the institutional foundation for what later became known as the Great Moderation — accrued more diffusely and over a longer horizon, but they were real. Whether the trade was worth what it cost is the question the historical record allows us to ask in retrospect, and that the Fed of 1979 had to decide in real time without the benefit of hindsight.

### The counterfactual: Argentina, the alternative Volcker thought he was preventing

The strongest defense of Volcker's choice is that the alternative was worse — not in some abstract sense, but in a specific sense visible on the historical record of what entrenched inflation does to a country. Bernanke's 2005 essay on very high inflation in Latin America, written from inside the Federal Reserve System and rooted in the same intellectual tradition that informed Volcker's 1979 decision, gives the analytic statement: when inflation expectations de-anchor and an indexation regime takes hold, monetary policy loses its grip on the price level, the cost of any later disinflation rises, and the political feasibility of disinflation falls. The longer the regime persists, the harder it is to break, and the larger the eventual real economic cost.

The cleanest cases were South American. Argentina between 1975 and 1990 ran inflation that averaged roughly 300 percent annually and reached 5,000 percent in 1989. Over those fifteen years Argentine real per-capita GDP fell about 20 percent. Real wages fell roughly 40 percent. Saving in pesos became impossible — anyone who could moved their wealth into dollars or hard assets, and a domestic financial sector capable of funding long-term productive investment effectively ceased to exist. Poverty in greater Buenos Aires rose from 5 percent of households in 1975 to 38 percent by 1989. Five separate stabilization programs failed before the 1991 currency board pegged the peso to the dollar — a program that itself collapsed a decade later in the 2001 default that wiped out savings for the second time in a generation.

Brazil's experience between 1980 and 1994 was structurally similar, although the indexation regime that took hold made daily life functional in a way Argentina's did not. Real wages and pensions were re-indexed to the price level monthly or weekly; price stickers in São Paulo supermarkets were replaced every morning. Ordinary Brazilian families experienced a kind of running treadmill — nominal incomes rose constantly, purchasing power held only because everything around the household was being repriced at the same rate. The cost was less visible than Argentina's poverty figures but no less real: a domestic financial sector that could not fund anything beyond short-term contracts; an economy organized around the politics of indexation rather than productive investment; a working-class household that could not save toward retirement, education, or a home in any conventional sense. The Plano Real of 1994 finally broke the inflation; it required a new currency, a substantially reformed central bank, and political conditions that took fifteen years to assemble.

These were the regimes Volcker thought he was facing in 1979. American inflation was 13.5 percent that year and rising. Wage indexation had spread through major union contracts. Inflation expectations measured in survey data showed double-digit inflation as the median forecast for the next decade. Carter's voluntary wage-price guidelines had collapsed; the energy reforms that might have addressed the supply side had failed politically; Burns at the Fed had been unable or unwilling to apply sufficient monetary restraint. The window for stabilization was visibly closing. Bernanke's later analytic point — that the longer the delay, the larger the eventual cost — was the operating logic of Volcker's 1979 decision in a non-academic vocabulary. He believed, watching Argentina and Brazil and the early indexation behaviors emerging in American wage bargaining, that another five years of accommodating stagflation would carry the United States across the line where disinflation became politically impossible.

The cost the alternative would have imposed, had Volcker accommodated for another five years, is harder to specify than the cost of his recession because the counterfactual was not run. But the rough order is visible in the South American cases. A doubled poverty rate. A 20 percent fall in real per-capita GDP. The destruction of any saving instrument denominated in the domestic currency. A working-class retiree on a fixed Social Security or private pension watching her purchasing power erode by half over a decade — with no recourse, no political weight to demand re-indexation, and no functioning financial market in which to escape into hard assets the way wealthier households could. The American household that lost the most under Volcker's recession was the Hagedorn farm. The American household that would have lost the most under entrenched inflation was the passbook saver, the fixed-pension retiree, the wage-earner without indexation in her contract. The two costs are not the same.

The aggregate U.S. evidence from the 1970s sharpens the point. Real interest paid on passbook savings accounts was negative in every year from 1973 through 1981 — depositors lost real purchasing power even on conservative savings, year after year. Social Security retirement benefits, though indexed annually after 1972, lagged the price level enough that the typical retiree's real consumption fell several percentage points over the decade. Households whose wealth sat in long-duration nominal bonds — a substantial share of middle-class retirement portfolios — watched the real value of those bonds fall by close to half between 1972 and 1981. None of these costs entered the GDP statistics that the Volcker recession would later show up in so clearly; they appeared as reduced real consumption among retirees, deferred retirement among workers without indexed pensions, and the slow erosion of a class of household savings that the political system had no mechanism to compensate. They were not the Hagedorn farm. They were the wage-earner who reached retirement age in 1980 and discovered that what she had saved across thirty years of postwar prosperity was worth half what it had been worth when the saving was done.

Volcker's choice was therefore not between cruelty and gentleness. It was between two patterns of cruelty — one acute and concentrated, one chronic and diffuse — under conditions in which both costs were already partly being paid. The political economy of the choice is recognizable: the diffuse cost was politically inert (the retiree and the wage-earner could not organize themselves the way the auto union or the farm bloc could) while the concentrated cost was politically explosive (rural foreclosure crisis, Rust Belt collapse, sustained protests outside the Federal Reserve building). A democratic system facing a choice between distributional patterns of those two shapes will tend, on the historical record, to defer the concentrated cost and accept the diffuse one. Volcker's contribution was to refuse that calculation.

This does not absolve the costs he imposed, and it does not establish that no other path was available. The chapter has named the alternatives an earlier political coalition might have built — incomes policy, aggressive supply-side energy reform, the institutional fiscal-monetary coordination that Sargent and Wallace would have required — and noted that none of them were politically achievable in the Carter–Volcker years. What it establishes is that the cost of *not* breaking inflation in 1979 was not zero, and that the people who would have borne that cost were a recognizable class of American households whose suffering would not have appeared on the same charts that documented the recession. Friedman and Schwartz, in *A Monetary History*, made the symmetric case for 1929–33: that the Federal Reserve declined to act when action would have prevented a larger cost. Bernanke applied that lesson in 2008. Volcker applied a structurally similar judgment in 1979, accepting one cost to prevent another. The Hagedorns paid a price; so did the families that the alternative would have ruined. Both costs are part of the historical record of the disinflation, and a balanced account of what the period demanded must hold them in view together.

This is the *paired vindication* Chapter 8 named in a different vocabulary. There, Attwood was directionally right that monetary contraction destroys real lives and Peel and Overstone were directionally right that an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master needs institutional architecture to constrain it; the contest was over which worry to architect against. Here the structure repeats. Volcker's Federal Reserve architected against entrenched indexation and accepted what disinflation did to Iowa farms and Rust Belt towns. The household whose savings were preserved by the disinflation and the household whose farm was lost to the prime rate were both vindicated against the alternative that would have ruined them; the institutional choice was about which kind of cruelty the system would accept. The two-sided account is not balance for its own sake. It is what the historical record produces when read whole.

By 1983, inflation had fallen from 13.5 percent to 3.2 percent — not because the oil shortage was solved or productive capacity restored, but because sustained monetary tightening had reset wage-price expectations through real economic contraction. Workers, facing unemployment, stopped demanding wage increases. Businesses, facing bankruptcy, stopped raising prices. The reset took years of lost output, destroyed careers, communities that did not recover. It also produced the foundation for the Great Moderation that followed: a quarter-century in which inflation stayed broadly anchored, monetary policy regained operational credibility, and the political-economic conditions for the post-1990 expansions became possible. Whether the trade was net favorable depends on which households and which decades the accounting weights — and that, ultimately, is the question this chapter has tried to keep open rather than close.

### Distributional consequences

The 2008 response revealed something the 1797 Bank Restriction's defenders had not had to confront in their own lifetimes: that fiat money's flexibility serves those who design the transmission mechanism. Quantitative easing inflated asset prices — the S&P 500 rose 186 percent between 2009 and 2015; housing prices were up 50 percent by 2019 — while median wages stagnated. The wealthiest 10 percent of households owned 84 percent of stocks; the bottom 50 percent owned essentially nothing. COVID-19's direct fiscal transfers ($1,200 payments, enhanced unemployment) produced a more equitable recovery than 2008's asset-purchase-only approach, demonstrating the same point in the other direction: the architecture of money creation determines who benefits, and that architecture is a political choice.

The eurozone is the contrasting case. The Maastricht Treaty banned the European Central Bank from acting as lender of last resort — a design choice that distinguished the eurozone from every successful currency union and forced adjustment through unemployment when asymmetric shocks hit. Greece in 2010 was Churchill's 1925 in a different vocabulary, as Chapter 9 traced. Only Mario Draghi's 2012 "whatever it takes" declaration belatedly established the lender-of-last-resort function that should have existed from inception. The euro succeeded as a medium of exchange but struggled as a complete monetary system because it deliberately excluded the fiscal backing that makes fiat currencies work.

### The public-choice critique deserves an answer

Critics raise concerns that deserve a serious answer. The strongest version of the case against discretionary fiscal policy was made by James Buchanan and Richard Wagner in *Democracy in Deficit* (1977), and it does not depend on metallism. Their argument is institutional. Once the technical constraint of solvency is removed, what stops a democratic government from running deficits — especially in election years — beyond what productive capacity can absorb? Voters tend to misperceive the cost of deficit finance, underestimating the future tax burden and the inflation tax. Politicians' time horizons are short. Tax cuts and spending increases have concentrated, salient beneficiaries; tax increases and spending cuts impose diffuse, painful losses. Surpluses in good times, which Keynesian theory requires, are politically unrewarding in a way that deficits in bad times are not. William Niskanen's analysis of bureaucratic incentives points in the same direction: agencies have structural reasons to expand budgets regardless of macroeconomic conditions, and information asymmetries let them. The 1970s, by Buchanan's reading, were not an aberration but a natural consequence of replacing the old "fiscal constitution" of balanced peacetime budgets with technocratic discretion.

### Fiscal dominance: a deeper objection

The strongest restatements of this concern run through monetary economics rather than public choice, and they cut close to the bone of the framework defended in this book. Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace's 1981 "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic" showed that under what they called fiscal dominance — when the fiscal authority sets primary deficits independently and the central bank is left to finance whatever surpluses do not appear — monetary restraint cannot anchor the price level on its own. "Tighter money now," they wrote, "can mean higher inflation eventually," because the debt arithmetic has to balance, and if expected primary surpluses do not arrive on the schedule the government has implicitly promised, the price level itself becomes the variable that adjusts. John Cochrane's 2023 *The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* generalizes the same intuition into a statement about how price levels are determined. "The fiscal theory says that the price level adjusts so that the real value of nominal debt is equal to the present value of primary surpluses." If credible expected surpluses do not exist, the adjustment runs through inflation rather than through repayment.

Both arguments name a constraint the productive-capacity framework does not, by itself, address. The framework explains *when* nominal spending meets real bottlenecks — when an economy at or near full utilization absorbs additional demand into prices rather than into output. Sargent-Wallace and the fiscal theory explain *why a nominal anchor can fail in the first place* — why the central bank's interest-rate rule, no matter how disciplined, cannot determine long-run inflation when fiscal policy is not providing the primary surpluses the rule presupposes. They are not the same claim, and it is a category error to treat them as one. They answer different questions. A complete account of fiat money has to answer both.

The historical record contains episodes that look like fiscal dominance. The United Kingdom's postwar policy of holding gilt yields below the inflation rate while running large deficits — the financial repression of 1945–1951 — produced an effective inflation tax that resolved much of the wartime debt overhang at savers' expense. Brazil's 1980s and early-1990s indexation regime, in which the price level rose roughly with the velocity of fiscal-monetary accommodation, was the textbook case of monetary policy chasing fiscal facts. Turkey's 2021–2024 episode, in which a politicized central bank pursued unorthodox cuts while deficits ran wide, produced annual inflation above 70 percent and a currency that lost three-quarters of its dollar value in three years. None of these episodes is consistent with a simple "productive capacity is the only constraint" reading. Each looks more like Sargent-Wallace or the fiscal theory in concrete form: a state issuing currency without the credible future surpluses to back the debt path it was running, and a price level that adjusted accordingly.

What the productive-capacity framework adds, when set alongside Sargent-Wallace and the fiscal theory, is the conditional under which fiscal-monetary coordination is *not* dominant: a regime in which the central bank operates with credible independence, the fiscal authority commits to long-run primary surpluses sufficient to back the debt path it is running, and the institutions enforcing both commitments are politically robust enough to survive election cycles. Under those conditions, productive capacity becomes the binding constraint, because the nominal anchor is intact. Outside those conditions — and the conditions are not automatic — the binding constraint is fiscal-monetary credibility itself, and Sargent-Wallace's arithmetic asserts itself. What kind of institutions can sustain those conditions is the question the two traditions sketched later in this chapter take up in different ways.

### The knowledge problem

There is a deeper Hayekian objection that does not fit neatly under public choice or fiscal dominance, and the chapter is honestly stronger for naming it. Hayek's 1945 article "The Use of Knowledge in Society" argued that the central problem of economic order is that the knowledge relevant to the allocation of resources — what is scarce, what is wanted, what can be substituted, what is being produced where — is not given to anyone in its totality. It is dispersed among millions of producers, consumers, and traders, much of it tacit, all of it changing. The price system works, on Hayek's reading, because it condenses that dispersed knowledge into signals that decentralized actors can act on without needing to grasp the full underlying picture. A central planner, no matter how well-intentioned or institutionally robust, cannot substitute for that mechanism, because the planner does not have access to the underlying knowledge. The argument is what Hayek made in 1945, and it is what *Denationalisation of Money* (1976) extended into a critique of central-bank monopoly: the state's control over money removes the disciplinary function of competition and substitutes the discretion of agents who, on the knowledge-problem reading, cannot have what they would need to know to exercise that discretion well.

Apply that argument to the productive-capacity framework. The framework treats sectoral capacity as the binding constraint and proposes that fiscal councils, independent central banks, and inflation-threshold triggers can read sectoral slack in real time. Hayek's worry is precisely that they cannot — that the chip shortage in 2021 looked like a localized supply story until it wasn't, that the labor-market tightening of 2022 was visible to the participants months before it appeared in the aggregate data, that any institution charged with reading the output gap from the center is doing the kind of computation Hayek argued is impossible in principle. The Buchanan-Wagner critique attacks political incentives. Sargent-Wallace attacks debt sustainability. Hayek's knowledge-problem critique attacks the underlying epistemic premise: that anyone, sitting at the center of an economy, can know well enough and quickly enough what the binding constraint is at any given moment. The chapter does not have a clean answer to this objection. Both of the institutional traditions sketched below — the rules-based and the stabilizer-based — face it. The rules-based tradition needs a fiscal council that can read the output gap from the center; the stabilizer-based tradition needs to identify when the job guarantee's automatic adjustment is enough and when discretionary action is required on top. Neither escapes the knowledge problem. The honest position is that the conservative tradition is partly right about something the framework cannot resolve by design, and that any institutional answer has to be built with humility about what it can know.

### Two traditions, one institutional question

The public-choice, fiscal-dominance, and knowledge-problem critiques all name the same gap. The framework explains *what* the binding constraint on currency-issuing governments is — productive capacity at sectoral resolution — but does not by itself specify *how* democratic political institutions can be expected to respect that constraint when the political costs of doing so are high. The gap is real, and the framework does not close it without help.

Two traditions have proposed answers, and the choice between them is one of the live political-economy debates the framework opens up. This chapter does not settle the choice; settling it is the work of policy debates the descriptive case enables, and those debates require their own arguments and their own books. What the chapter does is present each tradition's answer on its own terms and then note what the historical record this book has traced suggests about how the choice has tested in practice.

#### The rules-based answer

The first tradition, descending from Buchanan-Wagner and the broader public-choice school, proposes that the only durable answer to political incentives is institutional pre-commitment. Politicians cannot be trusted, in real time, to enact the surpluses booms require or the tax adjustments inflation eventually demands; the tools have to be removed from the political path. The architecture is rules-based: constitutional or quasi-constitutional fiscal rules requiring symmetric responses across the cycle (automatic deficits in slumps, automatic surpluses in booms); independent fiscal councils with binding authority over the non-inflationary fiscal envelope; pre-legislated automatic tax and transfer adjustments triggered at named inflation thresholds; sunset clauses on discretionary spending programs.

Three contemporary cases approximate pieces of this design. The European Union's post-2024 revised fiscal framework requires member states to submit medium-term net-expenditure plans with debt-reduction trajectories, evaluated against country-specific debt-sustainability analyses produced by the European Commission. It has real teeth on the deficit side — the Excessive Deficit Procedure has been activated against deficit overshoots — and notional ones on the surplus side; the procedure has never been activated to compel a surplus during a boom. The United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility, established in 2010, sits closer to the institutional posture of an independent fiscal council; it is independent, produces official forecasts, and assesses Treasury policy against the government's stated rules. What it does not have is binding authority — the Treasury can publish a Budget the OBR has flagged as inconsistent with the government's own rules, and the rules can be revised when they bind. The Chilean structural-balance rule, adopted in 2001, has run for nearly twenty-five years as the closest contemporary working example of automatic stabilizers operating at scale, with independent expert committees estimating trend GDP and the long-run copper price and the budget operating against the structural fiscal balance these estimates produce.

The rules-based tradition argues that these architectures, refined and made more binding, are how a fiat regime durably resists the political-economy pressures Buchanan-Wagner named. The Chilean rule is, in this reading, a prototype; the EU's asymmetric weakness is a design failure to be corrected; the OBR's lack of binding authority is a remaining gap to close. A Buchanan-compatible regime would marry the EU's binding authority with symmetric triggers, the OBR's institutional independence with binding fiscal-envelope authority, and the Chilean rule's automatic stabilizers extended from commodity-price smoothing to inflation control more broadly.

Where the tradition is most vulnerable on the historical record now available is in cases where the rules themselves caused the harm they were designed to prevent. The clearest contemporary example is the eurozone after 2010. The Maastricht Treaty's three-percent-deficit and sixty-percent-debt rules were Buchanan-style binding constraints, removed from democratic contestation. When asymmetric shocks hit the periphery, the rules functioned as their architects expected: they prevented expansionary fiscal response. The result was Greek pensioners cut by forty percent, Spanish youth unemployment above fifty percent, deflationary policy that prolonged the recession across the continent for half a decade. Mario Draghi's 2012 "whatever it takes" declaration marked the European Central Bank's belated recognition that the lender-of-last-resort function the Maastricht design had excluded was structurally necessary. The fiscal architecture has been revised repeatedly since. The case shows that rules-based regimes are not neutral — they encode a judgment about which failure mode the system needs to guard against, and getting that judgment wrong has real human costs that fall on the same constituencies that bore the cost of the gold standard's discipline a century earlier.

#### The stabilizer-based answer

The second tradition, descending from the modern Modern Monetary Theory literature — Wray, Mosler, Kelton, Tcherneva, Mehrling — proposes a different answer to the same critique. Where the rules-based tradition responds to political-incentive failures by removing tools from the political path, the stabilizer-based tradition responds by building countercyclical mechanisms into the structural economy that operate without requiring real-time political action.

The flagship proposal is the federal job guarantee. A federally funded employment program at a fixed wage, available to anyone willing to work, automatically expands in slumps — when private demand contracts, workers who lose jobs take the guaranteed-employment wage, fiscal outlays rise, real demand is supported. The program automatically contracts in booms — when private wages rise above the guarantee floor, workers leave the program for better-paid private work, fiscal outlays fall, no separate political action is required to enact the contraction. The mechanism does the countercyclical work Buchanan-style fiscal rules try to do, without disabling the discretionary policy response that democratic accountability sometimes requires. Inflation, in this framing, is addressed through the same combination of mechanisms — tax adjustments at organized labor and capital, antitrust enforcement against pricing power, sectoral capacity investment — that have addressed it historically when democratic systems have actually had to.

The argument extends to a deeper political-economy question. The Buchanan tradition assumes voters punish spending cuts but not inflation; therefore the inflation-control mechanism has to be removed from democratic politics or the system fails. The stabilizer-based tradition observes that this assumption is empirically wrong. Voters punish hyperinflation severely when it arrives. Carter lost in 1980 partly because of inflation. Macron, Trudeau, and Biden's party lost seats in 2022–24 in part because of inflation. The "voters do not care about inflation" assumption that grounds Buchanan-Wagner's structural pessimism does not match the political record across democracies. Democratic accountability already includes inflation accountability, slower and noisier than rules-based regimes promise but real and observable across the post-1971 record.

The deeper claim, made most directly by Kelton, is that the language of fiscal discipline has been used asymmetrically. The "we cannot afford it" framing of fiscal policy has been deployed for forty years to block universal healthcare, climate spending, and infrastructure investment, but has rarely been deployed to block tax cuts for asset-holders or military spending. Buchanan's binding rules entrench this asymmetry by treating excessive spending as the only failure mode the system has to guard against, while excessive austerity — under-spending on idle workers, under-investment in productive capacity, deflationary policy that prolongs recessions — is treated as the absence of a problem rather than as its own problem. The MMT tradition makes that asymmetry visible and rejects it. The household-budget metaphor is, in this reading, not an honest description of fiscal reality but a class commitment in technical clothing.

#### What the historical record this book has traced suggests

The choice between these traditions is, ultimately, a political-economy commitment that the historical record alone cannot fully settle. But the record this book has traced is not silent on the question, and a balanced summary runs in a particular direction.

The post-1971 record of advanced-economy fiat regimes is more often a record of running too tight than running too loose. Japan's three-decade balance-sheet expansion against persistent deflation. The eurozone's prolonged post-2010 recession under Maastricht-style rules. The slow U.S. recovery from 2009 to 2014 against a Federal Reserve that repeatedly worried about inflation that did not arrive. The 2021–22 episode is the only clear case of an advanced-economy democracy producing significant inflation under fiat conditions, and the post-hoc decompositions attribute most of it to supply shocks layered on top of stimulus rather than to the structural deficit-bias Buchanan predicted. The "democratic inflation bias" that grounded the public-choice critique in 1977 is not, on the available record across the half-century since, the dominant failure mode of advanced-economy fiat regimes. Excessive caution has been at least as common as excessive looseness, and in several major cases more common.

Where rules-based regimes have been tried at scale, the historical record runs against them more often than for them. The eurozone's experience with Maastricht is the clearest contemporary case but not the only one. The classical gold standard, which is the deepest historical example of Buchanan's preferred discipline-from-rules approach, killed its way through the 1920s and 1930s when conditions demanded the response a rules-based regime had been designed to prevent. The 1925 return to gold under Churchill, the 1931 collapse of sterling, the U.S. clinging to gold through 1933 — all of these are cases of rules-based monetary regimes operating as their architects expected and producing precisely the human outcomes the regimes were ostensibly designed to make impossible. The pattern is consistent: rules-based regimes constrain at the moment constraint becomes counterproductive, because the conditions under which the rules were designed are not the conditions under which the rules eventually have to operate.

Where democratic systems have had to address inflation, they have. Volcker's 1979 disinflation was a democratic political action — appointed by Carter, sustained by Reagan, paid for by elected administrations that knew the costs and accepted them. The post-2022 turn to monetary tightening across advanced economies happened without binding fiscal rules forcing it. Democratic accountability for inflation is real, slower than rules-based regimes promise, and has been demonstrated across the post-1971 period when it has actually been needed. None of which proves the modern MMT tradition's preferred policy program right in detail. It does suggest, on the available historical evidence, that the structural pessimism about democratic capacity that grounded Buchanan-Wagner has been less empirically supported by the half-century since 1977 than the public-choice tradition's case for rules requires.

#### What the book contributes — and what it does not

The book's contribution is the historical case for the descriptive claims both traditions share. Money is institutional credit. Taxation drives currency demand. Productive capacity is the binding constraint on what currency-issuing governments can spend. These claims are the foundation on which any serious policy program — Buchanan's, Kelton's, or whatever synthesis the next generation builds — has to rest. The chapters of this book have assembled the historical evidence that the claims are true and the historical material that shows what is at stake when they are denied.

The choice between the rules-based and the stabilizer-based traditions is not a choice the historical record alone can fully settle. But the record is not silent on it. The eurozone case, the post-1971 pattern of advanced economies running too tight more often than too loose, and the recurring failure of rules-based regimes precisely when conditions demanded response — these together support a particular reading: that the modern MMT tradition's account of what the post-fiat institutional architecture should look like has stronger historical-record support than the rules-based tradition has acknowledged. The structural pessimism that grounds Buchanan-Wagner — the assumption that democratic systems will systematically over-issue without binding rules — is not what the half-century since 1977 has actually shown. The opposite failure mode, in which institutional caution and rules designed for an inflationary world produce avoidable contraction in a deflationary one, has been at least as common, and on several major occasions more costly. Readers are free to weigh the evidence differently. They are not free to do so without engaging it.

The historical record does not write policy. It does establish what kind of policy the evidence supports and what kind it does not. The household-budget metaphor describes a household, not a sovereign currency issuer. Productive capacity is the binding constraint, and institutional design has to manage it. The choice between rules-based and stabilizer-based architectures cannot be made by waving away the half-century of evidence that one of those architectures has more often produced the harm it was supposed to prevent. These are conclusions the historical record forces. What political program follows from them is the work of the political community. But the descriptive case is no longer optional, and the policy debate that proceeds without it has been operating without its strongest available foundation.

---

What the age of pure fiat has shown — across the 2008 response, the 2020 mobilization, the 2021 inflation, and the long shadow of Volcker's 1979 decision — is narrow but defensible. The binding constraint on what governments can spend in their own currency is not gold reserves or accumulated revenues; it is the economy's ability to produce goods and services, specified at sectoral as well as aggregate resolution. "Can we afford it?" becomes a question about real resources and political choice rather than about accounting. The institutional answer to that question is the live political-economy debate the chapter has sketched, between the rules-based tradition and the stabilizer-based tradition; the historical record this book has traced has weighted that debate without closing it, and the next chapter takes the implications directly into the debates currently underway, where the household-budget metaphor still operates as if a half-century of evidence had not put it past its expiration date.
