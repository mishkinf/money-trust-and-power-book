# Chapter 10 Sources & Summary

## **Chapter Summary**

In July 1944, 730 delegates from 44 nations gathered at the Mount Washington Hotel to design the postwar monetary system. Keynes's bancor — a supranational currency with symmetric adjustment for surplus as well as deficit countries — lost to White's dollar-centered system, backed by American gold holdings (80 percent of global reserves). The Bretton Woods compromise established dollar dominance but carried a structural tension Triffin would identify in 1960: the world needed dollar outflows for liquidity, requiring persistent U.S. deficits, while those same deficits steadily undermined the gold convertibility that anchored the system. The arrangement worked for 27 years, enabling rapid postwar trade expansion and economic growth. When pressures accumulated — France's gold offensive (1965–1968), Britain's 1967 sterling crisis, the London Gold Pool's $3 billion drain — Nixon closed the gold window on August 15, 1971. What was announced as temporary became permanent.
