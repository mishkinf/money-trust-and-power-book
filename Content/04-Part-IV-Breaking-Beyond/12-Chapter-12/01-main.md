# PART IV: THE BREAKING AND BEYOND

## Chapter 12: The Tests

The age of pure fiat began on August 15, 1971, with Nixon's Sunday-evening television address, and runs through the present. Across the half-century that followed, the world's monetary architecture has been backed by no commodity, anchored to no metal, constrained — in principle — only by what the productive economies of the issuing states could produce when called on. The architecture has been tested. This chapter walks through the four largest tests of the period and what each of them answered.

The question the orthodox tradition keeps asking the framework defended in this book runs like a refrain across every monetary debate of the post-1971 era: *if gold was never the foundation, what keeps fiat money from becoming fantasy?* Each of the four cases that follows is a partial answer, and each attaches one of the orthodox steelmans named in the Preface as the worry it tests. The 2008 financial crisis, with Japan's three-decade quantitative experiment as its chronic counterpart, tests Friedman-Schwartz's monetary-contraction worry. The 2021–22 inflation tests Buchanan-Wagner's public-choice critique of democratic deficit-bias and Sargent-Wallace's fiscal-dominance argument. The 1979–82 Volcker disinflation tests the Currency School's worry about discretionary issuance under fiscal pressure, through the counterfactual of what entrenched inflation does when no Volcker arrives. The cryptocurrency movement tests Hayek's knowledge-problem critique against the empirical record of what algorithmic scarcity has and has not delivered. Together the four cases describe the shape of the answer the framework can give to the governing question. They do not settle every disagreement; what they settle is whether the descriptive case is strong enough to bear the institutional architecture the next half-century has to build.

### Case 1 — 2008 and Japan: money creation without consumer inflation

The trading floor of the New York Federal Reserve was silent on the morning of March 16, 2008. Outside, Bear Stearns — one of Wall Street's oldest investment banks — was collapsing. Inside, Fed officials faced a choice that would have been unthinkable a generation earlier: create $30 billion out of thin air to facilitate JPMorgan's acquisition of Bear Stearns, or watch the financial system unravel. There was no gold to back the money, no reserves to draw down, no physical constraint. There was only a decision: would the Federal Reserve use its power to create money, or would it stand aside and let the crisis deepen?

The Fed chose to act. Over the next eighteen months, it would create trillions of dollars through programs with names like TALF, TARP, and quantitative easing. The money appeared as entries in electronic ledgers — the same double-entry bookkeeping that Bank of England clerks had used in 1697, now executed at the speed of light. Critics called it "printing money." Defenders called it "providing liquidity." Both were right. The Fed was doing what central banks had always done: creating purchasing power to serve public purposes. The difference was that in 2008, there was no pretense that gold backed the operation. The system ran on pure fiat — money created by decree, sustained by trust.

The proximate cause of the crisis was a collapse in U.S. housing prices, which triggered failures in mortgage-backed securities, which spread to investment banks, which threatened the entire global financial system. But the deeper cause was a failure of trust: banks stopped lending to each other because they did not know which institutions were solvent. Central banks responded with unprecedented interventions. The Federal Reserve cut interest rates to near zero and created new lending facilities to inject money directly into frozen markets. When these proved insufficient, the Fed launched quantitative easing — central banks creating money to buy assets like government bonds and mortgage-backed securities, injecting money directly into the economy.

Why didn't this massive money creation cause inflation? Two factors explain the outcome: productive capacity and transmission mechanism.

First, the productive capacity principle. The economy was operating far below potential — unemployment reached 10 percent, factories sat idle, office buildings emptied. The money created didn't chase limited goods; it replaced destroyed purchasing power and mobilized unused resources. Had the Fed created trillions when the economy was at full employment, inflation would have resulted. Real resources constrained the economy, not money.

Second, the transmission mechanism. QE increased central bank reserves dramatically, but reserves are not the same as bank credit to the real economy. When the Fed purchased bonds from financial institutions, it credited their reserve accounts — increasing the monetary base. But these reserves largely remained within the financial system rather than becoming loans to businesses and households for productive purposes. What matters for inflation is bank credit creation into the real economy — loans that finance actual spending on goods and services. This explains why QE inflated asset prices (stocks, bonds, real estate) without inflating consumer prices. Investors who sold bonds to the Fed reinvested in other financial assets, driving those prices up; but this money circulated primarily within financial markets rather than creating new spending on consumer goods. Asset inflation and consumer inflation respond to different mechanisms — a distinction obscured by treating all "money creation" as equivalent.

Between 2008 and 2014, the Fed's balance sheet expanded from $900 billion to $4.5 trillion — a five-fold increase representing growth from 6.2 percent of US GDP in 2007 to 25.9 percent of GDP by 2014. The European Central Bank, Bank of England, and Bank of Japan followed similar paths. Together, major central banks created roughly $10 trillion in new money — an amount that would have been physically impossible under a gold standard and politically unthinkable under Bretton Woods.

Critics predicted disaster. Creating money on such a scale, they argued, would trigger hyperinflation, destroy the dollar's value, and collapse the international monetary system. None of this happened. Inflation remained low — in some countries, dangerously low, threatening deflation. The dollar strengthened against most currencies. The system absorbed trillions in new money without breaking.

It is worth saying clearly that the critics were not crazy. The Austrian and monetarist warnings about fiat money rested on a real concern: a currency that depends entirely on institutional discipline can be ruined when that discipline fails. Hayek, Friedman, and their successors had watched governments debase currencies for political ends across two centuries, and the historical record gave them grounds to expect the same again. Their error was not in identifying the risk but in misreading the constraint. They assumed money creation must produce inflation; the evidence shows it produces inflation only when it exceeds the economy's capacity to absorb it. Their warnings remain useful as a reminder of what fiat money requires of its institutions — not as a refutation of fiat itself.

The Friedman-Schwartz claim that the 1929–33 contraction was made worse by Federal Reserve passivity is the operating premise of every modern central bank, and it was the explicit doctrine Bernanke applied in 2008. In 2002, then-Fed Governor Ben Bernanke offered an extraordinary apology at a conference honoring Milton Friedman's 90th birthday: "Regarding the Great Depression. You're right, we did it. We're very sorry. But thanks to you, we won't do it again." Six years later, Bernanke chaired a Federal Reserve that did not commit the 1931 error. The institutional minimum the monetarist tradition had insisted on for half a century — keep the banking system from imploding when liquidity collapses, anchor expectations, accept that the rest is judgment — held in 2008. What the framework defended here adds to that minimum is the observation the 2008–2014 record then settled in its direction: that aggressive central-bank action against abundant capacity slack produces neither the hyperinflation Hayek had warned of nor the velocity-stable inflation Friedman's later monetarism would have predicted. The relationship between money supply growth and the price level is contingent on whether productive capacity can absorb the money created. In 2008–2014 it could.

#### How federal spending actually works

The household-budget metaphor that dominates American political discourse — that the government must collect taxes before it can spend, that it must "find the money" the way a family finds the money for groceries — does not describe the operational reality of how the United States Treasury actually pays bills in the modern era. The mechanics are public, documented in Federal Reserve daily releases, and accessible to anyone who reads them. They are also, on the operational record, the inverse of what the metaphor implies.

The Treasury maintains a single primary checking account at the Federal Reserve, the Treasury General Account, or TGA. This is the account from which virtually all U.S. government disbursements are made, in the Federal Reserve's own description of its operational role. When Congress authorizes a payment — disability benefits, defense procurement, federal salaries, Social Security checks, interest on the national debt — Treasury executes by issuing an instruction to the Federal Reserve. The Fed performs two simultaneous accounting operations: it debits the TGA by the amount of the payment, and it credits the reserve account of the commercial bank where the payment recipient holds an account. The recipient's bank, in the same sequence, credits the recipient's deposit account.

The point worth holding on is what the second operation actually is. The credit to the commercial bank's reserve account is the creation of new central-bank money — high-powered money in technical vocabulary, sometimes called base money. It does not come from anywhere prior. The Federal Reserve does not retrieve it from a vault. It does not borrow it from a foreign creditor. It does not wait for a tax receipt to clear before executing the credit. The reserves the Fed credits to the commercial bank exist after the credit and did not exist before. The Treasury has, in operational terms, created money by spending it.

Taxation works in the opposite direction. When a taxpayer pays the IRS by check, the bank on which the check is drawn loses reserves: the Fed debits that bank's reserve account and credits the TGA. The reserves the Treasury receives through tax collection are reserves the banking system loses. They do not flow through to fund any subsequent spending. Stephanie Bell's 1998 Levy Institute working paper, *Can Taxes and Bonds Finance Government Spending?*, walks through the accounting at length and reaches the operational conclusion the household-budget metaphor cannot accommodate: "Modern governments…finance all of their spending through the direct creation of new (high-powered) money." A footnote earlier in the same paper makes the chronological point in unmistakable terms: "government spending must originally have preceded taxation. That is, the payment of taxes could not increase the Treasury's account at the Fed…reducing bank reserves, until the reserves had been created."

Bond issuance does the same kind of work in a different operational mode. When the Treasury issues new debt and sells it to private buyers, the buyers' banks lose reserves: those reserves flow into the TGA. When the Federal Reserve subsequently buys those Treasuries from the secondary market — the standard quantitative-easing operation, repeated at vast scale across 2008–2014 and 2020–2022 — the reserves are recreated and credited back to the banking system. The combined operation of bond issuance and central-bank purchase nets, in reserve terms, to roughly zero, with the Treasury obligated to pay interest to the bondholders in the interim. Bond sales, on the operational record, function as a reserve-draining instrument that allows the central bank to maintain a positive target federal funds rate. They do not, in any technical sense, finance the spending that has already occurred or has yet to occur.

A practical question follows. If taxes and bonds do not finance spending, why does the Treasury bother to collect taxes or issue debt at all? The operational answer, which Chapter 5 reached two centuries earlier in the vocabulary of the Bank of England's founding, is that taxation and bond issuance are not financing operations. They are reserve-management operations and demand-creation operations. Taxation creates a population that needs the issuer's money to discharge a state-imposed obligation — which is why Bank of England notes circulated in 1799, why Mesopotamian temple credits had value, and why the modern dollar holds purchasing power. Every American taxpayer needs dollars on April 15. Bond issuance manages the level of bank reserves that the Treasury's spending has already injected into the financial system, so that the Federal Reserve can hit its target overnight interest rate. Both operations are real. Neither is a financing operation in the sense the household-budget metaphor implies.

The operational record sets a tight bound on the political-discourse claim that the United States might one day run out of money. The United States cannot run out of money it issues, in the sense that claim implies, because the Treasury does not need to acquire dollars before spending them. It creates them by spending. The government can run into real binding constraints — a productive economy that cannot absorb additional nominal demand without prices rising, a foreign-creditor system that loses confidence in the currency, a political system that cannot deliver the institutional arrangements a credible fiat regime requires. The chapters of this book have traced what each of those constraints looks like under historical stress. The constraint that public discourse most commonly invokes — that the government must collect before it can spend — is not on the list. It cannot be, because the operational record contradicts it.

The Bank of England in 1694 worked the same way, in a vocabulary that fit its century. The Bank issued notes when it lent to the Crown, the Crown spent the notes into circulation, and the notes returned to the Crown through tax receipts that retired them — the spend-tax-destroy cycle Chapter 5 traced. The technology has changed across three hundred and thirty years. The principle has not.

#### The human cost

The 2008 collapse hit two populations at once. The institutional response was directed first at Wall Street because Wall Street was where the wires were on fire — but the people who paid the actual cost of the crisis were the same in both places. They had trusted institutions that turned out not to be trustworthy, and they had no way of knowing this in advance.

On Wall Street the cleanest record is in the wreckage of Lehman Brothers' 401(k) and pension plans. As of December 31, 2007, employee retirement accounts held $228.7 million in Lehman stock — 10.6 percent of the plan's total assets. The stock closed that day at $65.44 a share. Nine months later it was worth nothing. Twenty-six thousand five hundred Lehman employees and retirees were beneficiaries of pension and savings plans that had been considered, before September 2008, among the more conservative on the street.

Charles Kwalwasser was an intellectual-property attorney who had joined Lehman's legal department in 2004. He held roughly $200,000 of his savings in the company's stock, which a man with his experience and a four-year tenure at a hundred-and-fifty-eight-year-old investment bank had no particular reason to think was risky. "Lehman was huge," he told an interviewer years later. "I don't think anyone thought the company was going to go bankrupt." On Friday, September 12, 2008, he came home for the weekend like everyone else. On Monday morning he no longer had an employer, and the largest single asset on his personal balance sheet had moved to zero in roughly forty-eight hours. He eventually rebuilt — he is now general counsel of a small consumer-products company — but the rebuilding took years and the savings did not come back.

Renee Spero was an assistant vice president in financial analysis, ten weeks pregnant on the Saturday it became clear that Lehman would not be rescued. She watched the news from her apartment. The institutional collapse arrived for her as a question she did not know the answer to: "I didn't know; do I go somewhere on Monday?" She had met her husband at Lehman; that was a complete sentence about how thoroughly her professional and personal lives were entangled with an organization that had ceased to exist between Friday's close and Monday's open. She moved briefly to Barclays during the wind-down, then was unemployed.

Maureen McKenzie, a bookkeeper for the Chamber of Commerce in Kirkwood, Missouri, bought her modest ranch home from her father in March 2004 with a conventional 30-year mortgage at about $900 per month. Two years later, in September 2005, she refinanced into a 40-year payment-option ARM for $184,500. The loan had a teaser rate of 2.25 percent — an initial payment of only $758 per month. Within days the rate reset. By August 2007 her loan balance had hit its cap at $202,950, terminating the option payment; her required monthly payment doubled to $1,611. McKenzie, now on disability for fibromyalgia, could not possibly afford it. She lost her home to foreclosure in May 2008. By late 2008 she had moved into her second apartment after foreclosure, her credit destroyed, the wealth she had worked decades to accumulate gone. She was one of approximately 7.8 to 10 million American families who lost homes to foreclosure between 2007 and 2014 — roughly 10.4 percent of all homeowners with mortgages.

The institutional Wall Street story of September 2008 is the story those people lived through. The Federal Reserve's $30 billion to facilitate the JPMorgan acquisition of Bear Stearns in March, the AIG rescue in September, the trillion-dollar TARP authorization in October — every one of those interventions was justified, in part, on behalf of the people whose retirements had been built on these institutions. The people who actually lost the retirements found, in the months and years that followed, that "the system was saved" was true at the level of the system and untrue at the level of their own savings. That gap between what was rescued and what was not was the politics of every monetary debate of the next decade. The Tea Party emerged from it. Occupy emerged from it. Both were responses, in different vocabularies, to the same underlying perception: that the institutions had been preserved and the people who depended on them had not.

The 2008 response also revealed something the 1797 Bank Restriction's defenders had not had to confront in their own lifetimes: that fiat money's flexibility serves those who design the transmission mechanism. Quantitative easing inflated asset prices — the S&P 500 rose 186 percent between 2009 and 2015; housing prices were up 50 percent by 2019 — while median wages stagnated. The wealthiest 10 percent of households owned 84 percent of stocks; the bottom 50 percent owned essentially nothing. COVID-19's direct fiscal transfers ($1,200 payments, enhanced unemployment) produced a more equitable recovery than 2008's asset-purchase-only approach, demonstrating the same point in the other direction: the architecture of money creation determines who benefits, and that architecture is a political choice.

#### Japan as the chronic counterpart

What 2008 demonstrated about money creation against capacity slack, Japan had been demonstrating chronically since the 1990s. Japan's three-decade experience provides a longer test of the same proposition under different conditions. Since 1990, Japanese government debt has exceeded 260 percent of GDP. The Bank of Japan holds over half of outstanding government bonds. From March 2001 to March 2006 the BOJ ran the world's first quantitative easing program, expanding current-account balances from ¥5 trillion to ¥30–35 trillion — 6 to 7 percent of GDP. It later implemented negative interest rates, unlimited quantitative easing, and direct equity purchases. Critics predicted hyperinflation and currency collapse. Instead, Japan faced persistent deflation: inflation averaged negative 0.3 percent from 1995 to 2013 despite this unprecedented money creation.

But the numbers mask the human devastation. The monetary expansion occurred during Japan's "employment ice age" (1993–2004), when companies froze hiring and an entire generation fell through the cracks.

Michinao Kono, forty-five years old, embodies this catastrophe. In summer 2019, he applied for one of three clerical positions created by the city of Takarazuka specifically for employment-ice-age generation workers. He was one of 1,815 applicants — a competition ratio of 605 to 1. He did not get the job.

Kono had attended Kyoto University — Japan's second-oldest and among its most selective institutions — for eight years without graduating. He frequently skipped classes, failed to accumulate sufficient credits. During his third and fourth years his mailbox "overflowed with recruitment brochures"; even during the 1990s economic malaise, Kyoto University students remained in high demand. But Kono never participated in *shushoku katsudo*, the highly choreographed Japanese recruitment ritual where university students don black suits for marathon group interviews. When asked why he never sought work, his answer was devastating: "It was in the employment ice age. I thought even if I tried, it would be in vain."

Days became weeks became months became years. Kono retreated into *hikikomori* — social withdrawal — living in his parents' home for decades. He attended concerts by all-girl pop group Morning Musume, booked cheap flights to East and Southeast Asia, accumulated approximately 3 million yen (~$28,400) in credit-card debt before defaulting. His father, a former employee of one of Japan's legendary trading houses, had earned enough for a car and home with a front yard — markers of middle-class prosperity. Now elderly, his father could no longer drive, his mother suffered from curvature of the spine, and the family lived off the father's pension.

In November 2019, Kono took a job as a dishwasher at a ramen restaurant — approximately 150,000 yen per month (~$1,380), just slightly above minimum wage. He quit in early January 2020. "It wiped me out physically." By July 2019 he had begun leading an unpaid self-help group for shut-ins, monthly meetings addressing what Japan called the *8050 Problem*: adult children in their forties and fifties dependent on parents in their seventies and eighties. Government surveys counted 613,000 hikikomori aged 40–64 in 2019 alone. The total across all age groups exceeded 1.46 million by 2022, though leading researcher Professor Tamaki Saito argues the real figure is likely twice that.

Why did massive monetary expansion fail to create jobs for Kono and 613,000 others? Because Japan maintained robust productive capacity but suffered from demand deficiency, not supply constraints. The BOJ's quantitative easing increased bank reserves dramatically, but reserves don't automatically create bank credit to the real economy. What matters for employment is whether businesses have confidence to invest and hire, whether consumers have income to spend. The money circulated through financial markets, depressing long-term government bond yields but failing to translate into robust credit creation for businesses or jobs for workers. The economy had room to absorb money creation without inflation — so much room that Japan faced deflation despite creating money. But having "room" for monetary expansion doesn't mean the expansion will fill the room. Money creation alone is insufficient; you need transmission mechanisms that create jobs and incomes, not just reserves.

Eighteen years of deflation hadn't just lowered prices. It had broken the social contract, teaching young Japanese like Kono that effort was futile. Kono spent two decades in his parents' home waiting for an economy that never arrived.

#### What Case 1 settles

Two cases, one finding. The 2008 response under acute stress and Japan's chronic experiment under structural deflation both ran the same test: does fiat money creation against abundant productive capacity produce CPI inflation? On the available evidence the answer is no. The Friedman-Schwartz lesson — central banks must prevent depression-scale collapses by acting aggressively — was applied successfully in 2008 and stood as the institutional minimum. The framework's additional claim — that capacity slack determines whether money creation produces inflation — held under both acute and chronic conditions. What neither case tested is what happens when capacity binds. The next case is that test.

### Case 2 — 2021: money creation meeting real capacity limits

By December 2021 the conditions of 2008 had reversed. Unemployment had fallen to 3.9 percent — near full employment. The output gap had closed to just −0.5 percent of potential GDP, turning positive in 2022. The economy was at or near aggregate capacity when the third round of fiscal and monetary stimulus hit. Specific sectors — semiconductors, container shipping, energy — were already operating at hard constraints. The Fed's balance sheet, which had taken six years to expand from $900 billion to $4.5 trillion across 2008–2014, expanded from $4.2 trillion to $9.0 trillion in roughly two years across 2020–2022. The American Rescue Plan added $1.9 trillion in March 2021 to the prior December 2020 package's $900 billion. By June 2022, headline U.S. CPI reached 9.1 percent — the highest reading since 1981.

Same mechanism. Same scale. Opposite result. The framework defended in this book predicts exactly this asymmetry. Inflation is not a mechanical function of the volume of money created; it is a function of whether that money runs into productive capacity that can absorb it. When capacity is abundant, money creation fills unused capacity and prices remain quiet. When capacity binds — at the macro level or in specific sectors that determine the prices households pay — money creation translates disproportionately into prices. What the 2021–22 episode tested in real time was not whether fiat money inevitably fails when capacity binds; it tested whether the framework's reading of binding-capacity inflation could hold against the orthodox steelmans the post-1971 record had not yet had occasion to confront under fiat conditions.

The reading the productive-capacity framework carries into the episode is direct: 2021–22 was what binding capacity looks like in real time, and the binding was not what the standard output-gap arithmetic assumed. COVID had not just shifted demand toward goods and away from services. It had actively reduced and reorganized the productive base. Three percentage points of the U.S. labor force exited between February and April 2020; participation was still about a percentage point below trend through late 2021. Permanent business closures across hospitality, retail, and live entertainment ran into the hundreds of thousands. The supply-chain network on which pre-pandemic global manufacturing had relied was disassembled in spring 2020 and only partially rebuilt. Into this reduced and reorganized capacity, fiscal and monetary stimulus arrived at a scale the productive base could not fully absorb without prices doing the adjusting.

#### Four supply disruptions

Four supply-side shocks defined the inflationary mechanics of the period. Semiconductor shortages devastated automotive production; 11.3 million vehicles were removed from global production schedules in 2021, with $110 billion in lost output by May. Lead times for semiconductor orders extended from three to four months pre-pandemic to over twelve months. Port congestion strangled supply chains; on September 19, 2021, a record 73 container ships waited offshore at the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. Container transit times from China to the U.S. increased 82 percent between July 2020 and December 2021. The Russian invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022 sent Brent crude above $100 a barrel for the first time since 2014; diesel prices surged 109 percent year-over-year by June 2022. And consumer spending patterns had shifted dramatically during the pandemic — services down 20 percent between February and April 2020, goods up across categories — leaving households with $2.1 to $2.5 trillion in excess savings by mid-2021. When that pent-up demand hit supply-constrained sectors, inflation followed the bottlenecks rather than the money supply.

#### Two figures

What binding-capacity inflation meant at household and small-business resolution shows up most clearly in two figures the 2021–22 reporting captured.

Ty Stehlik works the front desk at a Milwaukee hotel. Through 2020 and into 2021 he pressed his employer for a raise and finally got one in fall 2021 — an extra dollar an hour, bringing his wage to $15. By the standards of the post-2008 wage record it was a real raise, roughly 7 percent in nominal terms, larger than the average annual increase American workers had seen across the 2010s. By the standards of the world Stehlik actually had to live in, it disappeared the day it was granted. The annual CPI release for November 2021 showed prices rising at 6.8 percent year-over-year, the fastest pace since 1982; the December release the following month put the figure at 7 percent. Rent in his Milwaukee neighborhood had risen by an even larger margin. Food prices had risen sharply on items he bought routinely. A roommate with medical debt was sharing the apartment to make rent feasible. By January 2022, Stehlik told a *Washington Post* reporter, "That raise meant nothing." He still relied on family support to cover rent and groceries; coworkers were juggling two or three jobs and reaching the same conclusion. The wage gain was real in nominal terms; the wage gain was nothing in real terms; both statements described the same household at the same moment.

John Nephew runs Atlas Games, a small board-game and card-game publisher in Duluth, Minnesota. His business model relies on inventory cycles ordered out of Chinese manufacturing months ahead of the retail seasons that turn the orders into revenue. In December 2020 he ordered a 40-foot shipping container of games from a manufacturer in Shanghai, expecting delivery in roughly six weeks — the standard just-in-time cadence Atlas had operated on for years. The shipment took nearly six months to arrive. Across that span, the Port of Los Angeles recorded its busiest month in history. Vessels waited days to dock; containers waited additional days to be loaded onto trains; trucking and warehousing capacity downstream had been broken by the same pandemic that had broken the rest of the supply chain. The freight bill for Nephew's container, when it finally arrived, was about $12,000 — at least 50 percent above what Atlas Games had budgeted at order placement. The inventory shortfall, the freight cost overrun, and the unpredictable lead times that followed left a small operator with the choice every American small business in tradeable goods faced through 2021: absorb the cost overruns and watch margins collapse, or pass them to customers as price increases. Most chose some mix. The aggregate inflation reading of the period was, in part, the sum of those decisions.

What Stehlik's wage and Nephew's container show is what the macro decompositions show in summary form: the inflation of 2021–22 was not a clean test of any single framework. Demand was elevated. Supply was disrupted. Capacity was actively shrinking under pandemic constraints. Each story was a different angle on the same episode, and the institutional response had to address all three at once.

#### The orthodox steelmans tested

Two orthodox steelmans cut into the 2021–22 episode and the framework owes each an answer.

The first is Buchanan-Wagner. *Democracy in Deficit*, published in 1977, predicted that democratic governments freed from gold's automatic discipline would tilt structurally toward stimulus, particularly in election years, and tolerate more inflation than the long-run optimum required because voters tend to misperceive deficit costs and politicians' time horizons are short. The 2021 American Rescue Plan was a politically chosen stimulus package, sized partly by political calendar — a new administration in its first months, a Senate margin requiring rapid passage — and partly by economic calculation. Olivier Blanchard's February 2021 PIIE post argued that the package's $2.8 trillion of demand against a generously measured $900 billion output gap was "nearly three times" the gap. Lawrence Summers wrote in the *Washington Post* that the package risked "inflationary pressures of a kind we have not seen in a generation." Both calls were directionally right.

The framework's reply is not that they were wrong but that the magnitude they got right was layered on top of a supply-side mechanism the orthodox demand-overshoot reading underweights — that a politically sized package collided with capacity that pandemic shocks had actively destroyed, and the inflationary outcome was the joint product. The IMF's October 2022 cross-country sectoral PCE decomposition, the New York Fed's structural multi-sector model, the San Francisco Fed's Shapiro decomposition, and the New York Fed's quantification of the fiscal-stimulus contribution under supply constraints all converge on roughly the same picture: aggregate demand pressure accounts for the larger share of the U.S. inflation overshoot, on the order of 60 percent in the New York Fed model, with sectoral supply doing the remainder; the fiscal package's direct contribution to total overshoot lands on the order of a third against a counterfactual without the package. That is closer to the orthodox demand-overshoot reading than to either the transitory consensus or the supply-only framings of early 2021. It is the part of the record that vindicates Blanchard's and Summers's directional call.

What the framework rejects is the *general* form of Buchanan-Wagner's worry — that democratic regimes will run too loose on average and that this is the dominant failure mode of advanced-economy fiat. The post-1971 record across advanced economies has been at least as often a record of running too tight as of running too loose. Japan's three-decade deflationary undershoot. The eurozone's prolonged sub-target inflation through the 2010s. The slow U.S. recovery from 2009 to 2014 against a Federal Reserve repeatedly worrying about inflation that did not arrive. The 2021–22 episode is the clearest case the period offers of the opposite failure mode — a politically sized package producing significant inflation under fiat conditions — and even there the political-economy story is supply-meets-stimulus, not stimulus alone. Buchanan-Wagner's worry about the political sizing of the 2021 package was empirically vindicated. Their worry's general form, that democratic systems systematically over-issue, was not what the half-century since 1977 has actually shown.

The second steelman is Sargent-Wallace and the fiscal-dominance argument. The 1981 paper "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic" showed that under conditions where fiscal policy is set independently of monetary policy and is structurally deficit-biased, monetary restraint cannot anchor the price level on its own. The framework defended in this book accepts the operational claim. What it rejects is the vocabulary in which the orthodox tradition has expressed it.

The standard restatement, picked up in John Cochrane's contemporary fiscal theory, holds that "the price level adjusts so that the real value of nominal debt is equal to the present value of primary surpluses." The framing imports a solvency metaphysics the chartalist tradition specifically rejects. A sovereign currency issuer does not have a solvency constraint in its own currency in the sense the household-budget metaphor implies — its obligations are denominated in a unit it controls. What the issuer can lose is *credibility* — the institutional trust that the unit will continue to hold value, that the tax system will continue to drain the spending the issuer has injected, that the political system will continue to produce the policy responses inflation episodes eventually require. Sargent-Wallace, read in the chartalist vocabulary, is not a constraint on solvency but a constraint on credibility: when the institutional architecture cannot deliver the inflation-anchoring response that elevated capacity utilization eventually demands, the price level itself becomes the variable through which the system adjusts.

The historical record contains episodes that look like fiscal dominance in this sense. The United Kingdom's postwar policy of holding gilt yields below the inflation rate while running large deficits — the financial repression of 1945–1951 — produced an effective inflation tax that resolved much of the wartime debt overhang at savers' expense. Brazil's 1980s and early-1990s indexation regime, in which the price level rose roughly with the velocity of fiscal-monetary accommodation, was the textbook case of monetary policy chasing fiscal facts. Turkey's 2021–2024 episode, in which a politicized central bank pursued unorthodox cuts while deficits ran wide, produced annual inflation above 70 percent and a currency that lost three-quarters of its dollar value in three years. Each of these looks like Sargent-Wallace in concrete form: a state issuing currency without the credible institutional architecture to back the debt path it was running, and a price level that adjusted accordingly.

The 2021–22 U.S. inflation, by contrast, was met by a Federal Reserve that did anchor — late, with consequences, but successfully. Inflation peaked at 9.1 percent in June 2022 and fell back to roughly 3 percent by 2024. The institutional response Sargent and Wallace described as needing to exist was the response that arrived. The fiscal-dominance worry was real, and the U.S. institutional architecture cleared the bar.

#### What Case 2 settles

The 2021–22 episode tested whether the institutional architecture managing fiat money could absorb a binding-capacity shock without running into the indexation regimes the post-1971 record has shown what unanchored inflation produces. The architecture absorbed it. The cost — Stehlik's nominal raise erased, Nephew's freight overruns, the broader squeeze on every household whose income did not rise as fast as its grocery and rent bills — was real, distributional, and politically observable. Inflation cost the Biden administration the 2024 election in a manner Carter had lost in 1980. Democratic accountability for inflation, which Buchanan-Wagner's framework had not expected to operate, did. The framework's reading: capacity is the binding constraint, the constraint is not the fixed concert hall the standard output-gap arithmetic assumed, and the institutions managing fiat under capacity stress must be credibility-bearing enough to anchor expectations when the test arrives. The 2021–22 record shows the architecture passed. It also shows that *passing* the test is not the same as *acing* it, and that the framework's reading of capacity has to be more dynamic than the static output-gap arithmetic the orthodox tradition had been operating with.

### Case 3 — Volcker: the human cost of restoring credibility

The 1970s inflation crisis is often cited as proof that fiat money inevitably fails. The real story reveals what happens when supply shocks reduce productive capacity beyond monetary policy's control — and what kind of institutional response such a moment can demand.

OPEC's oil embargo of October 1973, followed by the 1973 and 1979 price increases, did not just raise prices. It reduced the economy's productive capacity. Oil rose from three dollars a barrel in 1972 to twelve in 1974 and thirty-nine by 1980 — a thirteen-fold increase in eight years. This was not too much money chasing too few goods. It was fewer goods available for any amount of money. Less energy meant factories could not run at full capacity, trucks could not deliver goods, and the economy could literally produce less, regardless of monetary policy.

Central banks faced what was, in effect, a binary decision. They could accommodate the shock and let prices rise to maintain employment, accepting the resulting inflation. Or they could suppress demand by raising rates, forcing unemployment to reduce inflation through recession. Neither option addressed the root problem of insufficient energy production. Monetary policy could only determine who bore the cost of the shortage — consumers through inflation, or workers through unemployment. This was structurally distinct from the 1960s episode in which excess money creation at full capacity had bid up prices and tightening could solve the problem; in the 1970s, reduced productive capacity caused inflation regardless of money supply.

Arthur Burns (Fed Chair 1970–1978) correctly diagnosed "cost-push" inflation and recognized that monetary policy could not create real resources. Better tools existed in principle — Strategic Petroleum Reserve, coordinated energy policy, temporary incomes policy. Whether any was politically achievable when wage-price expectations were already spiraling, and Carter's voluntary guidelines had failed, is the harder question. Instead the U.S. chose Volcker's recession.

#### Volcker

Paul Volcker's appointment as Fed Chair in August 1979 marked a turning point. The man who had drafted the contingency plans at Treasury in 1971 and watched Connally overrule him at Camp David was now the man Carter had asked to defend the dollar that the Camp David weekend had set adrift. Volcker himself was an austere figure — six feet seven inches tall, prone to a cigar, anti-romantic in temperament, and preoccupied throughout his career with credibility, expectations, and the institutional discipline he believed central banking required. The voice that runs through his memoir and his FOMC remarks is institutional rather than ideological: he was not arguing that gold should return or that fiat had failed, but that what he repeatedly called "inflationary psychology" had become entrenched, and that the longer the regime lasted the worse the eventual reckoning would be. He understood, too, that aggressive U.S. tightening would amplify debt vulnerability in major borrowing countries — Argentina, Brazil, Mexico — and proceeded anyway.

The Fed funds rate reached 20 percent in June 1981 — the highest in U.S. history. Unemployment peaked at 10.8 percent in November 1982 — the highest since the Great Depression. The recession was devastating: GDP contracted 2.9 percent in the year ending Q4 1982. But these aggregate numbers obscured the concentrated devastation in specific industries and regions. Youngstown, Ohio, lost forty thousand steelworkers between 1977 and 1983 as the Campbell Works, U.S. Steel, and Republic Steel all closed; with twenty-percent interest rates, laid-off workers could neither buy nor refinance nor sell. Farmland values fell sixty percent between 1981 and 1986; Iowa farm foreclosures doubled, and rural Main Streets emptied across the Midwest. Housing starts fell forty-five percent — from 2.0 million to 1.1 million units — and construction lost eight hundred thousand jobs. Michigan's unemployment hit 16.9 percent; Flint's exceeded twenty. The auto industry lost three hundred thousand jobs. Detroit, Cleveland, and Pittsburgh entered long-term decline from which many neighborhoods never recovered. The Rust Belt was born.

#### The Hagedorn farm

Dean and Kaye Hagedorn's hundred-year-old family farm near Spencer, Iowa, exemplified the human cost. The farm had passed through three generations: 560 acres of corn and beans on flat black-soil prairie, the kind of land Iowa farms had been built on since homesteading. Dean's father had paid down the original mortgage during the New Deal years; Dean himself had taken over in the late 1960s and farmed it through the boom decade that followed. The 1970s commodity boom — driven partly by the 1972 Soviet wheat purchase, partly by inflation that pushed food prices up faster than nominal interest rates — looked like a permanent shift to Iowa farmers and their bankers alike. Dean borrowed to expand. He bought additional acreage, upgraded the equipment, took out a Production Credit Association line of credit at the rates of the time. His banker called it good farming. His debt was within what the cash flow could service when interest rates were six or seven percent and corn was four dollars a bushel.

Volcker's tightening killed both halves of that math at once. By 1981 the prime rate was above twenty percent and the PCA rate had moved with it; by 1982 corn had fallen below three dollars and Iowa farmland values had begun the slide that would take them down sixty percent before it ended. Dean's September 1982 diary entry, written on lined notebook paper that survives in the family's records, says it as plainly as anyone has said it: "I've never been so terrified in my life. We owe over $18,000 just on interest for PCA for one year! What do you do?" He did what farmers in his position did: he borrowed against next year's crop, refinanced where he could, sold off equipment to pay the interest, watched his banker grow gradually less friendly through 1983 and 1984. Kaye took a job in town. The children — there were three — understood that something was wrong without being told what.

The end came in the fall of 1985. The corn harvest that year was a hundred and forty-six thousand bushels, a strong year by historical standards, worth ninety-four thousand dollars on the local elevator. The bank took the entire check. Against the accumulated debt — more than three hundred thousand dollars after four years of compound interest on rolled-over notes — it barely covered a year's interest. The Hagedorns filed Chapter 12 bankruptcy in early 1986 and lost the farm at auction that summer. The buyer was a corporate operation out of Des Moines that had done its arithmetic on land prices that had now fallen to a level Dean's grandfather had paid in the 1920s. The Hagedorn name came off the mailbox. The diary stopped soon after.

They had done nothing wrong. The PCA loan had been prudent at the rates it was contracted at. The expansion had been responsible against the cash flows of the time. What had changed was none of those things. What had changed was the federal funds rate, set in Washington by a Federal Reserve that had concluded — facing what it took to be a worse alternative — that the distributional cost of breaking the wage-price dynamic was the lesser evil. The cost turned out to be the Hagedorn farm, along with roughly two hundred thousand others between 1981 and 1986. Whether the Fed was right about the alternative is the question the next section takes up.

#### The Argentina counterfactual

The strongest defense of Volcker's choice is that the alternative was worse — not in some abstract sense, but in a specific sense visible on the historical record of what entrenched inflation does to a country. Bernanke's 2005 essay on very high inflation in Latin America, written from inside the Federal Reserve System and rooted in the same intellectual tradition that informed Volcker's 1979 decision, gives the analytic statement: when inflation expectations de-anchor and an indexation regime takes hold, monetary policy loses its grip on the price level, the cost of any later disinflation rises, and the political feasibility of disinflation falls. The longer the regime persists, the harder it is to break, and the larger the eventual real economic cost.

The cleanest cases were South American. Argentina between 1975 and 1990 ran inflation that averaged roughly 300 percent annually and reached 5,000 percent in 1989. Over those fifteen years Argentine real per-capita GDP fell about 20 percent. Real wages fell roughly 40 percent. Saving in pesos became impossible — anyone who could moved their wealth into dollars or hard assets, and a domestic financial sector capable of funding long-term productive investment effectively ceased to exist. Poverty in greater Buenos Aires rose from 5 percent of households in 1975 to 38 percent by 1989. Five separate stabilization programs failed before the 1991 currency board pegged the peso to the dollar — a program that itself collapsed a decade later in the 2001 default that wiped out savings for the second time in a generation.

Brazil's experience between 1980 and 1994 was structurally similar, although the indexation regime that took hold made daily life functional in a way Argentina's did not. Real wages and pensions were re-indexed to the price level monthly or weekly; price stickers in São Paulo supermarkets were replaced every morning. Ordinary Brazilian families experienced a kind of running treadmill — nominal incomes rose constantly, purchasing power held only because everything around the household was being repriced at the same rate. The cost was less visible than Argentina's poverty figures but no less real: a domestic financial sector that could not fund anything beyond short-term contracts; an economy organized around the politics of indexation rather than productive investment; a working-class household that could not save toward retirement, education, or a home in any conventional sense. The Plano Real of 1994 finally broke the inflation; it required a new currency, a substantially reformed central bank, and political conditions that took fifteen years to assemble.

These were the regimes Volcker thought he was facing in 1979. American inflation was 13.5 percent that year and rising. Wage indexation had spread through major union contracts. Inflation expectations measured in survey data showed double-digit inflation as the median forecast for the next decade. Carter's voluntary wage-price guidelines had collapsed; the energy reforms that might have addressed the supply side had failed politically; Burns at the Fed had been unable or unwilling to apply sufficient monetary restraint. The window for stabilization was visibly closing. Bernanke's later analytic point — that the longer the delay, the larger the eventual cost — was the operating logic of Volcker's 1979 decision in a non-academic vocabulary. He believed, watching Argentina and Brazil and the early indexation behaviors emerging in American wage bargaining, that another five years of accommodating stagflation would carry the United States across the line where disinflation became politically impossible.

The cost the alternative would have imposed, had Volcker accommodated for another five years, is harder to specify than the cost of his recession because the counterfactual was not run. But the rough order is visible in the South American cases. A doubled poverty rate. A 20 percent fall in real per-capita GDP. The destruction of any saving instrument denominated in the domestic currency. A working-class retiree on a fixed Social Security or private pension watching her purchasing power erode by half over a decade — with no recourse, no political weight to demand re-indexation, and no functioning financial market in which to escape into hard assets the way wealthier households could. The American household that lost the most under Volcker's recession was the Hagedorn farm. The American household that would have lost the most under entrenched inflation was the passbook saver, the fixed-pension retiree, the wage-earner without indexation in her contract. The two costs are not the same.

The aggregate U.S. evidence from the 1970s sharpens the point. Real interest paid on passbook savings accounts was negative in every year from 1973 through 1981 — depositors lost real purchasing power even on conservative savings, year after year. Households whose wealth sat in long-duration nominal bonds — a substantial share of middle-class retirement portfolios — watched the real value of those bonds fall by close to half between 1972 and 1981. None of these costs entered the GDP statistics that the Volcker recession would later show up in so clearly; they appeared as reduced real consumption among retirees, deferred retirement among workers without indexed pensions, and the slow erosion of a class of household savings that the political system had no mechanism to compensate.

Volcker's choice was therefore not between cruelty and gentleness. It was between two patterns of cruelty — one acute and concentrated, one chronic and diffuse — under conditions in which both costs were already partly being paid. The political economy of the choice is recognizable: the diffuse cost was politically inert (the retiree and the wage-earner could not organize themselves the way the auto union or the farm bloc could), while the concentrated cost was politically explosive (rural foreclosure crisis, Rust Belt collapse, sustained protests outside the Federal Reserve building). A democratic system facing a choice between distributional patterns of those two shapes will tend, on the historical record, to defer the concentrated cost and accept the diffuse one. Volcker's contribution was to refuse that calculation.

#### What Case 3 settles

This is the *paired vindication* the Preface named and Chapter 8 traced through Attwood. There, Attwood was directionally right that monetary contraction destroys real lives and Peel and Overstone were directionally right that an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master needs institutional architecture to constrain it; the contest was over which worry to architect against. Here the structure repeats. Volcker's Federal Reserve architected against entrenched indexation and accepted what disinflation did to Iowa farms and Rust Belt towns. The household whose savings were preserved by the disinflation and the household whose farm was lost to the prime rate were both vindicated against the alternative that would have ruined them; the institutional choice was about which kind of cruelty the system would accept.

By 1983, inflation had fallen from 13.5 percent to 3.2 percent — not because the oil shortage was solved or productive capacity restored, but because sustained monetary tightening had reset wage-price expectations through real economic contraction. Workers, facing unemployment, stopped demanding wage increases. Businesses, facing bankruptcy, stopped raising prices. The reset took years of lost output, destroyed careers, communities that did not recover. It also produced the foundation for the Great Moderation that followed: a quarter-century in which inflation stayed broadly anchored, monetary policy regained operational credibility, and the political-economic conditions for the post-1990 expansions became possible. The Currency School's worry about discretionary issuance under fiscal pressure was vindicated through what entrenched inflation does when no Volcker arrives — the South American counterfactual. What the Currency School got wrong was the *form* of the answer. The architectural answer the regime needed was not gold rules but discretionary central-bank credibility, which Volcker built. Whether the trade was net favorable depends on which households and which decades the accounting weights — and that, ultimately, is the question this case has tried to keep open rather than close.

### Case 4 — Crypto: the fantasy of escaping institutions

By the 2020s, cryptocurrencies were promising to return money to a commodity basis — not gold but mathematics, with supply limited by algorithm. The argument descends from a serious intellectual tradition. Friedrich Hayek's *Denationalisation of Money* (1976) made the most uncompromising version: "the root and source of all monetary evil is the government's monopoly on money," with gold "an anchor — and any anchor is better than a money left to the discretion of government — but a very wobbly anchor." Hayek's proposal was to remove the state's monopoly and let private institutions issue redeemable currencies in open competition. George Selgin's *Less Than Zero* (1997) extended the tradition with a "productivity norm" — a rule under which the price level varies with goods' unit costs of production, distinguishing benign productivity-driven deflation from malignant money-demand deflation. Lawrence H. White's *The Theory of Monetary Institutions* (1999) developed the institutional foundations. Saifedean Ammous's *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018) brought the argument into the cryptocurrency era, framing Bitcoin's hard-coded 21-million supply cap as the first instance of absolute, credibly enforced monetary scarcity in history — gold's discipline without gold's logistical and political vulnerabilities. This is a serious tradition, not a fringe complaint, and the case it makes against discretionary central banking deserves a serious answer.

The first part of the answer is one this book has already given. The framework defended throughout these chapters is, on the question of whether monetary institutions matter, in substantial agreement with the Hayek-Selgin-White lineage. Money is not a thing but a relationship enforced by institutions; institutions can fail; the failure modes the tradition warns about — chronic inflation, debasement under fiscal pressure, the erosion of saving for political convenience — are real, documented across the historical record assembled here. The book's institutionalism is not a rejection of the sound-money tradition's underlying concern; it is an alternative answer to the same question. The tradition asks how monetary discipline can be enforced when the institutions issuing money have political incentives to debase it. The answer it proposes — algorithmic or metallic constraint — and the answer this book proposes — credible, rule-bound, democratically accountable institutions backed by productive capacity — are different solutions to a problem both traditions agree exists.

The protocol's discipline-from-rules deserves credit on its own terms. Across the same fifteen-year window in which the Federal Reserve's balance sheet expanded from $0.9 trillion to $9 trillion, Bitcoin's pre-programmed halving cadence has produced exactly the issuance the protocol specified, with no central authority capable of altering the rule. As mechanism design, the supply schedule is a real accomplishment, and it answers the discipline-from-rules question Hayek and Selgin posed more credibly than gold ever did — gold's supply was hostage to mining discoveries, central-bank conversions, and political seizures in a way Bitcoin's is not. The cypherpunk tradition has been explicit that the protocol is the achievement and that the failures around it have been failures of the institutions built on top of it, which the tradition warned against from the beginning. The fairer comparison the tradition demands — Bitcoin's monetary-base discipline against the Federal Reserve's balance sheet over the same window — does not flatter the Federal Reserve.

#### Has algorithmic scarcity produced functional money?

Where the book parts company is on the empirical question. The evidence is now substantial. Bitcoin has experienced three drawdowns in the 75–90 percent range since 2013; annualized volatility has averaged 46–70 percent — several times major equity indices, an order of magnitude above gold. A currency whose dollar-equivalent value can fall by three quarters within a year is not, on the historical evidence, useful for the function money is supposed to serve: enabling people to price contracts, plan investments, and discharge obligations across time. Stablecoins — the workaround the industry developed to address the volatility problem — do so by reintroducing the dollar at a remove, every dollar-pegged stablecoin a claim on a bank deposit somewhere in the legacy financial system. They are not alternatives to fiat money. They are derivatives of it, distributed through cryptographic rails. Adoption as currency has remained limited even in El Salvador, the only country to make Bitcoin legal tender; three years in, the U.S. dollar continues to dominate roughly 85–90 percent of Salvadoran transactions, and routine commerce runs on dollars rather than the experiment.

The institutional question the cypherpunks set out to abolish keeps reasserting itself in the failure record around the protocol. Mt. Gox in 2014 lost roughly 850,000 bitcoins to multi-year custodial theft. Terra/Luna's algorithmic stablecoin destroyed forty to sixty billion dollars in May 2022 through a mechanism indistinguishable in essentials from the runs that ended Edward Backwell's career: confidence broke, redemption demand exceeded redemption capacity, the algorithm failed in the same way and for the same reason a goldsmith with insufficient reserves would have failed in 1672. Celsius and Voyager collapsed across mid-2022 with combined customer-fund shortfalls in the billions, revealing that the largest non-bank crypto lenders had been operating as unregulated fractional-reserve institutions throughout. Silvergate and Signature Banks failed in March 2023 to classical bank runs, distinguished from those of 1929–33 only by the speed at which crypto-native depositors could move funds. The protocol works. The institutions built on top of it have reproduced the failure modes the tradition set out to escape.

#### Terri Smith

In November 2022, FTX — the second-largest cryptocurrency exchange in the world, helmed by a thirty-year-old whose face had appeared in the Super Bowl advertisement of the previous February — collapsed in seventy-two hours. A run on customer deposits revealed an $8–9 billion shortfall: customer funds had been routed through an affiliated trading firm, Alameda Research, which had borrowed and lost most of them. The bankruptcy filing on November 11 froze accounts. More than a million customers may have lost money in the collapse.

Terri Smith was one of them. A semi-retired architect in the Seattle area, she had become serious about crypto investing in the summer of 2021, studying the technology, distributing her holdings across several exchanges, settling on FTX for the speed and the interface. At one point she had watched her investments double in roughly five months — the kind of move that, in the 2021 environment, looked less like luck than like participation in a transformative new asset class. She was traveling when the run began. By the time she got back to her account, withdrawals were restricted; by the time the bankruptcy filed, the roughly $30,000 she still held on FTX was trapped inside the estate. She told NPR the experience felt "like theft."

She was not the kind of figure cypherpunk literature had imagined would be the casualty of the institutional failure modes the protocol was supposed to abolish. She was the protocol's success story — the educated semi-retired professional who had come to crypto through the technology's promise, who had distributed her holdings across exchanges precisely to manage institutional risk, who had read enough to know what she was doing and had committed savings she could not easily replace. The diversification did not save her. The institutional question reasserted itself through the very platforms the protocol had been built to make unnecessary, in a vocabulary — *theft*, *betrayal*, *fool* — that the Lehman casualties of 2008 would have recognized in their own terms.

#### What Case 4 settles

Hayek's knowledge-problem critique attacks the underlying epistemic premise of any institutional framework charged with reading the economy from the center. The cryptocurrency tradition proposed to escape that problem entirely — to substitute algorithmic scarcity for institutional judgment, removing the question of who knows what when. The Bitcoin protocol delivers on the algorithmic-scarcity claim with a discipline gold never could. What the empirical record now shows is that the institutional question Hayek's critique pointed at does not vanish when the protocol's discipline is in place; it relocates to the institutions built on top of the protocol — the exchanges, the lenders, the stablecoin issuers, the cross-platform clearing infrastructure — and reasserts itself in worse form than the central-banking institutions the tradition set out to escape.

The deepest agreement this book has with the Hayek-Selgin-White tradition is that institutions matter and can fail. The deepest disagreement is on whether the failure can be engineered around. On the historical record now available, it cannot. Algorithmic discipline, on the available evidence, has not delivered what credible institutional discipline can deliver, and the proposed escape from the institutional question has turned out to be a recreation of it with worse institutions.

### What the four cases have shown

Four cases. Four answers to the same question. The 2008 response and Japan's chronic experiment showed that money creation against abundant productive capacity does not produce CPI inflation; the Friedman-Schwartz monetary-contraction worry was vindicated as a 1929–33 lesson and met as the institutional minimum every modern central bank now keeps. The 2021–22 inflation showed that capacity is a moving target rather than a fixed concert hall, that orthodox demand-overshoot worries can be empirically real even when the framework's broader description is correct, and that the institutional architecture managing fiat under capacity stress can — when its credibility holds — anchor expectations through the test. The 1979–82 disinflation showed what discretionary central-bank credibility can do, and what its acute distributional cost looks like when the alternative being avoided is the chronic distributional cost of entrenched indexation. The cryptocurrency record showed that the institutional question reasserts itself even where algorithmic protocol was supposed to abolish it.

What keeps fiat money from becoming fantasy, the four cases together suggest, is institutional capacity, real-resource constraint, political legitimacy, and the willingness to honor distributional choices rather than displace them onto whichever class is least able to organize against them. None of this is automatic. None of it is guaranteed. The historical record this book has traced shows what kinds of institutional arrangements have produced workable fiat money and what kinds have not. What the institutions of the next half-century should be — between the rules-based tradition descending from public-choice economics and the stabilizer-based tradition descending from the modern Modern Monetary Theory canon — is the live political-economy debate the framework opens up. The Epilogue takes that debate up directly.
