# Chapter 12 Sources & Summary

## **Chapter Summary**

The modern fiat system, established after 1971, has now operated longer than the classical gold standard did. The 2008 crisis showed that central banks could expand reserves substantially without producing the inflation that orthodox monetarists predicted, because the expansion replaced destroyed credit in an economy operating well below capacity. The 2020–22 episode tested the framework under different conditions — a constrained-supply economy meeting unprecedented fiscal stimulus — and produced inflation in the magnitude the productive-capacity framework would predict. The chapter takes both episodes as evidence about what fiat money can and cannot do, where the public-choice and Currency School worries identify real risks, and where the institutional architecture of modern central banking has answered them. The cryptocurrency movement, which proposes algorithmic scarcity as a return to monetary discipline, is engaged on its own terms: the critique it embodies — that discretionary central banks have a documented track record of debasement — has a long pedigree, and the chapter takes seriously why algorithmic scarcity has not, in practice, produced functional money. The age of fiat is best understood not as a triumph but as a continuation of the longer historical pattern in which money is whatever the institutions strong enough to enforce credit relationships can make stick.
