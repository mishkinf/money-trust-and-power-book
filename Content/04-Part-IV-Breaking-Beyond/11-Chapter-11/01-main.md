# PART IV: THE BREAKING AND BEYOND

## Chapter 11: The Breaking of Bretton Woods

### De Gaulle's Challenge

In February 1965, French President Charles de Gaulle stood before television cameras and delivered a challenge that would shake the international monetary system to its foundations. France, he announced, would begin converting dollar reserves into gold—demanding the metal the United States had promised to deliver at $35 per ounce.

This was monetary policy with sharp geopolitical edges. De Gaulle was pressing the structural tension at the heart of Bretton Woods: the world needed dollars for trade and reserves, requiring the United States to run balance-of-payments deficits, while those same persistent deficits steadily undermined the gold backing that anchored the dollar's value at $35 an ounce.

Robert Triffin had named this dilemma in 1959 and 1960, calling it logically untenable in the long run even as the system was working in the short. Six years later, de Gaulle was testing how much pressure the gold-conversion clause could bear in practice. His answer would not end the gold standard immediately, but it would mark the beginning of the unraveling.

Bretton Woods had been born in a world of dollar shortage. Europe and Japan needed dollars to buy food, fuel, machinery, and raw materials from the United States. Dollar outflows through aid, military spending, investment, and imports helped rebuild the world economy. In the late 1940s and early 1950s, that was the system's virtue. The reserve center supplied the liquidity everyone else needed.

But the same mechanism changed character as reconstruction succeeded. The world no longer suffered only from too few dollars. It began to accumulate too many claims on American gold. What had looked like generosity in the age of shortage began to look like privilege in the age of glut: the United States could pay for troops, foreign investment, imports, and geopolitical commitments with liabilities other countries held as reserves. De Gaulle's complaint was therefore not merely French nationalism. It was a challenge to the political economy of the system.

The phrase that would stick was "exorbitant privilege." It captured the resentment precisely: ordinary countries had to earn reserves, but the reserve issuer could create the asset other countries used as reserves. Bretton Woods had made that privilege tolerable by tying it to gold. De Gaulle was asking what remained when the tie became doubtful.

### Britain's 1967 crisis: when the pound broke

On Saturday, November 18, 1967, at 9:30 PM, Chancellor of the Exchequer James Callaghan appeared on television to announce that the pound would be devalued from $2.80 to $2.40—a 14.3 percent reduction. The announcement came on a weekend to minimize market disruption, but the crisis had been building for months.

Britain's problem was structural. The Labour government had inherited an £800 million balance-of-payments deficit when it took office in 1964. Worse, £4.5 billion in overseas sterling balances—debts to Commonwealth countries and former colonies holding pounds as reserves—created permanent vulnerability. Every time confidence wavered, holders could convert sterling to dollars, draining Britain's reserves. The pound was overvalued at $2.80, making exports uncompetitive, but devaluation would anger sterling holders and undermine London's role as a financial center.

Wilson's government tried everything to avoid devaluation. They imposed austerity budgets, raised interest rates, negotiated IMF standby credits, and implemented a six-month wage and price freeze in July 1966. The Bank of England intervened repeatedly to defend the parity, spending reserves to buy pounds whenever the rate weakened. For three years, Wilson insisted devaluation was unthinkable—"the pound in your pocket" would not be affected.

But on September 7, 1967, a seemingly minor dock strike at Liverpool escalated into a nationwide port shutdown. Exports stalled. The trade deficit widened. Speculators sensed weakness and began selling sterling. By early November, reserves were hemorrhaging. The Bank of England lost $1.5 billion in a matter of weeks. The dam broke. On November 16, the Cabinet met in emergency session and voted to devalue.

Callaghan's Saturday evening broadcast was somber: "From now on the pound abroad is worth 14 percent or so less in terms of other currencies." The next day, Prime Minister Wilson addressed the nation, attempting damage control with what became the crisis's most infamous line: "It does not mean, of course, that the pound here in Britain, in your pocket or purse or in your bank, has been devalued." Technically true—domestic prices hadn't changed instantly—but widely mocked as duplicitous. Devaluation would soon drive import prices higher, eroding purchasing power exactly as Wilson denied.

The rescue that followed turned devaluation into an externally supervised austerity program. Callaghan resigned as Chancellor three days later, switching positions with Home Secretary Roy Jenkins. The crisis marked a humiliating retreat from Britain's postwar pretensions to great power status. Sterling, once the world's dominant reserve currency, was now a liability requiring international support.

The devaluation exposed Bretton Woods' central weakness more clearly than any theoretical paper could. Britain had tried to maintain a fixed exchange rate, pursue full employment, and restrict capital flows enough to protect the first two goals. Even that compromise failed. Capital controls delayed the reckoning, but speculators could still sell sterling forward, delay payments in pounds, accelerate payments in other currencies, and use offshore eurodollar markets beyond British regulatory reach. The 1940s machinery was becoming porous in a 1960s financial world.

The rescue showed the distributional logic of fixed parities under stress. The IMF's £1.4 billion loan came with £500 million in spending cuts, higher interest rates, strict monetary targets, and wage restraint. British growth slowed in 1968 as fiscal austerity contracted demand. Devaluation had not freed Britain from discipline; it had changed the institution imposing it. Workers still bore the adjustment costs while creditor confidence remained the system's first claim.

The dollar lesson was immediate. If Britain — America's closest ally, armed with capital controls, sterling-area ties, and IMF support — could not hold $2.80, how long could the United States defend $35 gold while foreign dollar claims multiplied and gold reserves fell? Foreign central banks watching London drew the obvious conclusion: fixed parities were political promises, and political promises could break.

De Gaulle accelerated French conversions after November 1967. Four months later, in March 1968, the London Gold Pool collapsed after hemorrhaging $3 billion as speculators tested the $35 official price. Sterling's fall did not cause the dollar's break by itself. It demonstrated the mechanism by which the break would come: one reserve promise failed, confidence migrated to the next, and the holders of paper claims learned to ask for metal before the window closed.

The remaining question was timing. At some point the United States would have to choose between honoring convertibility until the gold ran out and preserving the gold by ending convertibility. Britain's crisis made that choice visible four years before Nixon made it.

### The Triffin Dilemma plays out

Triffin's 1959–60 Congressional testimony, introduced in the previous chapter, identified the mathematical impossibility built into Bretton Woods' design. Here the numbers track its progression. U.S. gold reserves peaked at $24.6 billion in 1949–50 against modest foreign liabilities. Depending on whether one counts official holdings alone, private liquid claims, or offshore eurodollars, the crossover arrived between 1962 and 1964: foreign dollar claims had become larger than the gold stock available to redeem them. By 1966, reserves stood at $13.2 billion against roughly $14 billion in central-bank dollar holdings. By March 1968, reserves had fallen to $10.7 billion after the London Gold Pool lost $3 billion in five months. By August 1971, roughly $10 billion in gold stood behind several multiples of that amount in foreign dollar claims. The precise denominator varied by source. The direction did not.

The most important number was not any single year's reserve total. It was the crossing of a psychological line. Before the crossover, foreign central banks could believe that gold convertibility was strained but arithmetically plausible. After it, the promise depended on everyone not asking for fulfillment at once. That is not convertibility in the old sense. It is a bank run held back by diplomacy.

Diplomacy can slow a run, but it cannot repeal arithmetic forever.

The arithmetic constrained every available response. Stopping U.S. deficits would have created a global dollar shortage and triggered the deflationary collapse the gold hoarding of the 1930s had already shown the world. Continuing deficits guaranteed that liabilities would outrun gold, as they did. Increasing gold reserves was impossible because mining could not match the growth of world trade. Devaluing the dollar would have been the orderly solution, but it would also have rewarded every holder who rushed to convert before the next adjustment, accelerating the bank run rather than preventing it. The system broke because it was designed with the contradiction inside it. Triffin had shown this in 1959. France tested it systematically between 1965 and 1968, converting dollars into gold and forcing the abstract promise into bullion. Britain raised the question of a $3 billion gold cover on 11 August 1971. Nixon closed the gold window four days later.

That is why Triffin's dilemma was not a forecasting trick. It was a theory of institutional self-destruction. To keep the world liquid, the reserve country had to issue claims. To keep those claims trusted, it had to limit them. The two requirements could coexist only while the stock of outstanding claims remained small enough, or while confidence remained strong enough, that redemption was mostly ceremonial. Once redemption became strategic, the system had no stable resting place.

### De Gaulle's gold offensive: converting doubt into bullion

The abstract dilemma became concrete crisis when countries began testing American promises. The most aggressive challenger was France under President Charles de Gaulle, who viewed dollar dominance as American imperialism by monetary means.

On February 4, 1965, de Gaulle held a press conference that sent shockwaves through the Bretton Woods system. He announced France would begin converting substantial dollar reserves into gold, declaring: "We consider it necessary that international trade be established on an indisputable monetary base that does not bear the stamp of any particular country." The barely-veiled critique was clear—the dollar's reserve status gave America "exorbitant privilege" to finance deficits by printing money the world was forced to accept.

France backed words with action. Between 1962 and 1966, France reduced its dollar reserves and accumulated gold aggressively. French conversions alone accounted for $2.8 billion of the $3.3 billion total decline in U.S. gold stocks during this period—an astonishing 85 percent of American gold losses. Ships carrying gold bars sailed regularly from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to the Banque de France in Paris. Each shipment represented a vote of no confidence in the dollar's future.

The French weren't alone. Spain, under Franco's government, exchanged $60 million for gold in 1964-65—described as "the biggest transaction of the Franco era." Even this relatively modest sum mattered because it demonstrated that countries across the political spectrum—from Gaullist France to Francoist Spain—were questioning dollar credibility.

The conversions created a vicious cycle. Each country that converted dollars to gold had rational self-interest: get gold while reserves lasted. But collective action accelerated the crisis. As reserves dwindled, remaining dollar holders grew more nervous, increasing conversion pressure. The classic bank run dynamic—first out survives, last out loses—applied to sovereign central banks just as to retail depositors.

The cruelty of the mechanism was that loyalty became dangerous. A country that held dollars to support the system increased its exposure if others converted. A country that converted protected itself while weakening the system everyone relied on. Reserve cooperation therefore had the same collective-action problem as a bank lobby during a run: every participant preferred calm in general and early exit in particular.

No treaty can make last place attractive.

America couldn't simply refuse conversion without destroying the system's foundation. The entire Bretton Woods architecture rested on the promise that dollars were "as good as gold" because foreign official holders could convert them at $35 per ounce. Refusing conversion would prove the promise was empty, triggering immediate collapse. But honoring every conversion request would exhaust gold reserves, also triggering collapse. The U.S. was trapped.

The most dramatic test came four days before Nixon closed the gold window. On August 11, 1971, the British government—America's closest ally—raised the possibility of gold cover for a $3 billion dollar position. In Paul Volcker's later telling, the moment's significance was unmistakable: "If the British…were going to take gold for their dollars, it was clear the game was indeed over." Later scholarship has treated the request more carefully, as a contingent cover question rooted in sterling-defense politics rather than a simple demand for immediate shipment. But the political meaning at Treasury was the same. If even Britain was testing the promise, confidence had reached the breaking point.

Britain's request was particularly devastating because it came from a country deeply invested in the system's survival. If even Britain—with its "special relationship" to America, its dependence on Atlantic alliance, its own interest in reserve currency stability—was asking how much gold stood behind its dollars, no trust remained. The request demonstrated that when push came to shove, even allies would protect their own reserves before protecting the architecture.

The parallel to the modern dollar trap is not exact in mechanics, but it is clear in structure. In the 1960s, France and Britain accumulated dollars and then discovered that escape by conversion could itself break the system. Today, large dollar-reserve holders face a fiat version of the same constraint: selling too quickly would damage the value of what remains, while holding means exposure to American policy. De Gaulle could demand gold because Bretton Woods still promised a metal exit. Under the post-1971 dollar system, that exit is closed.

### The London Gold Pool: holding back the tide

The crisis came in waves. In November 1961, eight central banks formed the London Gold Pool, contributing 240 metric tons ($270 million) to defend the $35 per ounce price. The United States contributed 50 percent ($135 million); seven European central banks split the remainder. The Bank of England executed all transactions, selling gold when the London price approached $35.20 and buying when it weakened below $35.08.

For four years, the Pool worked. Between 1962 and 1965, it accumulated a cumulative surplus of $1.4 billion in purchases—the Pool was actually making money. But from 1965 to March 1968, it suffered a cumulative deficit of $3.7 billion. France withdrew in June 1967; the U.S. share rose from 50 to 60 percent. Between November 1967 and March 1968, the Pool lost nearly $3 billion in five months.

The Pool was a revealing institution because it tried to make a market price behave like a treaty clause. Private demand for gold was telling central banks that $35 was too low. The Pool answered by selling official gold into the market to keep the signal from becoming public proof. In quiet years, that could work. In crisis, it meant throwing reserves at a price everyone increasingly knew was political.

Sterling's devaluation changed the psychology. If one reserve currency could be marked down over a weekend, gold buyers could imagine the dollar being marked down next. The rush into gold after November 1967 was not irrational speculation. It was a rational attempt to hold the asset that could not be devalued by press conference.

On March 14-15, 1968, the crisis reached its peak. The Queen declared a bank holiday to close the London gold market. On March 16-17, central bankers met in emergency session in Washington. The London Gold Pool was disbanded. In its place emerged the two-tier gold market: an official tier where central banks traded at $35 per ounce, and a private tier where market forces determined the price. When the private market reopened on April 1, 1968, gold traded at $38 per ounce—an 8 percent premium. By May 21, it had reached $42.60.

The two-tier system was a tacit admission that the Bretton Woods gold price was unsustainable. Central banks agreed to trade gold only at $35 per ounce among themselves, to supply no gold to private markets from official stocks, and to buy little or no gold from private sellers. The system limped along for three more years, but the end was inevitable.

It also split monetary truth in two. Officially, gold was still $35. Privately, it was worth more. Officially, the dollar remained convertible. Practically, convertibility depended on central banks agreeing not to behave like private holders. The system survived after 1968 by narrowing the class of people allowed to test it.

### Camp David, August 13–15, 1971

Volcker received the call to Camp David on Friday, August 13, 1971. The Treasury car drove him from Washington up through the Maryland forests to the Catoctin Mountain retreat where Franklin Roosevelt had hosted Churchill and where John F. Kennedy had walked alone after the Bay of Pigs. He did not yet know what would be decided there. He suspected, having watched the system's gold backing shrink to a fraction of foreign dollar claims, that the answer would not be one he could be proud of.

The British cover question had arrived earlier in the week: what stood behind a three-billion-dollar position if sterling defense required protection? The figure, in the context of America's remaining gold stock, was not survivable as a simple conversion demand; it was roughly thirty percent of what was left. He had drafted, months earlier, a contingency plan in case convertibility had to be suspended. The plan that had circulated through Treasury under his hand contemplated a coordinated international devaluation of the dollar against gold, paired with an orderly reform of the system's reserve mechanism. What he was about to walk into at Camp David was something different.

The figures waiting at the cabin when Volcker arrived were the men who would make the decision: Treasury Secretary John Connally, the former Texas Governor whose negotiating ethic Volcker had not been raised to share; Federal Reserve Chairman Arthur Burns, who would argue against closing the window because he understood what it meant for the institution he led; OMB Director George Shultz; Paul McCracken and Herbert Stein of the Council of Economic Advisers; Peter Peterson of the new Council on International Economic Policy; and the speechwriter William Safire, whose later account would render most of the weekend visible to outside readers. The President was at Camp David. The decision he would take at the end of the weekend was, in some sense, settled. The question was the package around it.

Connally, who chaired the substantive discussions through Friday and Saturday, framed the problem the way the Nixon White House preferred to frame all problems involving foreign capital: the foreigners were taking advantage of America, and the American answer would be to stop letting them. The window would close. The dollar would be devalued. Wage and price controls would be imposed domestically to prevent the short-run inflation the devaluation would otherwise drive through the import sector. A ten-percent import surcharge would be added to bring trading partners to the table on currency adjustment. The package was, on the merits and on the politics, more aggressive than Volcker had recommended. He had wanted a coordinated international move; he was getting a unilateral one. He had wanted gold convertibility to be suspended explicitly as a temporary measure leading to system reform; he was watching it become rhetorically temporary and operationally permanent.

Burns argued against closure through the Friday and Saturday meetings. The Fed Chairman was the only senior figure present whose institutional remit included the dollar's external value, and he believed that the closure would be read by foreign central banks as default. Connally overruled him. Volcker's own role across the weekend was the role he had been trained to play: walk through the consequences of each option in turn, clarify the economic mechanism for non-economists in the room, and tell the President, when asked, what each variant of the package would cost. He told them what each variant would cost. The President chose Connally's package.

On Sunday evening, August 15, Nixon appeared on television. He did not use the phrase "gold window." He spoke instead of the country's "international money speculators," who, the President said, had "waged an all-out war on the American dollar." He announced the suspension of convertibility "temporarily" while the United States renegotiated the terms of the international system. The package included the wage and price controls and the import surcharge. The stock market, when it opened on Monday, rose. Public reaction was favourable. The decision, framed as decisive American leadership, played politically in a way an orderly multilateral devaluation would not have played.

Volcker spent the next two years inside the diplomatic effort to assemble a successor system. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 attempted a fixed-rate framework at devalued parities; it lasted fifteen months. By March 1973 the major currencies were floating against each other, and the Bretton Woods architecture was finished. The man who had drafted the contingency plans was now the senior official managing the consequences. He resigned from Treasury in 1974 to teach at Princeton, took the New York Fed presidency the following year, and in 1979 — when the inflation that the Connally package had partly enabled had run far enough to test the dollar's credibility under pure fiat — accepted Jimmy Carter's invitation to chair the Federal Reserve.

He would have a different chair, then, and a different problem. The system the Camp David weekend had ended was the system Volcker had spent his life inside. The system that replaced it was the one he would be asked, in October 1979, to defend.

The suspension was announced as temporary. It became permanent. By 1973 the Bretton Woods system had collapsed entirely. Major currencies floated against each other, their values determined by market forces rather than fixed parities. For the first time in history, the world's monetary system was based entirely on fiat currency — money backed by nothing but government decree and public confidence.

The gold standard, which had dominated monetary thinking for two centuries, was over. The world did not collapse. Inflation rose in the 1970s and was eventually broken; trade continued to expand; financial markets adapted to floating exchange rates. The post-1971 record made one observation available that the gold-standard century had concealed: institutional credibility, fiscal capacity, and competent monetary management could carry a currency without commodity backing.

But the end of gold did not mean the end of hierarchy. The dollar remained the central reserve asset because the world had already built its trade, debt, oil pricing, reserve management, and financial plumbing around dollar markets. The United States lost the obligation to redeem dollars for gold while retaining much of the benefit of issuing the asset others needed. That was the deeper transformation of 1971: the reserve center escaped metal discipline without losing reserve status.

For the United States, this created room no gold-standard country had enjoyed. It could run deficits, issue liabilities in its own currency, and rely on the depth of Treasury markets and the strategic need for dollar reserves to sustain demand. For countries outside the core, the constraint changed form rather than disappearing. They no longer needed gold to defend a parity, but many still needed dollars to buy oil, service external debt, import essential goods, or reassure investors. The old gold constraint had become a dollar constraint.

The anchor had moved from a metal in vaults to a balance sheet in Washington.

That is why the post-1971 system both vindicated and complicated the book's argument. It vindicated it because money clearly did not need commodity backing to function. It complicated it because not every fiat currency occupied the same place in the hierarchy. A country that issues debt in its own currency to domestic holders lives in a different monetary world from a country that must earn or borrow dollars to survive. Japan and Argentina, in the next chapter's terms, are not separated mainly by the size of their debt ratios. They are separated by their position in the currency hierarchy.

Milton Friedman warned that governments without gold's discipline would inflate currencies to worthlessness. Gold bugs predicted the dollar would collapse within a decade. Austrian-school economists insisted that money required commodity backing or chaos would follow. For the first time in history the world's reserve currency was backed by nothing but trust. Whether institutional credibility alone could sustain a global monetary system, and whether the public-choice and Currency School worries would eventually find an institutional answer, were the questions the next chapter would take up: slack, capacity stress, deliberate credibility, and the attempted escape into private money.
