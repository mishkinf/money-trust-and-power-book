# Chapter 9 Sources & Summary

### **Chapter Summary**

The chapter pairs the two interwar monetary catastrophes. Weimar shows the orthodox fear of discretionary over-issue under fiscal and political pressure: the Ruhr occupation, passive resistance, public payrolls, and Reichsbank finance destroyed the mark and wiped out middle-class savings, traced through Victor Klemperer's diaries and Dorothea Guenther's Preuss family memoir. Britain and France show the opposite danger: gold-standard discipline at untenable parities transferred adjustment costs onto workers, farmers, and unemployed young people. Churchill's 1925 return to gold at the old $4.86 parity imposed a severe internal deflationary adjustment on British workers; the chapter distinguishes the exchange-rate quote from Keynes's broader estimate of the wage-and-cost adjustment the parity required. Coal miners faced wage cuts and a seven-month lockout. Meanwhile, France and the United States hoarded gold, creating the deflationary shock that deepened the Depression. Roosevelt's 1933 break from gold — bank holiday, Emergency Banking Act, Executive Order 6102, gold nationalization, dollar devaluation, and gold-clause abrogation — was followed by rapid recovery. The cross-country pattern documented by Eichengreen and Sachs remains the chapter's comparative frame: countries that left gold earlier recovered earlier.
