# Chapter 05 Sources & Summary

### **Chapter Summary**

Chapter 5 explains the Bank of England's operating mechanism after Chapter 4 establishes the institutional bargain. Parliament's tax commitment made government debt credible; the Bank transformed that debt and subscribed capital into paper claims that could circulate. The targeted expansion restores the daily and institutional texture of that mechanism: clerks, ledgers, cashiers, directors, paper instruments, Exchequer links, government contractors, commercial bills, and the gradual routinization of trust. The micro-restoration adds sharper tax-receivability and settlement-hierarchy language: Bank paper sat nearer to the fiscal center than goldsmith receipts or country-bank notes, and country-bank money depended on London correspondents and access to Bank money for finality. The chapter emphasizes balance-sheet money creation, government spending before full tax collection, tax-linked demand, commercial bill discounting, the hierarchy between Bank money and country-bank money, country-bank fragility, and the real constraint of productive capacity. The chapter now treats the Bank as a hybrid system: not pure commodity money, not modern pure fiat, but transferable paper made credible by convertibility, public debt, parliamentary taxation, legal privilege, and network acceptance.

**Current source-pass note:**
32. Paterson biographical and Darien material has been moved out of this chapter's live argument and is now handled mainly in Chapter 4's source note. The redistribution/tax-policy digression was cut from Chapter 5 to keep this chapter operational rather than programmatic.
33. The chapter and summary now avoid the overcompressed claim that early Bank of England notes simply "were accepted for taxes" in the modern legal-tender sense. The live argument is narrower: Bank paper gained acceptability because it was connected to public debt, Exchequer operations, parliamentary taxation, London settlement, institutional privilege, and convertibility. Bank of England notes did not become general legal tender until 1833; the earlier claim is about proximity to the fiscal center and settlement hierarchy, not full legal-tender status.
