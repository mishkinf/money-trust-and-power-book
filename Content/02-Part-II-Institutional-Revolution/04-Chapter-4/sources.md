# Chapter 04 Sources & Summary

### **Chapter Summary**

After the Stop of the Exchequer exposed the weakness of goldsmith banking, the Glorious Revolution created the constitutional foundation for public credit. Chapter 4 now gives more texture to the 1688-1689 settlement: James II's absolutist and religious crisis, William's conditional accession, the Bill of Rights, parliamentary supply, appropriations, audit, and the Civil List. The restoration pass adds the mundane fiscal machinery that made creditors believe the new regime: named taxes, funded debt, repeated parliamentary supply, visible revenue streams, and a governing coalition internally exposed to the consequences of default. The Bank of England is framed as the institutional answer to Backwell's failure: goldsmith banking supplied the techniques, the tally system supplied the spend-against-future-revenue logic, Parliament supplied fiscal credibility, and the Bank fused them into scalable public-private credit. The chapter stresses that the Bank was neither simple private banking nor pure state fiat, but a public-private hybrid whose credibility came from parliamentary taxation, legal privilege, convertibility expectations, and its connection to the Exchequer.

**Source caution:**
18. The famous Paterson line about creating money "out of nothing" is treated in the chapter as a historically important but citation-sensitive phrase. The relevant public source trail is Saxe Bannister's 1858-1859 edition of *The Writings of William Paterson, Founder of the Bank of England, and of the Darien Colony*, Clapham's discussion of the Bank's early history, Richards's "First Fifty Years" article, and the later literature on the quote's contested transmission. A first OCR search of the Internet Archive scans of Bannister's three volumes did not locate the exact phrase, so the line should remain a famous attributed formulation rather than an unqualified primary quotation unless a stable scan/page is later found.
