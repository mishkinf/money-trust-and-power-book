# PART II: THE INSTITUTIONAL REVOLUTION

## Chapter 4: When Private Money Failed

**Chapter Premise:** Goldsmith banking emerged accidentally after Charles I's 1640 seizure of deposits, creating England's first private paper money through fractional reserve lending. The Stop of the Exchequer in 1672 destroyed this system, proving that private money creation—however ingenious—could not provide systemic stability.

---

A cold rain fell on London as William of Orange rode through the city gates in December 1688. He had crossed the Channel with an army, but he would take the throne without a battle. King James II, abandoned by his own officers and nobles, had fled to France. What followed was not merely a change of monarchs but a constitutional revolution—a fundamental restructuring of government that permanently transferred power from crown to Parliament, reshaping not just England's government but its entire financial system.¹

The settlement was explicit: William and Mary would rule, but only if they accepted Parliament's supremacy. The Bill of Rights (1689) declared that the monarch could not suspend laws, levy taxes, or maintain a standing army without parliamentary consent. For the first time in English history, the power to spend and the power to tax were legally inseparable. A king could no longer borrow on his personal credit and leave Parliament to clean up the mess. Every loan, every expenditure, every military campaign would require legislative approval.²

This was not a theoretical nicety. It was a financial revolution disguised as a political one. By tying the crown's hands, Parliament made England's debt credible. Lenders no longer had to trust a king's word; they could trust a nation's law.

---

### The goldsmith catastrophe: Why private money failed

To understand what the Bank of England would become, one must first grasp what existed before it—and why it failed catastrophically. The story begins not with the Glorious Revolution but with a desperate wartime improvisation that would accidentally create England's first private paper money, only to see it collapse in spectacular ruin.

In 1640, **Charles I seized £200,000 from the Royal Mint** to finance his escalating conflicts with Parliament and Scotland. The King's agents simply walked into the Mint, took the money, and left merchants and nobles with worthless receipts. The act destroyed confidence in government storage overnight. Wealthy Londoners who had trusted the crown with their bullion now faced a choice: where could they safely store their wealth?³

The answer came from an unlikely source: the goldsmiths of Lombard Street. This narrow medieval thoroughfare—just 260 meters from Bank junction to Gracechurch Street—had become London's financial heart. The street was lined with "good and lofty buildings" whose ground floors housed goldsmith shops, each marked by hanging signs that served as landmarks in an era of limited literacy: The Unicorn for Edward Backwell's establishment, The Grasshopper for the Gresham family, The Vine for Thomas and Robert Vyner.⁴

Inside these establishments, large arched windows opened onto the street, fitted with horizontal shutters that lowered at night for security. The lower shutter folded down to form a counter where customers stood outside while the goldsmith served from within. Samuel Pepys recorded visiting Backwell's shop on June 17, 1662, finding "his lady going to take coach stood in the shop" where a Portuguese merchant offered her "a gilded glassfull of perfumed comfits." These were not merely banking offices but social spaces where business and display intertwined—gilt vessels, imported delicacies, the banker's wife greeting clients.⁵

Behind the ground-floor shop, the buildings extended deep with living quarters above and, crucially, secure vaults below or in back rooms. These vaults—originally built to secure goldsmith inventory—became the foundation of England's first banking system. After 1640, goldsmiths began accepting deposits of gold and silver from wealthy Londoners who no longer trusted the crown. They issued receipts as proof of storage, initially simple warehouse documents: "Received from John Smith ten pounds sterling in gold coin, to be returned upon presentation of this receipt."⁶

By 1669, the scale of goldsmith banking had grown dramatically. Backwell showed Pepys "the model of his houses that he is going to build in Cornhill and Lumbard Street; but he hath purchased so much there, that it looks like a little town." By December 1670, Backwell had expanded his own house from 13 to 21 hearths—a substantial enlargement reflecting banking operations that now served hundreds of London's elite. Inside, scribal clerks maintained leather-bound ledgers in quill pen, recording deposits and withdrawals in double-entry bookkeeping. Backwell's 1671-72 customer ledger would eventually contain 666 folios detailing 400 customers' accounts, including the king himself, Prince Rupert, the East India Company, and hundreds of merchants, doctors, lawyers, and military officers.⁷

But during the English Civil War (1642-1651), a revolutionary change occurred. Goldsmiths began issuing receipts "payable to bearer" rather than to a named individual. This subtle shift transformed the receipts from personal IOUs into something that could circulate throughout London. A merchant receiving a goldsmith receipt in payment didn't need to visit the goldsmith to redeem it—he could simply pass it to the next person. London had accidentally invented **the first true private paper money in English history**.⁵

By the 1660s, goldsmith banking had become the dominant force in London finance. Edward Backwell, originally a goldsmith on Fleet Street, had transformed his business into something resembling a modern bank. His depositors included Charles II himself, the future James II, Samuel Pepys, and the East India Company. Contemporary accounts placed him at the center of a dense web of Restoration London commercial trust: a banker the Crown's officers visited regularly, a counterparty whose word was treated as good across the City, described in one period observation as "the frankest of the money men." He appears in Pepys's diary less as a famous individual than as the everyday infrastructure of English finance — present at the parties, consulted on the king's debts, collecting deposits and issuing receipts as routinely as a modern reader visits an ATM. Other major goldsmith-bankers — Robert Vyner, Alderman John Coggs, Gilbert Whitehall — competed for the business of England's elite, but Backwell's balance sheet, his royal client list, and his physical expansion on Lombard Street placed him first among equals.⁶

But this dominance would face a devastating test. On September 2, 1666, fire broke out in Thomas Farriner's bakery on Pudding Lane. Within hours, driven by strong winds, it had become an inferno consuming the City of London. By 10:00 a.m. on Monday, September 3, the flames reached "the wealthiest part of the City, the area around Lombard Street and Gracechurch Street." Pepys witnessed goldsmiths rushing to save their businesses: "Among others I now saw my little goldsmith, Stokes, receiving some friend's goods, whose house itself was burned the day after." Robert Vyner "had just enough time to collect his papers, detailing the vast debt owed to him by the crown, and plate." By 4:00 p.m., "the financial centre of the city (and home to many of England's wealthiest men) succumbed to the fire." Thomas Vincent recorded: "the houses tumble, tumble, tumble with a great crash, leaving the foundations open to the view of the Heavens."⁸

The Great Fire destroyed Lombard Street's physical infrastructure but could not destroy the system that had emerged there. Goldsmiths' ledgers—saved in desperate rushes as flames approached—contained the real value: the records of who owed what to whom. Backwell and others rebuilt. Within five years, Lombard Street rose again, more prosperous than before. Backwell's 1669-1670 expansion came after the Fire, proof that private banking had survived catastrophe. The ledgers, the reputations, the network of trust—these proved more durable than buildings. It was a crucial lesson in what mattered for a banking system: not physical gold in vaults but the institutional memory of obligations. The system that survived the Great Fire would prove unable to survive something more dangerous: a king's default.⁹

### The Discovery: How Bankers Realized Gold Could Multiply

The transformation from warehouse to bank happened through careful observation and bold calculation. A London goldsmith—we don't know which one first made the leap, as no one documented the eureka moment—noticed a pattern in his vault.

**The observation:** Day after day, week after week, the gold sat undisturbed. Depositors left it there. When withdrawals occurred, they averaged only about 1/36th of total deposits on any given day. Some customers withdrew, others deposited, but the net balance barely fluctuated. The vault held £10,000 in gold, yet typical daily withdrawals never exceeded £300.

Then a merchant approached requesting a loan of £500 for a trading voyage to the East Indies. He needed capital to purchase wool for export, couldn't wait months for his own ship to return, and offered to pay 8 percent interest—two points above the legal usury limit of 6 percent, quietly arranged as "exchange" rather than loan.

**The goldsmith's calculation:**

- Gold in vault: £10,000
- Typical daily withdrawals: £300 (3% of deposits)
- Maximum withdrawal in any week: £500 (5% of deposits)
- Probability all depositors withdraw simultaneously: Near zero based on years of observation
- Loan requested: £500
- Interest at 8%: £40 annually
- Current income from storage fees: ~£10 on £10,000

The mathematics were compelling. If he lent £500 from £10,000 in deposits, he would still have £9,500 in the vault—far more than needed to meet typical withdrawal demands. The risk was real but calculable: what if depositors demanded more than usual? But years of experience suggested they wouldn't.

**The innovation:** Instead of giving the borrower physical gold, the goldsmith issued him a bearer receipt for £500—"payable to bearer on demand." The borrower walked out with paper. The goldsmith still had all £10,000 in gold sitting in his vault. But now £10,500 in receipts circulated in London—£10,000 in legitimate deposit receipts plus £500 in the loan receipt.

**Money had been created from nothing.**

The goldsmith had just discovered fractional reserve banking through empirical observation and arithmetic. The ledger showed the mechanism clearly in double-entry form:

**Assets (what goldsmith owns):**
- Gold in vault: £10,000
- Loan to merchant: £500

**Liabilities (what goldsmith owes):**
- Deposits: £10,000
- Bearer receipt issued: £500

The books balanced perfectly. But the goldsmith now had £10,500 in claims against £10,000 in gold. This would work perfectly as long as not everyone demanded their gold simultaneously.

The first goldsmith who tried this probably felt enormous anxiety about the risk. What if word spread? What if all depositors came tomorrow? What if the borrower defaulted and couldn't repay? The receipt circulated through London—from borrower to wool merchant to cloth seller to tavern keeper. Each person who received it assumed it was backed by actual gold in the vault. And in a sense, it was—just not exclusively.

But nothing catastrophic happened. The loan receipt circulated like any other. The borrower repaid after the voyage returned profit. The system worked. Word spread among goldsmiths—cautiously, through observation of competitors rather than public announcement. Within a decade, every major goldsmith on Lombard Street did the same thing: lending multiples of their actual reserves through issuance of bearer receipts.

They had accidentally discovered what modern economics calls money creation through credit expansion. The goldsmith didn't lend existing money—he created new money by making the loan. The mechanism: a promise backed by sufficient reserves to meet probable demand, not actual demand.

**How fractional reserves actually worked:** Historical records show typical cash balances fluctuated by only 1/36th of total deposits day-to-day. This meant goldsmiths could safely maintain reserve ratios (the percentage of deposits held as reserves rather than lent out) of approximately 10 percent—holding just £1 in gold for every £10 in circulating receipts. The bolder goldsmiths went lower. The more cautious went higher. Richard Cantillon, writing in the 1730s, documented ranges from 10 percent to 66 percent across different goldsmith-bankers.

---

**Was this authorized?** No. Goldsmith banking emerged in a legal gray area—no charters, no parliamentary authorization, purely emergent based on commercial practice. This contrasts sharply with chartered institutions like Sweden's Riksbank (1668) or Amsterdam's Wisselbank (1609), which operated under explicit government mandate. English goldsmith receipts represent perhaps the only clear historical case of large-scale private money creation without initial state sanction.⁹

### The Stop

The morning of January 2, 1672 was cold on Lombard Street. The proclamation that would destroy the goldsmith-bankers had been signed at Whitehall the day before, but Backwell did not yet know it. The London Gazette would not print it until January 4. The news travelled the way bad news travelled in seventeenth-century London — from clerk to clerk, by foot, faster than any printing press, and Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn would have heard within hours.

What Charles II had done, in the language of statute, was to suspend repayment of all debts owed by the Crown to its bankers — £1.3 million in total — for one year. What he had done, in plain language, was default. The king needed cash for the Third Anglo-Dutch War; he had eighty-two warships being fitted out; he could not pay both his fleet and his bankers, and so he had stopped paying his bankers. The statute called this the Stop of the Exchequer. The bankers, when they understood what had happened, called it ruin.

Backwell did the arithmetic the way any banker does. The Crown owed him £295,994 — twenty-two percent of the total Stop, and very nearly the whole of his capital. The interest alone, at the rate the king had been paying, would have supported his family and his clerks for years. That stream had now stopped. His depositors, meanwhile — the Duke of Albemarle, Sir George Carteret, the East India Company, hundreds of merchants — could still demand their money on any morning they chose. The asset side of his ledger had just frozen. The liability side had not. The fundamental equation of banking, borrow short and lend long, had become a death spiral, and he had a few weeks at most before it ran its course.

He chose, in the end, to do what any man in his position would have done. He kept the doors open and begged. The king, he was told, would pay six percent interest someday, when revenues allowed; the principal would not be paid for at least a year. Backwell knew what those words were worth from a Treasury that had just admitted it could not pay. He knew, too, what would happen at the Unicorn the moment the news spread.

It spread within days. Depositors began arriving on Lombard Street, some calm and asking careful questions, others demanding their gold immediately. Each withdrawal pulled at reserves that had been calibrated for the calmest of normal times. Each anxious question spread to the next depositor in line. The classic dynamics of a bank run — rational for any individual, catastrophic for all of them together — were now playing out on a single narrow street in the City of London.

The Treasury summoned an emergency meeting on **Sunday, January 7, at four in the afternoon**. The minute books preserve the attendance: the Duke of York, Prince Rupert, the Duke of Monmouth, "Sir Robt. Viner, Alderman Backwell, Mr. Meynell, Mr. Lyndsy, Mr. Snow, Mr. Portman, and other bankers concerned." Backwell sat at that table knowing his life's work was crumbling. He had built a banking empire that served royalty and merchants; he had accumulated, by the standards of any age, real wealth; he had expanded his Lombard Street holdings into what Pepys called "a little town." His business model had been sound, his lending prudent, his reserves adequate under any normal definition of the word. The king had simply changed the rules.

The bankers asked for what they could ask for: quarterly interest payments, legal protection against lawsuits from depositors, a formal accounting of the debt. The Treasury promised to "ask the King's pleasure." On **January 19**, the Crown stripped Backwell of his appointment as receiver of the Queen's Portuguese revenues, eliminating one of the last income streams he still possessed. There was no rescue. There were no emergency loans. The goldsmith-bankers were on their own.

The cascade began within weeks. Five of the major goldsmith houses — Backwell's, Vyner's, three others — failed in the first months. Nine more were severely undermined. Over ten thousand wealthy families who had trusted their savings to the bankers or held their receipts in payment were financially ruined. Bishop Gilbert Burnet wrote that "the bankers were broken, and multitudes who had put their money in their hands were ruined by this dishonourable and perfidious action." Commerce in London ground to a halt. Credit dried up. Cash became scarce.¹⁰

Backwell himself fought for a decade. The Crown made sporadic, insufficient payments. He continued in official service because the Crown was his only hope of eventual recovery, and because he had nowhere else to turn. The banking operations never recovered liquidity. The depositors who had trusted the Unicorn became creditors in a bankruptcy proceeding that would outlive him. Some accepted partial payments. Others sued. Many waited years for pennies on the pound. In 1682 — ten years to the month after the Stop — Backwell was formally declared bankrupt. He died in exile in the Netherlands on June 13, 1683, his life's work destroyed not by any error of his own but by a king's choice between fleet and creditors.¹¹

Sir Robert Vyner, owed over £400,000 — the largest single creditor — fared no better. Vyner had made Charles II's coronation regalia. He had served as Lord Mayor of London in 1674–75. He had stood at the pinnacle of Restoration society. The Stop ruined him too. The courts declared him bankrupt in 1683–84; his will ordered the sale of his entire estate to pay creditors thirty percent of principal — thirty pence on the pound, after twelve years of waiting. He died on September 2, 1688, reported to have been broken-hearted by the death of his only son, though no one who knew his finances doubted that the Stop had done its work too.¹²

**The legal battle consumed a generation.** The crown's promise of "one year" suspension proved worthless. From 1672 to 1675—three years—the government paid nothing. Not interest, not principal. When payments finally began in 1675, they covered only a fraction of obligations. From 1680 to 1685, the crown paid only 56 percent of expected amounts. Under James II (1685-1688), payments collapsed to just 21 percent. After the Glorious Revolution (1688), payments "dried up altogether" as William and Mary reallocated hereditary excise revenues to the Nine Years' War against France.¹³

Creditors turned to the courts. The Court of Exchequer ruled for the bankers in 1692—twenty years after the Stop. The Court of Exchequer Chamber reversed the decision in 1696 "on technical grounds which left a general feeling that an injustice had been done" among creditors. The House of Lords ruled for the bankers in January 1700—but Parliament had already allocated all hereditary excise revenues to current spending, rendering the legal victory meaningless.¹⁴

Parliament imposed the final settlement in 1701 through the Appropriation of Revenue Act. The terms were devastating: interest rates reduced from 6 percent to 3 percent, payments only from December 1705 forward (ignoring all missed interest from 1680 to 1704), and recognition of principal at effectively 50 percent of original value. In 1716, the National Debt Act reduced the rate further to 2.5 percent. A modern scholar calculated the effective investment return over the entire period: 0.01 percent annually. Creditors who had lent the king money in 1672 expecting 8-10 percent returns received virtually nothing over thirty-four years.¹⁵

The message was unmistakable: private banking without institutional support was financial suicide. When the Bank of England was chartered in 1694—still years before the Stop's creditors would receive final settlement—Parliament designed it explicitly to prevent such catastrophe. The Bank would have a royal charter, parliamentary backing, and the power to issue uniform currency. It would serve as lender of last resort when private credit froze. Most importantly, its debts would be secured by parliamentary taxation, not royal whim. The goldsmith catastrophe had taught England that private money creation requires public institutional support.

The Stop proved two things. First, goldsmith receipts had become true money—otherwise the default wouldn't have caused such catastrophic systemic disruption. Second, and more importantly, the Stop demonstrated the fatal flaw of unregulated private money: no crisis resolution mechanism. When the system failed, there was no lender of last resort (an institution that can provide emergency liquidity to banks during crises), no deposit insurance, no government backstop.¹³

The solution, when it finally came in 1694, was state intervention. The Bank of England was chartered explicitly to solve the problems the goldsmith catastrophe had revealed: create a stable institutional framework, provide government finance on sound terms, issue uniform currency backed by parliamentary taxation, and offer crisis liquidity when private credit froze.¹⁴

The Stop of the Exchequer proved that private money creation, however ingenious, could not provide systemic stability. What England needed was not more private ingenuity but a public institution—one that could create money with the full backing of the state's fiscal power. That innovation would come in 1694, but not before one final attempt to solve the crisis through war and revolution. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 would provide the constitutional foundation; the Bank of England would provide the institutional mechanism. Together, they would transform not just England's finances but the nature of money itself.

That same lesson would be relearned in September 2008. When Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on the fifteenth of that month, the global financial system seized within hours. Money market funds — which millions of Americans used like checking accounts — broke the buck. The repo markets, where banks borrowed overnight against collateral, froze. Asset-backed commercial paper became unsellable. The shadow banking system that had grown up alongside regulated banking — a vast network of private money creation operating outside the formal regulatory perimeter — collapsed at the speed of a wire transfer. The Federal Reserve responded with emergency lending facilities, trillions in liquidity support, and explicit guarantees for money market funds. Critics called it a bailout. The honest description is that the Fed had been forced into the role the Bank of England was chartered to play in 1694: lender of last resort to a private credit system that could not save itself. Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn would have recognized the dynamics on Lombard Street in 1672 instantly. The instruments had changed; the failure mode had not.

The answer came from a Scottish merchant named William Paterson, who proposed in 1694 something that seemed impossible: a bank that could create money "out of nothing," tied directly to Parliament's power to tax. Not the king's promise. Not gold reserves. Not merchant reputation. Parliamentary taxation — the one thing that had proven reliable since 1688. The mechanism he designed would become the template for every central bank since.
