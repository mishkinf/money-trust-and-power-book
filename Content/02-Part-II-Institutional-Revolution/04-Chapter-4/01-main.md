# PART II: THE INSTITUTIONAL REVOLUTION

## Chapter 4: When Private Money Failed

A cold rain fell on London as William of Orange rode through the city gates in December 1688. He had crossed the Channel with an army, but he would take the throne without a battle. King James II, abandoned by officers and nobles, had fled to France. The transfer looked bloodless from a distance. Up close, it was a revolution in the thing that mattered most to creditors: who controlled the purse.

The settlement was explicit. William and Mary would rule, but only if they accepted Parliament's supremacy. The Bill of Rights of 1689 declared that the monarch could not suspend laws, levy money, or maintain a standing army without parliamentary consent. England had long traditions of tax consent, but now the power to spend and the power to tax were being tied together in a more durable constitutional form. A king could no longer borrow on his personal credit, default when convenient, and leave the City to guess whether repayment depended on royal gratitude.

The political drama had been about religion, dynasty, and fear of absolutism. James II had tried to suspend laws that blocked Catholics from public office, expanded the standing army, and prosecuted the seven bishops who petitioned against his Declaration of Indulgence. The birth of a Catholic male heir in 1688 turned a temporary anxiety into a dynastic future. William's landing was therefore a military intervention, a Protestant succession crisis, and a parliamentary opportunity at once.

For money, the decisive fact was not that James lost. It was that William won on terms. The Convention Parliament did not merely replace one ruler with another. It made the new monarchy dependent on parliamentary supply. The Commons could now use money as leverage: taxes had to be voted, appropriations could be specified, accounts could be audited, and war finance had to pass through institutions that could say no. That did not make Parliament altruistic. It made the state's promises more credible because the people authorizing debt service were also the people controlling the revenues that would pay it.

This was not a theoretical nicety. It was a financial revolution disguised as a political one. By tying the Crown's hands, Parliament made English debt more credible. Lenders no longer had to trust a king's word alone. They could look to a nation's law.

The new controls were practical. Supply bills could specify what money was for. Commissioners of public accounts could examine how war funds had been spent. Annual sessions made the Crown return again and again to the Commons for revenue. The Civil List separated the monarch's household and ordinary civil expenses from the extraordinary costs of war. Each device narrowed the space in which a ruler could treat public money as personal money.

Creditors noticed because these devices changed enforcement. A tax voted for debt service was not a private assurance whispered by a minister. It was a public arrangement many political actors now had reason to defend. If the Crown wanted more money next year, it had to preserve credibility this year. The machinery looked dull because that is how credibility often looks: voted supply, named taxes, audited accounts, appropriations, and recurring cash flows under parliamentary supervision.

The comparison with France made the advantage sharper. Louis XIV could command a richer and more populous kingdom, but French public credit remained tangled in privilege, venal office, tax farming, and the fear that royal necessity would rewrite obligations. England was smaller, but after 1688 it could offer creditors something France struggled to match: a mechanism for making repayment politically costly to abandon. That mechanism, not English virtue, was the revolution.

That was the beginning of funded debt in the modern English sense. A lender was not merely promised that the king intended to repay. He could see the revenue source, the statute, the parliamentary session, the officers, and the political consequences of nonpayment. The state's promise became less personal and more procedural. The procedure was imperfect, often corrupt, and deeply unequal, but it was legible. Creditors lend more cheaply when they can see not only who owes them, but how the debtor will be made to pay.

### The wreckage private banking left behind

The need for a new institution was visible on Lombard Street. Backwell, Vyner, and the goldsmith-bankers had already shown that private credit could create money. Their receipts circulated. Their ledgers transferred balances. Their vaults held only a fraction of the claims written against them. In normal times, the system worked because reputation, convertibility, and interbank settlement were enough.

The Stop of the Exchequer showed the limit. The goldsmiths' assets were claims on the Crown; their liabilities were promises to depositors. When Charles II suspended payment in 1672, the asset side froze while the liability side remained payable on demand. The failure was not that the goldsmiths had misunderstood arithmetic. It was that their private money rested on a public debtor they could not discipline.

That balance-sheet mismatch is worth slowing down over. A depositor did not care that Backwell's claim on the king might someday be honored. He wanted his money now. A goldsmith could be solvent in the long run and ruined in the present if enough depositors demanded payment before the Crown repaid. The Stop turned a sovereign decision into a private liquidity crisis. The public debtor could wait. The private banker could not.

That distinction matters. Goldsmith receipts were not worthless fantasies. They were useful precisely because London trusted the houses that issued them. But they had no public crisis mechanism. No lender of last resort stood behind them. No Parliament had pledged taxes to protect their liabilities. No institutional rule prevented the sovereign from shifting the cost of war onto the bankers' balance sheets. Private money had expanded trust, then discovered that trust was not the same thing as authority.

The goldsmiths had built techniques that the future would keep: deposit accounts, transferable receipts, clearing among houses, loans against public revenue, and fractional reserves. What failed was not the banking technology. It was the political foundation beneath it. A private banker could create credit, but he could not make the sovereign's promise final. He could not compel taxes. He could not bind a future ministry. He could not turn a personal royal debt into a national obligation.

This is why the Bank of England should not be described as a miracle invention that appeared from nowhere in 1694. It was an institutional recombination. Goldsmith banking supplied the techniques. The tally system supplied the older logic of spending against future revenue. The Glorious Revolution supplied constitutional constraint. Parliament supplied taxes. The Bank fused them into a machine that could scale.

The lesson the City drew was not that paper money was impossible. The lesson was sharper: paper money could not rest on a king's convenience.

### William Paterson's dangerous idea

By 1694, William III needed money for war against France. The old methods were exhausted. Forced loans had poisoned politics. Goldsmith credit had been shattered by the Stop. Taxes alone could not deliver cash quickly enough for the Nine Years' War. England needed a way to turn future parliamentary revenue into present purchasing power.

William Paterson, a Scottish merchant and projector, proposed the mechanism. He was the kind of man early modern finance kept producing: half visionary, half opportunist, always thinking in systems larger than the institutions around him. His later Darien scheme would imagine rerouting world commerce through a Scottish colony in Panama. Before that disaster, he helped imagine something more durable: a national bank joined to parliamentary credit.

The plan was audacious because it fused three things that had previously been unstable when kept apart. Private investors would subscribe capital. Parliament would authorize taxes to service the debt. A chartered bank would issue transferable paper claims that could circulate more widely than the private receipts of Lombard Street.

Paterson's most famous phrase - that the bank would profit from money created "out of nothing" - has a complicated documentary afterlife and should be treated carefully. But the underlying mechanism was not a myth. The proposed bank would not merely gather existing coin and hand it to the government. It would transform a funded public debt into circulating credit.

The timing gave the idea force. William was fighting Louis XIV, and war made liquidity urgent. Taxes could be voted, but taxes arrived slowly. Armies, navies, victuallers, and allies demanded payment before the full revenue stream could be collected. Paterson's proposal answered that time problem. Parliament's future tax stream would become a present asset; the Bank would turn that asset into paper; paper would move through the economy before taxes had done their work.

This was not a loophole in taxation. It was a way of reorganizing time. The state could not wait until every assessment had been collected, every excise remitted, and every customs receipt counted before paying men who needed wages now. Nor could it return to the old Stuart habit of squeezing private financiers until they broke. The Bank's founding proposal offered a third path: make future public revenue visible enough that private investors would advance present purchasing power against it.

That was why subscription mattered. Subscribers were asked to convert private wealth into a claim on a new institution whose main asset would be the government's funded debt. They were betting not simply on Paterson's cleverness or William's military need, but on the post-1688 settlement itself. If Parliament could not maintain the taxes pledged to the loan, the Bank would be born weak. If Parliament could, the Bank would turn a war loan into the foundation of a national credit system.

That was why the Bank of England belonged to the history of sovereignty as much as to the history of banking. It did not abolish the state. It made the state's future claims transferable through a corporation. A public promise was being packaged in a private form.

### The 1694 bargain

The bargain was straightforward. Parliament chartered the Bank of England on July 27, 1694. Subscribers raised a loan of £1.2 million for the government. In return, the government promised 8 percent annual interest, plus a management allowance, and Parliament earmarked taxes to pay that obligation. The Bank received privileges: corporate status, a close relationship with the Exchequer, and the right to issue notes and other paper instruments against its assets.

The subscription itself was a political signal. Investors were not simply buying into a company. They were testing whether the post-1688 state could turn parliamentary authorization into reliable income. The government promised roughly £100,000 a year in interest and management payments on the founding loan, funded by taxes Parliament had approved. A failed subscription would have told Europe that parliamentary finance was a slogan. A successful one told Amsterdam, London, and every future creditor that England could organize private capital around public revenue.

Nothing about this arrangement abolished private profit. The Bank was owned by shareholders. Its directors expected returns. Its privileges were valuable because they were exclusive. But this was not a return to the goldsmith model. A goldsmith note said, in effect: trust this house. A Bank of England note increasingly said something larger: trust this institution because Parliament has made the state's future revenues part of the promise.

The distinction was visible in the first transaction. The government did not wait for years of taxes to accumulate in a chest before fighting France. It borrowed against taxes that Parliament had committed to levy. The Bank turned that commitment into immediately spendable paper. Soldiers, naval suppliers, shipwrights, contractors, and merchants could be paid before the tax stream had fully arrived. The future was being pulled into the present through institutional credit.

This was the old tally principle in a new form. Medieval tallies had allowed the Crown to spend against future revenue inside a narrow administrative system. The Bank of England made the same logic liquid, transferable, and national. Its notes could move through markets; its stock could be owned by investors; its claims could be priced; its relationship to the state could be renewed, contested, and supervised through Parliament.

The newness lay in scale and marketability. A tally was tied to a particular revenue stream and an administrative world of sheriffs, Exchequer officials, and assignment. Bank stock and Bank paper belonged to a broader market. They could be bought, sold, discounted, pledged, and compared with other claims. Public debt was becoming not only an obligation but an asset class. Once that happened, creditors had a direct interest in the constitutional order that protected the debt.

This was the quiet political genius of the arrangement: it converted creditors from nervous outsiders into stakeholders in the regime. A goldsmith with a royal assignment could be sacrificed when the Crown ran short. A Bank shareholder held a claim embedded in an institution Parliament itself had created. The more widely those claims circulated, the more people had reason to defend the machinery that serviced them.

### What the paper did

The Bank's early paper was not one simple instrument. It issued sealed bills, running cash notes, and account transfers - a family of claims that performed different jobs. Some bore interest. Some were closer to bearer notes. Some functioned like instructions to move balances. The details mattered less than the new institutional fact: the Bank held claims on the state; the public held claims on the Bank; taxes stood behind the state's side of the arrangement. A private balance sheet had been connected to a public fiscal engine.

Critics saw the novelty almost immediately. By the mid-1690s, pamphleteers were already complaining that the Bank could issue notes in quantities no ordinary corporation could match. They were not wrong to notice the danger. The Bank's power was extraordinary. But the extraordinary feature was not that paper had replaced reality. It was that legal and fiscal reality had been reorganized so paper could carry state credit through private hands.

The result was a hierarchy. Coin still mattered. Gold and silver still anchored confidence, especially in foreign settlement. But domestic monetary trust no longer had to begin and end with metal in a vault. At the top stood the state, with its taxing power. Beneath it stood the Bank, privileged by Parliament and connected to the Exchequer. Beneath that stood the growing world of private payments.

---

### Why creditors believed it

The key innovation was not paper. Paper had existed. The key innovation was credible commitment.

After 1688, the monarch could not unilaterally levy taxes. That limitation sounds like a constraint on state power, and it was. But for creditors it was also a source of strength. If Parliament approved taxes for debt service, lenders could believe that repayment rested on a broader political settlement than royal appetite. No institution can make default impossible, but this one made repudiation harder, more public, and more damaging to the governing coalition. Bondholders, merchants, landowners, officeholders, and members of Parliament now had overlapping reasons to preserve public credit. Credible commitment was not a promise made by better people. It was a trap built by interested people.

Foreign creditors could read that difference. A Dutch investor did not need to love English politics to prefer an English funded loan to a French royal promise. He needed to believe that the English system had more veto points against arbitrary default and more insiders committed to payment. That belief could lower borrowing costs, lengthen maturities, and make war finance less dependent on emergency bargains. In the long run, this was how a smaller island state competed with a larger continental monarchy: by making its promises easier to finance.

This is the strongest version of the financial-revolution argument, and also its limit. Parliamentary control made the state more credible to lenders, but it did not make the state democratic in any modern sense. The taxpayers who serviced the debt were not identical with the investors who held it. The architecture solved the sovereign's credibility problem by giving creditors a better claim on future public revenue. That was a constitutional achievement and a distributional arrangement at the same time.

Nor did Parliament remove coercion from the fiscal state. It regularized it. Taxes could be broader, more predictable, and more enforceable because they were authorized through the accepted machinery of government. For lenders, that regularity was reassurance. For taxpayers, it meant the state could reach more deeply and more durably into economic life. Public credit is easy to praise if one looks only at interest rates. It looks more ambiguous when one remembers that every reliable coupon required a reliable tax.

That is why the Bank of England could do what Backwell could not. Backwell had been wealthy, trusted, and operationally sophisticated. He could issue receipts, clear accounts, and lend against future tax assignments. What he lacked was authority over the fiscal foundation beneath those assignments. The Bank did not eliminate that problem by being more clever. It changed the foundation. Its promises sat inside a parliamentary fiscal machine.

### Money creation with a governor

The Bank of England did create money, but not in the cartoon sense of costless magic. It created credit inside a set of political and legal restraints. Its notes were liabilities. Its assets included government debt. Its privileges depended on Parliament. Its reputation depended on convertibility, prudence, and the belief that the state would honor the funded debt.

That mixture is what made the institution powerful. Commodity theorists could look at the Bank and see paper that promised metal. Chartalists could look at the same institution and see tax-backed state credit. Both were seeing part of the machine. The Bank's notes mattered because they stood at the intersection of private balance sheets, public debt, tax authority, and legal privilege.

This is also why the Bank was not simply a libertarian triumph of private banking. It was a public-private hybrid. Private subscribers supplied capital and governance. Parliament supplied the charter, the fiscal backing, and the legal privileges. The Crown received spending power. The market received a more reliable monetary instrument. No single actor owned the entire system, which was precisely the point. The old sovereign's dilemma had been that money depended too much on one man's discretion. The new system distributed discretion across institutions.

The result was not democratic money in any modern sense. The investors were wealthy. The taxpayers were broader than the bondholders. War finance remained brutal. But the architecture changed. The Crown wanted war finance. Parliament wanted control. Investors wanted reliable income. Merchants wanted a payments system. Taxpayers wanted representation before extraction, even if representation remained narrow. The Bank worked because these motives could be made to reinforce one another.

---

### The price of credibility

The Bank of England solved one problem by creating another. It made public credit liquid. It made war finance scalable. It gave England a monetary instrument stronger than goldsmith receipts and more flexible than coin. But it also tied money creation to a state increasingly capable of mobilizing society for war.

That is the double edge of institutional money. A credible monetary system can finance roads, ships, trade, industry, and public administration. It can also finance conquest. The same fiscal machinery that made creditors comfortable made the British state formidable. Public credit was not morally innocent. It was power made liquid.

This is why the Bank belongs in a book about money, trust, and power rather than in a footnote about banking history. It changed who could be trusted, how trust could be priced, and how far the state could project force. The old problem had been that sovereign power was too arbitrary for creditors. The new problem was that once sovereign power became credible, it could borrow on a scale that made war and empire easier to sustain.

The Bank of England did not prove that money was just metal. It proved that a transferable promise could circulate when public debt, taxation, corporate privilege, and political constraint made the promise hard to break.

Backwell's world ended because private credit had no anchor strong enough to survive sovereign default. Paterson's world began by attaching credit creation to Parliament's power to tax. The next question is how that machine worked closely enough, and routinely enough, that people used its promises as money.
