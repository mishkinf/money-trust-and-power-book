# PART II: THE INSTITUTIONAL REVOLUTION

By the late seventeenth century England's monetary problem had a name: private credit could expand, but it could not survive the failure of the sovereign at its base. The goldsmith-bankers had built a working paper currency on Lombard Street; the Stop of the Exchequer in 1672 destroyed it in days. What England needed was not more private ingenuity but a public institution that could create money on the full credit of the state.

That institution would emerge from a two-step process: a constitutional revolution that put taxation in the hands of Parliament, and a financial innovation that turned parliamentary taxation into a perpetual machine for manufacturing credit. The two together would solve the credibility problem that had plagued European monarchs for three centuries — and would do so by serving the interests of those who held the new debt as much as those who paid the new taxes. The Bank of England's success was not neutral. It was a victory of bondholder over debtor that would shape British and global finance for the next three hundred years.
