# Chapter 07 Sources & Summary

### **Chapter Summary**

Chapter 7 now focuses on the Bank Restriction period as a proof-and-cost chapter. The 1797 suspension of gold convertibility lasted twenty-four years, yet Bank notes continued to circulate because of institutional credibility, merchant support, tax capacity, productive resources, and expectations of eventual resumption. The chapter is careful about temporariness: the suspension was not expected to last only "six weeks," but the first statutory endpoint was June 24, 1797, before repeated extensions turned an emergency bridge into a generation-long regime. The Bullionist Controversy is presented as a serious creditor-safety argument rather than a straw man, with Henry Thornton supplying the missing middle: paper can work without gold, but rapid contraction imposes real pain. The 1821 return to gold at the pre-1797 parity is framed as the decisive distributional event. Wheat-price collapse, named farmer insolvencies, laborer diet testimony, and Attwood's Birmingham critique show how restoring the old promise shifted the burden onto debtors, farmers, manufacturers, and workers.
