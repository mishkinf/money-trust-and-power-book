# PART III: THE GOLD STANDARD ERA AND ITS CONTRADICTIONS

The gold standard was not designed. It was the product of an arithmetic error by Isaac Newton in 1717, an unexpected flood of Brazilian gold a few years later, and the slow drift of a bimetallic system whose silver kept escaping abroad. By 1880 — a century and a half later — most of the industrial world was on it, treating an English accident as economic law.

Once installed, gold did something more dangerous than constrain monetary policy. It erased the memory of how money had worked before it. What William Paterson had stated plainly in 1694 — that the Bank of England created money out of nothing, sustained by Parliament's taxing power — became unspeakable by 1850. Thomas Attwood, who watched the 1821 return to gold destroy his Birmingham workers and said so in front of crowds of forty thousand, was dismissed in respectable London as a crank. The economists who replaced him produced a doctrine that worked beautifully for the people who held government bonds, and ruinously for everyone else. This was not the discovery of monetary truth. It was the consolidation of class interest into orthodoxy. And it would cost millions when the system finally broke.
