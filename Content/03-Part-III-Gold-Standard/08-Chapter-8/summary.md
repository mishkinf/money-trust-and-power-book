# Chapter 8 Summary: The Great Forgetting

Between 1810 and 1850, the Currency School's program — defensible on its own terms as a response to the genuine threat of unconstrained inconvertible paper — settled into British monetary doctrine and was embedded into statute by the Bank Charter Act of 1844. Thomas Attwood and the Birmingham School correctly identified monetary contraction as the cause of mass unemployment and gathered 1.28 million signatures on the Chartist petition; they were dismissed as "currency cranks" and excluded from respectable discourse. The chapter traces how the orthodoxy was consolidated through institutional channels — Parliament, the Bank's Court of Directors, Treasury consultation, and Bank operating problems — rather than through theoretical argument that out-reasoned the Banking School on the merits. Robert Peel defined the pound as "a certain definite quantity of gold" on May 6, 1844, and the Act passed two months later despite prophetic warnings from Benjamin Hawes, James Wilson, Thomas Tooke, and John Fullarton that credit would simply find alternative forms. It did. The Currency School's tragic error was assuming that bank deposits weren't money: by 1900 English payments by value were overwhelmingly settled through deposits rather than notes, and unconstrained deposit creation had become the modern banking system. The same trajectory played out in the United States after the National Banking Acts of 1863–1864. Samuel Gurney's testimony during the October 1847 crisis showed that the Act's rigid restrictions inflamed panic rather than preventing it; suspensions in 1847, 1857, and 1866 vindicated the Banking School in practice without dislodging the orthodoxy in theory. Lord Overstone (Samuel Jones Loyd), the Act's true architect, advised the men who could write and defend the bill; the Currency School's victory was institutional channel control, not intellectual triumph. Colonial hut taxes in Kenya, Uganda, and Rhodesia proved chartalism through coercion even as London economists denied it in theory: tax obligation in colonial currency forced currency adoption. What had been explicit in 1694 — that banks create money "out of nothing" — was now unthinkable in respectable economics. The household budget metaphor in modern American political discourse is the lineal descendant of this orthodoxy, surviving the gold standard that originally justified it because the rhetorical claim still does what Currency School orthodoxy always did: protects the real returns of those who hold government debt by foreclosing fiscal responses that might dilute them. For eighty years after 1844 the system seemed to vindicate itself. The orthodoxy settled between 1820 and 1850 would kill in 1929–1939; Part IV traces how that happened.
