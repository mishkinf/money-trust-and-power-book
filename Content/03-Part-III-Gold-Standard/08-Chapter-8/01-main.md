# PART III: THE GOLD STANDARD ERA AND ITS CONTRADICTIONS

## Chapter 8: The Great Forgetting

### The Man They Erased

On May 7, 1832, Thomas Attwood stood before the House of Commons making an argument that would get him dismissed as a crank. Britain's economic distress, he insisted, stemmed not from moral failing or foreign competition but from monetary contraction. He had watched it happen in Birmingham.

Attwood was no wild-eyed radical. Born in 1783 to a prosperous banking family, he had built a successful business manufacturing buttons and other metal goods in Birmingham—then England's industrial heart, second only to London in population and economic importance. His workers were skilled craftsmen, his business well-established, his social position secure. He was precisely the sort of respectable manufacturer whose opinion should have carried weight in Parliament.

Yet he had seen something that respectable economists—safely ensconced in London offices and university common rooms—refused to acknowledge. During the deflation that followed the 1821 return to gold, Attwood watched his workers starve—men who'd worked for him for years, whose skills were undiminished, whose dedication never wavered.

#### The Moment of Realization

During the years following the 1821 return to gold, Attwood witnessed the deflation's impact firsthand in his Birmingham manufactory. Skilled workers—men whose capabilities remained undiminished—found themselves unable to support their families not through any fault of their own but because the monetary system had contracted around them. The arithmetic was devastating: prices for buttons, buckles, and metal goods had fallen sharply, yet workers' families still needed to eat and landlords still demanded rent.

Attwood's customers couldn't afford to pay what his products had commanded during the war years, which meant he couldn't maintain wages at previous levels, which meant his workers' families faced deprivation despite their continued productivity. The mechanism was clear to him even if London's economists refused to see it: deflation had made nominal debt burdens heavier for everyone who owed money, while increasing the purchasing power of creditors. Workers weren't debtors in the formal sense, but they were trapped in the same mechanism—their labor was worth less in nominal terms even though their productivity was unchanged.

This wasn't laziness. This wasn't moral failing. Demand for Birmingham's products hadn't vanished—people still needed buttons, buckles, metal goods. The factories still stood. The workers still possessed their skills. But money had become scarce throughout Birmingham's industrial economy, and with it, the means to coordinate productive activity. The medium of exchange that connected all these elements had contracted, and with it the entire economy.

Attwood understood what David Ricardo and the Currency School did not: money was not neutral. Its quantity mattered for employment. When money contracted, real people suffered real deprivation—not because resources had disappeared but because the accounting system that coordinated economic activity had broken down. This was not a natural disaster like drought or plague. It was a policy choice, and it could be reversed through policy.

#### The Birmingham Rallies

Attwood didn't merely theorize—he organized. In 1829, he founded the Birmingham Political Union, which became one of the most powerful political organizations in industrial England. The rallies he convened demonstrated that working people and manufacturers could unite around monetary reform, creating a political force that terrified the establishment.

The scale of these gatherings was unprecedented for Birmingham. Contemporary accounts describe crowds of 40,000 workers and craftsmen—blacksmiths, button makers, metal workers, the skilled artisans who made Birmingham England's industrial powerhouse. These weren't desperate mobs but organized demonstrations of political power. The Birmingham Political Union could mobilize tens of thousands on short notice, demonstrating that monetary distress had created a constituency for radical reform.

The organizing required meticulous coordination. Mobilizing tens of thousands of workers, gathering over a million petition signatures, and sustaining a political movement for a decade demanded systematic infrastructure that Birmingham School leaders built through channels available to 19th-century political organizations. The movement grew not through demagoguery but through systematic education: workers understood they weren't suffering from personal failure but from policy choices that could be reversed.

Attwood didn't just write pamphlets—he organized the most successful mass political movement in British history. On December 14, 1829, he founded the Birmingham Political Union with fifteen others at the Royal Hotel, Birmingham. The first public meeting on January 25, 1830 at Beardsworth's Repository drew 10,000-15,000 people. By May 17, 1830, over 80,000 gathered. The crucial May 7, 1832 gathering on Newhall Hill during the "Days of May" constitutional crisis drew an unprecedented assembly. Contemporary newspaper estimates gave 150,000 to 200,000 — figures partisan accounts almost certainly inflated. Modern crowd-science analyses of the historic site footprint suggest the true gathering was closer to 50,000 to 100,000. Whichever number is right, no provincial city in British history had assembled anything like it, and the rally — coordinated with similar mobilizations in Manchester, Leeds, and London during the Days of May — decisively pressured the House of Lords during the constitutional crisis that produced the Reform Act.

The organization was sophisticated. A Political Council of thirty-six members collected dues of one penny per week, distributed reports to local and national press, and staged carefully orchestrated public meetings that emphasized "PEACE, LAW, ORDER, LOYALTY and UNION." By January 1831, membership reached 9,000 registered members paying weekly dues. The movement maintained strictly legal, non-violent methods throughout—a discipline that distinguished it from the Bristol riots of October 1831, where much of the city center burned with £300,000 in damage and up to 250 casualties.

The political success was spectacular. Earl Grey thanked Attwood "for all that he had done 'to maintain popular support for the measure outside the House of Commons.'" Lord Durham declared "the country owed Reform to Birmingham, and its salvation from revolution." The 1832 Reform Act passed, and Attwood was elected MP for Birmingham.

But the monetary reform failed completely. MPs grew "soon bored" of Attwood's currency theories. Benjamin Disraeli dismissed him as "a provincial banker labouring under a financial monomania." Working-class members wanted the vote, not currency expansion. As one historian noted, "It is doubtful whether the working classes of Birmingham were really concerned about currency at all, but they were concerned about the vote."

At a rally on May 7, 1832 — the same day Attwood would address Parliament — the Political Union assembled perhaps 40,000 people on Newhall Hill. The crowd stretched as far as observers could see. Attwood stood before them and made an argument Parliament would find unthinkable: Britain's economic distress stemmed from monetary contraction, not from moral failing or foreign competition. The 1821 return to gold had sharply contracted the money supply. The resulting deflation had destroyed workers' livelihoods not through any fault of their own but through a policy choice whose distributional consequences fell on debtors and wage-earners while conferring real gains on bondholders.

The voice he used was the legalist-radical register he had spent a decade developing — moral force packaged as constitutional discipline. Within the law the people were "strong as a giant," he told the rallies; outside it they were "weak as an infant." The Political Union's discipline reflected this: the meetings were organized, the conduct legal, the slogans uplifted into civic abstraction. The motto was "PEACE, LAW, ORDER, LOYALTY and UNION." A man capable of summoning 40,000 workers to a hillside was at the same time the man insisting they hold their political weight strictly within the bounds of legitimate constitutional pressure.

The establishment found these gatherings terrifying nonetheless. Forty thousand organized workers represented potential for revolution. The memory of France's upheavals was fresh — the Terror, Napoleon, the restored Bourbons overthrown again in 1830. Britain's ruling class feared that monetary distress might ignite similar chaos. The Birmingham Political Union's discipline made it more threatening, not less: this was not a riot that could be suppressed with soldiers, but a political movement that demanded response.

Attwood's message was clear and systematic:

- The 1821 return to gold had contracted the money supply sharply, causing deflation
- The deflation destroyed workers' livelihoods through monetary policy rather than moral failing
- Paper money could maintain full employment without gold backing, as the Bank Restriction period (1797–1821) had demonstrated
- Government should expand money supply to match productive capacity rather than constrain it to gold reserves
- The distributional consequences fell on debtors and wage-earners while conferring real gains on bondholders

The Birmingham Political Union's influence extended beyond monetary theory. By 1832, it had become a political force that Parliament could not ignore. The Reform Act of 1832—extending voting rights—passed partly due to pressure from Attwood's movement, which demonstrated that excluding working people from politics threatened civil order. Yet Attwood's monetary proposals, the core of his campaign, went nowhere.

#### The Chartist Petition

By 1839, depression had deepened across industrial Britain. Unemployment persisted. Wages remained suppressed. The promised recovery from 1821's deflation had never materialized for working people. Attwood helped draft the Chartist petition—a document demanding not just political reform but monetary expansion to relieve economic distress. What followed was an extraordinary mobilization.

1,280,958 signatures. The number represented perhaps 10% of Britain's adult population signing a single document. In an era before mass media or easy transportation, gathering this many signatures required tireless organizing across industrial England. Each signature meant: a local organizer explaining monetary policy to working people, convincing them that deflation wasn't their personal failure but policy choice, having a literate person record the name, coordinating across dozens of cities and hundreds of towns, physically transporting petition sheets to London.

The petition, when assembled, was physically enormous—thousands of pages bound together documenting mass support for monetary reform. Presenting it to Parliament required multiple men to carry the weight. This wasn't abstract political theory; it was the voice of over a million people demanding that policymakers recognize monetary contraction as the source of their suffering.

Yet when presented to Parliament on July 12, 1839, the petition was rejected 235 votes to 46 — a crushing defeat that demonstrated how thoroughly the political establishment had embraced Currency School orthodoxy. The vote wasn't close. Nearly five MPs voted against the petition for every one who supported it.

The parliamentary debates reveal how completely chartalist understanding had been erased. Opponents didn't engage Attwood's economic arguments—they dismissed him as deluded. There was no serious discussion of whether monetary contraction had caused unemployment, whether paper money could function without gold backing, or whether taxation created currency demand. These ideas, explicit just decades earlier when Adam Smith wrote about princes giving value to paper through taxation, had become unthinkable in respectable discourse. The 1,280,958 signatures might as well have been blank pages for all the consideration Parliament gave them.

#### The Dismissal: When Truth Becomes "Monomania"

The establishment's response was not to engage Attwood's arguments but to pathologize him. Benjamin Disraeli, the future Prime Minister, described him as "a provincial banker labouring under financial monomania"—an obsession with money that supposedly blinded him to economic reality. His followers were dismissed as "currency cranks" and "crude inflationists." The Birmingham School economists were excluded from serious academic discourse, their work treated as curiosities rather than legitimate economic theory.

Imagine the experience: You've built a successful business. You've watched skilled workers lose their livelihoods through no fault of their own. You've identified the precise mechanism causing this suffering—monetary contraction. You've articulated solutions that would work. You've mobilized over a million signatures demonstrating mass support. And Parliament's response is to declare you mentally unsound. "Monomania." Not wrong, not mistaken—*obsessed*. Psychologically damaged. Unfit for serious consideration.

This wasn't mere academic disagreement—it was character assassination masquerading as diagnosis. Economic journals ignored Birmingham School publications. Universities didn't teach their theories. When establishment economists mentioned Attwood at all, it was to ridicule him as an example of what happens when businessmen dabble in economic theory beyond their competence. His proto-Keynesian insights about the multiplier effect, his understanding that unemployment represented wasted capacity rather than natural equilibrium, his recognition that paper money could function without gold backing—all were buried under dismissive labels that questioned his sanity rather than his arguments.

The personal cost was real and calculable. From 1829 to 1839, Attwood devoted a decade to organizing the Birmingham Political Union and the Chartist movement. Hours that could have been spent building his business instead went to drafting pamphlets, coordinating rallies, traveling to meet with organizers across England, testifying before parliamentary committees. He made powerful enemies among the bondholding class—men who controlled access to credit, influenced government contracts, and shaped economic policy. His association with radical reform damaged his standing among Birmingham's commercial elite, some of whom distanced themselves from his "dangerous" ideas even as they privately recognized the suffering deflation caused.

The ridicule was personal and unrelenting. When Disraeli called him a "provincial banker labouring under financial monomania," it was not mere policy disagreement; it was a claim that Attwood's mental faculties had failed, that his preoccupation with monetary theory had become pathological. Economic journals ignored Birmingham School publications. Universities did not teach their theories. Whether the marginalization reflected a genuine intellectual judgment or the institutional weight of the metallist consensus is contested in the historiography; what is clear is that the contest was decided through dismissal rather than refutation.

And for what? To watch Parliament reject 1,280,958 signatures with barely a second thought. To see his economic ideas fade from respectable discourse and his name reduced — when it appeared at all — to that of a provincial crank who didn't understand real economics. Worse, to watch the suffering he'd tried to prevent continue for decades: the 1825–26 banking crisis, the agricultural distress of the 1830s, the industrial depressions that would recur through the century. Attwood displayed striking anticipations of later demand-side thinking — that money quantity matters for employment, that monetary contraction causes real suffering, that paper money can function without gold backing. He had identified the mechanism before economics had given that mechanism its modern name, and he had named the distributional consequences in a register Parliament could not unhear even when it chose not to act. The analytical apparatus that turned his intuition into a worked-out theory of demand management — the multiplier, the consumption function, the role of expectations — was not in his writings, and historians have repeatedly warned against reading him straightforwardly as a Keynesian before Keynes. He had been directionally right about money's non-neutrality, and the price he paid for being directionally right was the price the institutional contest extracted for political defeat. Being right about a mechanism was insufficient when the institutions that decided what counted as economics lay elsewhere.

On June 14, 1839, Attwood reluctantly presented the first Chartist petition with 1,280,958 signatures—nearly three miles long when sheets were joined end-to-end. On July 12, 1839, Parliament rejected it 235 votes to 46. Frustrated by both Parliament's indifference and the Chartists' disinterest in his monetary ideas, Attwood resigned from Parliament and left politics permanently. He died on March 6, 1856 as "an obscure figure" with "few obituaries."

The lesson was brutal: popular mobilization, however massive and disciplined, could achieve political reform but not monetary reform. Monetary policy was determined in Bank of England committee rooms and Chancellor advisory relationships — not in public meetings, however large, on a Birmingham hillside.

By the time Attwood died in 1856 at age 73, the forgetting was complete. Economic textbooks didn't mention him. The "Birmingham School" had become a footnote to serious economic thought, if it appeared at all. The ideas he had articulated — that money quantity matters for employment, that monetary contraction causes real suffering, that government could maintain full employment through monetary management — had become not merely wrong but unthinkable in respectable economics.

#### Vindication, Both Sides

The historiography has been generous to Attwood in retrospect, sometimes too generous. A century after his death, some economic historians described the Birmingham School's intuitions as anticipations of Keynesian thought. Asa Briggs's 1948 article on Attwood and the Birmingham Political Union noted "undoubted similarities of approach" between the Birmingham men and the demand-management economics that would emerge in the 1930s, while warning that "it would be unhistorical to call Attwood's analysis 'quasi-Keynesian.'" Henry Miller's 2012 reassessment of the Birmingham currency men in the House of Commons described them more accurately as "popular radicals" whose monetary program was a political project rather than a theoretical breakthrough. Attwood had identified the mechanism — that monetary contraction caused unemployment rather than reflecting it — and grasped that paper money could function without gold backing. The formal apparatus that turned that intuition into a full theory belonged to a later generation. He was directionally vindicated by the disasters of 1873–1896 and 1925–1931. The retrospective laurel of "proto-Keynesian," repeatedly placed on him in the twentieth century and repeatedly contested by historians who read his writings carefully, sits uneasily on the man's actual analytical work. The vindication was real. It was also smaller than the laurel implies.

The harder thing for a chapter that has just spent thirty pages on Attwood's case to say is that Peel and Overstone were also directionally vindicated. They were not simply running interference for bondholders. They had identified a real worry — one this book takes seriously enough to name in its preface: an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will, eventually, over-issue. Inconvertible paper had been allowed during the Restriction (1797–1821) because Britain was at war and no alternative was available. The post-war doctrine that money had to be tied to gold reflected the judgment that without that anchor, political pressure would push the Bank — and through the Bank the Treasury — toward the kind of debasement that French assignats had demonstrated in the 1790s and that the Restriction itself had partly exhibited. The Currency School's proposed mechanism — a hard rule binding note issuance to gold flows — was an attempt to remove monetary policy from the kind of political pressure that, on the historical record, had repeatedly produced inflation under fiscal duress.

The historical record in the long century and a half since 1844 has vindicated that worry repeatedly. Weimar Germany, 1921–23, produced an inflation that ran past 100 percent monthly for fourteen months and reached factors of a trillion before stabilization — the Reichsbank's discretion exercised under the fiscal pressure of treaty-imposed obligations, producing exactly the over-issuance Peel and Overstone had imagined. Hungary, 1945–46, set the largest hyperinflation on the historical record. Argentina between 1975 and 1991 ran four hyperinflationary episodes and ended in a currency-board attempt that was itself an institutional acknowledgement of the Currency School's diagnosis. Brazil, repeatedly through 1994. Zimbabwe, 2008. Venezuela, 2017. Turkey, 2021–24. In each case the structural setup matched what the Currency School had described: a state issuing its own currency, faced with fiscal pressure it could not meet through taxation or non-monetary borrowing, using the central bank to bridge the gap. In each case the eventual cost fell on wage-earners, fixed-pension retirees, and the holders of currency-denominated contracts — the same constituency that bore the cost of deflation under the gold standard, but transferred to them through the opposite mechanism. The worry was real then. It is real now.

What the Currency School got wrong was the *form* of the answer. A hard rule binding note issuance to gold flows did not anticipate deposits, did not provide the lender-of-last-resort flexibility that financial crises would soon require, and did not survive the suspensions of 1847, 1857, and 1866 — all of them within a generation of the Act's passage. The 1844 Act's failures across those crises are this chapter's later subject. But the failures were of a particular institutional design, not of the underlying worry. Both vindications stand together. Attwood was right that monetary contraction destroys real lives and that paper money can hold its value without gold; the Currency School was right that an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master needs institutional architecture to constrain it. The contest of 1820–1850 was a contest over which worry to architect against. The framework that won architected against the Currency School's worry and ignored Attwood's, and the choice carried distributional consequences whose direction is no longer in serious dispute even where the rightness of the policy choice still is. The institutional architectures Part IV examines — the rules-based tradition descending from public-choice economics, and the stabilizer-based tradition descending from the modern Modern Monetary Theory canon — are different attempts to address both worries: to combine the Bank Restriction's evidence that fiat can hold its value with the Currency School's insistence that institutional discipline is required to make it.

But being right about either worry was insufficient on its own to win the contest. The Currency School won not because its theoretical case was stronger than the Banking School's, but because the institutional contest was decided through channels where intellectual argument was only one of several inputs. How that contest was decided — through Parliamentary record, through banker correspondence, through the slow transformation of university teaching, through the patient multi-decade work of position-taking and gatekeeping — is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

### How the Forgetting Happened: Distributional Consequences and Institutional Power

The return to gold in 1821 was more than a technical monetary decision; it was the consolidation of a long political and intellectual contest about what money was for. Between 1800 and 1850, the earlier conception of money creation as a governance tool was displaced by the doctrine that taxes must fund government spending. The new doctrine tracked the material interests of those who held government debt — deflation raised the real value of their bond holdings — and the historiography has been arguing for a century about how much of that alignment was coincidence and how much was design. The distributional record itself is not contested.


The pattern is documented across decades: creditors realized gains, while debtors, wage-earners, and producers bore the deflation. Whether the policy framework that produced this pattern was the right one for the period — whether it was the necessary cost of monetary discipline or a distributional choice dressed as natural law — is precisely the question the contest between Attwood and Overstone was about, and that the chapter takes seriously rather than treating as already settled.

#### The Mechanics of Wealth Transfer

The distributional consequences of gold standard deflation followed a pattern so systematic it was almost mathematical. Consider the arithmetic from a creditor's perspective. A bondholder who lent £1,000 at 5 percent interest in 1820 expected to receive £1,050 in 1821—the principal plus contracted interest. Under normal circumstances, this would represent a 5 percent real return. But under the gold standard's deflationary pressure, something remarkable happened.

Between 1821 and 1824, the general price index fell approximately 20 percent. The bondholder still received £1,050, but that money could now purchase what £1,260 would have bought in 1820. The real return wasn't 5 percent—it was approximately 26 percent. The creditor gained an additional 21 percentage points through deflation alone, a windfall created not by productive activity but by monetary policy.

For debtors, the mechanics ran in brutal reverse. Consider Farmer Gigney, who borrowed £100 in 1820 when wheat sold for 74 shillings per quarter. He had expected to repay the debt by selling 27 quarters of wheat. By 1823 wheat had collapsed to 53 shillings per quarter, and the same £100 now required 38 quarters — a 41 percent increase in real burden, on a contract that had not changed by a word. The creditor, receiving £100 in 1823, could buy 41 percent more wheat than the day the loan was made. The transfer was not of money but of real purchasing power — of Gigney's labor, his land's productivity, his family's security. Workers faced a parallel squeeze: nominal wages proved sticky downward, so employers laid off rather than cut, and those who lost jobs found no unemployment insurance and inadequate charity behind them. Manufacturers like Attwood watched the squeeze close from both sides — selling prices fell, rents and debt service did not.

The scale of the wealth transfer was substantial. Britain's government debt stood at roughly £800 million in 1820. As a share of GDP the figure depends on which contemporary nominal-output series is used — Mitchell's *British Historical Statistics* and the Bank of England's reconstructed series yield ratios ranging from roughly 150 to 220 percent of GDP, with 200 percent toward the high end. On the central estimate, the 20 percent deflation between 1821 and 1824 increased the real value of the debt by something on the order of £160 million, transferred in real terms from those who worked to those who held paper claims on the state. The order of magnitude is robust across estimates; the precise figure is sensitive to which GDP series one trusts. Orthodox economics called the deflation sound money. Attwood called it theft through monetary policy. The arithmetic of debtor distress and creditor windfall favored his reading.

#### The Alternative They Rejected

Compare this to Thomas Attwood and the Birmingham School's proposal: maintain the paper currency established during the Bank Restriction (1797-1821), allow prices to stabilize at their current level, and adjust money supply to economic needs rather than gold constraints.

Under this system:
- Existing debts repaid at contracted rates without deflationary wealth transfer
- Workers face neither wage cuts nor unemployment spikes
- Government debt burden remains at nominal level rather than increasing in real terms
- Creditors receive exactly what they contracted for (5% interest) but not the additional 15-20% windfall from deflation

The orthodoxy's rhetoric obscured these distributional effects. Ricardo's Quantity Theory framed inflation as universally harmful, ignoring that mild inflation transfers wealth from creditors to debtors and productive entrepreneurs while deflation does the reverse. Say's Law claimed general gluts were impossible, denying that monetary contraction could cause systemic unemployment. The Currency School's focus on "automatic" gold adjustment portrayed the wealth transfer as natural market correction rather than policy choice.

When workers resisted wage cuts or debtors defaulted on loans whose real burden had doubled, orthodox economics blamed their moral failings—laziness, improvidence, speculation—rather than recognizing that monetary policy had systematically disadvantaged them.

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 embedded this framework in law, automating gold constraints and removing them from political contestation. The marginalization of Attwood and the Birmingham School cannot be reduced to technical economics — their analysis proved more accurate than Ricardo's in predicting depression and unemployment — and it cannot be reduced to power alone, either. It was a victory in a contest that combined intellectual conviction with institutional position, and the framework that won had distributional consequences whose direction is no longer in serious dispute even where the rightness of the policy choice still is.

#### The Pattern

Attwood's defeat illustrates how an economic orthodoxy consolidates when its conclusions track concentrated interests. The Napoleonic Wars had left Britain with £850 million in government debt — seventy times the 1700 level. Bondholders held the claims, and the political question was straightforward: repay in what? Paper at wartime prices, or gold at pre-war parity? The first answer favored debtors and producers; the second favored creditors. The choice was framed not as distributional but as technical — restoration of monetary order, automatic adjustment, neutral market forces. The human cost of the deflation entered the public record (Hansard, the Insolvent Debtor lists, Cobbett's *Rural Rides*) but not the dominant economic narrative. When dissenters pressed the point, they were treated not as wrong but as unserious: "financial monomania," "currency crank," "crude inflationist." The contest was decided through exclusion as much as through argument.

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 then embedded the orthodoxy in law, making gold constraints automatic and removing them from democratic contestation. Universities taught Currency School doctrine; prestigious professorships went to economists who accepted it; journals published papers supporting the conventional view and ignored the rest. By the 1850s a generation of economists had been trained to find what Attwood advocated not merely wrong but conceptually incoherent. The intellectual foundation William Paterson had articulated in 1694 — that the Bank created money out of nothing — had become unthinkable.

The framework was self-reinforcing. The 1825–26 banking crisis, the 1847 suspension of the Bank Charter Act, the recurring depressions of the late nineteenth century — each was read not as evidence against gold but as evidence that gold's discipline had not been applied rigorously enough. Attwood was directionally right about the mechanism and squarely right about the distribution. Being right was not enough.

---

The framework Attwood lost to has outlived by a century the gold standard that justified it. Modern political discourse across rich democracies runs on a household-budget metaphor — *we're broke*, *the national debt is out of control*, *we can't afford universal healthcare, universal childcare, climate investment*, *governments must balance the budget like a family* — that economic historians have spent fifty years showing is incoherent on its own terms. Households must acquire currency before they can spend it; a sovereign government that issues its own currency creates that currency by spending. The constraints on what each can do are not analogous, and the historians have made the case in detail. The metaphor has nevertheless persisted through eight decades of pure fiat money, longer than the metallic regime that originally justified it. What its persistence shows is the durability of intellectual frameworks once they have been built into institutions, journalism, and economic education. The Currency School's victory in 1844 still echoes — not because gold's defenders were necessarily correct in 1844, but because the institutions and the framework they established were robust enough to outlast the gold standard itself.

### The Parliamentary Triumph: The Bank Charter Act of 1844

#### Peel's Doctrine: Defining Money as Gold

Prime Minister Sir Robert Peel opened the debate on May 6, 1844, defining the pound as "a certain definite quantity of gold"—not an abstract unit, not a promise backed by taxation, but physical metal. This Currency School doctrine made alternatives literally unthinkable. What William Paterson stated in 1694—that the Bank creates money "out of nothing"—was now conceptually impossible. What Adam Smith wrote in 1776 about taxation giving value to paper money was forgotten.

Peel's system removed monetary policy from political contestation. The Bank would split into two departments. Note issuance above £14 million would be "governed in amount by the fluctuations in the stock of bullion" — automatically contracting when gold flowed out. No parliamentary debate, no democratic choice, no employment considerations. The deflationary mechanism delivered rising real bond values to creditors and squeezing nominal incomes to debtors and wage-earners; the architecture treated those distributional consequences as the automatic working of natural economic law rather than as political choices the framework had made on Britain's behalf.

The Act prohibited new banks from issuing notes, froze existing country banks' issuance at 1844 levels, and eliminated note-issuing rights upon merger. By economic attrition, private note issuance would disappear. Peel framed this as completing work begun in 1819, establishing continuity with the Resumption Act that had triggered devastating 1821 deflation. His political authority gave Currency School theory the weight of statesmanship.

#### The Banking School Opposition: Prophetic Warnings

In September 1843, James Wilson had founded *The Economist* magazine to attack the Currency School's rigid doctrines week after week, publishing sophisticated critiques of the proposed Act and warning that it would fail to control credit creation. Thomas Tooke and John Fullarton — the Banking School's leading theorists — made the same case in books and pamphlets. When Robert Torrens, a Currency School defender, eventually published his rebuttal in 1848, the title told the story: *The Principles and Practical Operation of Sir Robert Peel's Bill of 1844 Explained, and Defended Against the Objections of Tooke, Fullarton, and Wilson*. The intellectual opposition was real, prominent, and entirely unsuccessful.

**Benjamin Hawes**, Member of Parliament for Lambeth, led the parliamentary opposition on June 13, 1844, arguing there was no evidence to justify interfering with banks' note issuance. He challenged the Currency School's central claim head-on: where was the proof that convertible paper money caused high prices? After searching committee evidence and testimony, Hawes found no examples actually linking convertible bank notes to inflation. The Currency School was legislating based on theory, not evidence.

Most remarkably, Hawes predicted exactly what would happen — and was ignored. Restrict the legal currency below what the economy actually needs, he warned, and people will find alternatives: "the necessity for a circulating medium of some kind will prove too strong for your legislation, and will lead to a system of dealing in small bills and acceptances." This prediction proved devastatingly accurate. The Act was suspended during crises in 1847, 1857, and 1866. Credit found alternative forms — through bills of exchange and, most importantly, through deposits the Currency School failed to recognize as money.

**Mr. Hastie** warned of catastrophic collapse. **Charles Newdegate** spoke for agricultural interests: price reductions from currency contraction hit workers hardest — "a sheer loss without the shadow of an equivalent."

#### What Wasn't Debated

Remarkably, no MP cited Adam Smith's 1776 insight about taxation giving value to paper money. No one referenced the Bank Restriction period (1797–1821) as evidence paper money worked without gold — except Hawes, who was promptly ignored. The idea Paterson articulated in 1694 — that the Bank creates money "out of nothing" — was now unspeakable. Even the Banking School accepted the Currency School's premise that gold backing was necessary. The chartalist understanding had been so thoroughly erased it didn't appear as an option to reject.

Though no division lists survive, the debates make clear the bill enjoyed overwhelming support. Opposition speakers acknowledged their minority status. Chancellor Henry Goulburn noted the measure "had consequently met with the concurrence" of those who heard all objections. On July 19, 1844, the Bank Charter Act received Royal Assent. The Currency School had won not by silencing opposition but by controlling the institution that translated theory into law.

### How the Currency School Won

In February 1853, Lord Overstone sat at his writing desk and drafted a letter to George Warde Norman about the Political Economy Club. "Tooke and some followers of his are getting an undue predominance in the Club," he wrote — Thomas Tooke, the Banking School theorist whose 1838 *History of Prices* had been the strongest empirical case against Currency School orthodoxy. "They will ruin it if we do not take care what we are about. The election of new Members at our next meeting is a vital matter."

The letter was the institutional contest in miniature. The Political Economy Club, founded in 1821, was the room where serious economic discourse in London was made. Membership was elected. Members decided which papers got read aloud, which speakers got invited, which framings were treated as the consensus position. Overstone, sitting at the center of that network, treated the Club's next election the way a chief whip treats a vote: as the kind of small, low-visibility decision that determined which arguments would, twenty years later, count as economics.

The pattern repeated across every institution that mattered. **Samuel Jones Loyd** (1796–1883) — Lord Overstone after 1850 — was a banker with a personal fortune of £2 million in the 1850s, equivalent to roughly £3 billion today. Though he never sat in the Commons, his 1837 *Reflections Suggested by a Perusal of Mr. J. Horsley Palmer's Pamphlet*, his 1840 *Remarks on the Management of the Circulation*, and his 1844 *Thoughts on the Separation of the Departments of the Bank of England* were the analytical scaffolding the Bank Charter Act was built on. An 1857 parliamentary debate explicitly acknowledged his authorship: "The noble Lord opposite (Lord Overstone) one of its chief promoters, and generally supposed to be its author." When Sir Robert Peel, then Prime Minister designate, heard testimony before the Select Committee on Banks of Issue in 1840, he sat through five of Overstone's six days of evidence. Peel converted to Currency School thinking through that Parliamentary testimony — not through personal meetings. Overstone himself stated in 1857 that he had "no connexion, political or social with Sir Robert Peel" and "never exchanged one word upon the subject of this Act" with him directly. Loyd did not need parliamentary office. The institutional channels — Select Committee evidence, written analysis, the published record — were enough.

**George Warde Norman**, Overstone's closest friend, served as Bank of England Director for fifty-one years (1821–1872) and sat on the Committee of Treasury, providing institutional continuity for Currency School principles across two generations. The Norman family carried that continuity longer still. Norman's grandson Montagu Norman served as Bank of England Governor from 1920 to 1944. The Norman family carried Currency School principles inside the institutions that mattered for 123 years — from 1821 to 1944. The mechanism was not pure intellectual persuasion; it was the slower work of institutional position, family continuity, and the network effects that come with both.

Overstone maintained direct advisory relationships with successive Chancellors of the Exchequer. Sir Charles Wood wrote to him in May 1847: "you are 'the person to whom we are really indebted for the Act of 1844.'" William Gladstone praised his analysis as "the most Socratic document of modern times." Lord John Russell consulted him on suitability of industrialists for elevation to the House of Lords and expected him to advise the Leader of the House of Lords on banking questions. Francis Jeffrey, editor of the *Edinburgh Review*, asked Overstone's advice on "the respective merits of McCulloch, Torrens, and Mill as authors for definitive article on currency," giving him editorial influence over the journal that, more than any other, defined respectable economic opinion in London. Overstone himself published influential letters in *The Times* under the pen name "Mercator." Bank of England Directors testified in Parliament aligned with Currency School recommendations. Each of these channels, individually, would have been a useful asset. Together they were a closed loop.

The Currency School's dominance extended into the universities. Nassau William Senior held Oxford's Drummond Professorship during the Currency School's rise (1825–30) and again through its legislative triumph (1847–52); his lectures and writings naturalized the gold-standard premise as the starting point of monetary economics rather than as one position among several. John Stuart Mill's *Principles of Political Economy* (1848) became the standard textbook in the English-speaking world, running through seven editions until 1919, shaping what two generations of economics students were taught to take for granted. Mill engaged both schools fairly, but he accepted the gold standard premise; the textbook that introduced economics to the second half of the nineteenth century gave the Currency School its starting framing. By 1850, dissenting views had been excluded from the journals and societies that constituted respectable economic discourse, and the textbook generation was being trained to find Attwood's claims not merely wrong but conceptually incoherent.

The Banking School lost the institutional battle completely. Despite including prestigious economists — Thomas Tooke, John Fullarton, John Stuart Mill in his more cautious moments — and despite James Wilson founding *The Economist* in 1843 explicitly to oppose the Bank Charter Act, no Banking School advocates were identified as Bank of England Directors during the critical period. The Political Economy Club tilted Currency School. The Drummond Professorship and the Mill textbook did. The *Edinburgh Review*, *The Times*, the Treasury, the Bank's Committee of Treasury, the parliamentary committees, the chairmanships of the Bank itself — all of them tilted the same way. The contest was decided not through silencing, but through the patient capture of the institutions that translated theory into law and law into orthodoxy.

### Peel's tragic error: the birth of modern deposit banking

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 represents one of history's great unintended consequences. Peel crafted legislation to end fractional reserve banking. He succeeded in destroying private note issuance. He utterly failed to control credit creation.

The Act prohibited new banks from issuing notes, froze existing country banks' issuance at 1844 levels, and mandated banks lose note-issuing rights upon merger. In 1844, 277 country banks issued £8.6 million in notes. By 1921, Fox, Fowler and Company issued the last private English banknote — a 77-year transition to Bank of England monopoly.

But the Currency School made a tragic error: they assumed bank deposits weren't money. They focused obsessively on paper notes while ignoring checking accounts. Banks immediately pivoted. As monetary historian Charles Goodhart writes, "Both the Bank of England and the country banks, deprived of the right to issue notes at will, began to issue deposits to a fare-thee-well." Fractional reserve banking didn't end — it simply changed to demand deposits. Peel's Currency School had tried to lock water in a container by welding shut one valve while leaving five other valves wide open. Money creation simply flowed through the deposit-creation valves instead. By 1900, 99.91 percent of transactions used deposits rather than notes. The Act meticulously controlled something that had become irrelevant while accidentally authorizing the very thing it was designed to prevent: unconstrained private money creation.

*(See Figure 8.1: Notes versus Deposits — functionally identical money. The 1844 Act regulated paper notes; deposit creation operated unconstrained. By 1900 deposits represented 99.91 percent of transactions by value. The Act had meticulously controlled the form of money that became irrelevant while ignoring the form that came to dominate.)*

Even brilliant economists missed this. John Stuart Mill (1848) denied credit could "make something out of nothing" — yet loans literally create deposits. Walter Bagehot (1873) believed "banks cannot create money." Not until Keynes's *Treatise on Money* (1930) was the truth stated explicitly: "banks can safely create" money "provided they move forward in step." But even Keynes retreated in his 1936 *General Theory*. The full articulation awaited Post-Keynesian economists — Basil Moore's 1988 *Horizontalists and Verticalists* finally explained: "loans create deposits," not the reverse. Why did it take so long? Deposit creation was invisible. When a country bank printed a £5 note, everyone could see new money. When a bank created a £5 deposit by writing in a ledger, nothing physical changed.

The United States followed the same trajectory. The National Banking Acts of 1863–1864 imposed a 10 percent tax on state bank notes, making their issuance uneconomical, and state bank note circulation fell from $143 million in 1865 to $4 million by 1867 — effectively eliminated through punitive taxation. But state banks discovered the same workaround as English banks: deposits. By the 1890s, 90 percent of the U.S. money supply was in checking accounts. Individual deposits grew from 5 percent to 23 percent of GDP between 1863 and 1913. What emerged on both sides of the Atlantic was the modern two-tier monetary system: central-bank base money at the foundation, with commercial banks creating deposit money on top. The hierarchy that had existed informally when country banks pyramided notes on Bank of England reserves was now formalized. The Act designed to establish state control over money creation had instead driven that creation underground into deposit banking, where it would operate with even less oversight for the next century.

#### Samuel Gurney's October crisis

**Samuel Gurney** sat before the Parliamentary Committee in late 1847, his Quaker dress marking him as a member of Norfolk's most prominent banking family. The October crisis had tested the Bank Charter Act's iron restrictions in ways Robert Peel never anticipated. When panic swept London, depositors at Overend, Gurney & Company — the "bankers' banker" serving country banks across England — demanded their money. The Act's rigid cap on note issuance meant Gurney couldn't expand circulation to meet the panic, even with £8 million in deposits backing his operations. The crisis peaked on October 25th. Then the government suspended the Act.

"Those who had sent notice for their money in the morning," Gurney testified, "sent us word that they did not [need it]." The panic that the Act's restrictions had inflamed vanished the moment those restrictions lifted. Gurney understood what Peel's elegant theory had missed: in moments of crisis, confidence mattered more than currency ratios.

### Colonial proof: taxation drives money through force

While 19th-century orthodoxy denied that taxation drives currency adoption, colonial administrators demonstrated this principle empirically through systematic coercion. British colonial hut taxes were explicitly designed to force currency adoption. By the 1890s–1910s, Kenya, Uganda, Rhodesia, and other colonies implemented hut or poll taxes payable only in colonial currency. Kenya's 1901 Hut Tax Ordinance required 2–3 rupees per hut annually. Before 1901, Kenyan communities used cattle wealth and barter. The tax forced families to sell to settlers for rupees or send members to wage labor on colonial plantations.

The empirical sequence proved chartalism: before tax, British currency circulated among colonists but Africans didn't adopt it; after tax was imposed, currency adoption was forced legally. Tax obligation created currency demand. The irony was complete: while London economists denied taxation drives currency value, colonial administrators proved it through documented coercion.

---

The 1821 deflation had caused unnecessary suffering, destroyed livelihoods, and demonstrated that returning to gold at the old parity imposed arbitrary costs on debtors. Yet this lesson would be forgotten. A century later, Britain would repeat the mistake with even more devastating consequences — when Winston Churchill's 1925 return to gold at an overvalued parity would trigger mass unemployment, a general strike, and contribute to the global catastrophe that followed.

The gold standard had reached its intellectual apex. What William Paterson had stated plainly in 1694 — that the Bank of England created money out of nothing — was now unthinkable in respectable economics. The institutional reality of money creation had been buried beneath the surface of gold orthodoxy. But that success contained the seeds of its own destruction.

For eighty years after 1844 the system seemed to vindicate itself. Trade expanded, empires grew, London became the financial capital of the world. The mechanism appeared automatic. In fact it worked because the Bank of England exercised discretionary management behind the Act's rigid rules, because no major war tested the system, because South African and Alaskan gold discoveries kept the supply growing, and because Britain extracted from its colonies what its trade balances could not provide. When war came in August 1914 every combatant abandoned the gold standard within days, and the system that had seemed eternal collapsed in a week.

The orthodoxy that survived would face its real test in the 1920s and 1930s — when Churchill's return to gold, France's gold hoarding, and the Federal Reserve's adherence to Real Bills Doctrine would turn a financial panic into a global depression. The human cost of those choices would be measured not in thousands but in millions. The orthodoxy settled between 1820 and 1850 would kill in 1929–1939. Part IV traces how that happened.
