# PART III: THE GOLD STANDARD ERA AND ITS CONTRADICTIONS

## Chapter 8: The Great Forgetting

Here, for the first time, the book's pattern of paired vindication appears in full. Thomas Attwood saw the cruelty of monetary contraction from Birmingham; Peel, Overstone, and the Currency School saw the danger of unconstrained paper under fiscal pressure. The tragedy of the nineteenth-century settlement is not that one side was simply right and the other simply wrong. It is that Britain built an architecture to prevent one real danger while making another real danger look like nature.

### The Man They Erased

On May 7, 1832, Thomas Attwood stood before the House of Commons making an argument that would get him dismissed as a crank. Britain's economic distress, he insisted, stemmed not simply from moral failing or foreign competition but from monetary contraction. He had watched it happen in Birmingham.

Attwood was no wild-eyed radical. Born in 1783 to a prosperous Birmingham banking family, he belonged to the city's respectable commercial world — the world of banks, workshops, metal trades, and skilled artisans that made Birmingham England's great provincial industrial center. His social position was secure, his political manner legalist, his economic commitments those of a man who watched credit conditions move through the lives of working people. He was precisely the sort of respectable provincial banker whose opinion should have carried weight in Parliament.

Yet he had seen something that respectable economists—safely ensconced in London offices and university common rooms—refused to acknowledge. During the deflation that followed the 1821 return to gold, Attwood watched Birmingham's working families suffer as orders weakened, prices fell, debts grew heavier, and skilled labor that had been fully employable during the war years was suddenly treated as surplus.

#### The Moment of Realization

During the years following the 1821 return to gold, Attwood watched the deflation pass through Birmingham's industrial economy. Skilled workers whose capabilities remained intact found themselves unable to support their families because the monetary system had contracted around them. Manufacturers could not obtain wartime prices for goods, wages came under pressure, and debts grew heavier in real terms. Workers were not usually debtors in the formal sense, but they were caught in the same mechanism: their labor commanded fewer pounds even when their productivity had not fallen.

That was the insight Attwood carried into politics. The factories still stood. The workers still possessed their skills. But money had become scarce throughout Birmingham's economy, and with it the means to coordinate productive activity. This was not drought or plague. It was policy, and policy could be reversed.

#### The Birmingham Rallies

Attwood did not merely theorize. In 1829, he founded the Birmingham Political Union, one of the most powerful political organizations in industrial England. Its public meetings drew tens of thousands of workers and craftsmen — blacksmiths, button makers, metal workers, the skilled artisans who made Birmingham an industrial powerhouse. The crucial Newhall Hill gathering of May 7, 1832, during the "Days of May" constitutional crisis, was reported by partisan newspapers at 150,000 to 200,000 and is more defensibly bounded by modern estimates somewhere below that, perhaps 50,000 to 100,000. The exact number matters less than the political fact: no provincial city had assembled anything like it, and the House of Lords knew it.

The organization was sophisticated. A Political Council of thirty-six members collected dues of one penny per week, distributed reports to the press, and staged meetings under the motto "PEACE, LAW, ORDER, LOYALTY and UNION." Attwood's register was legalist-radical: lawful organization made the people powerful; disorder made them politically weak. The discipline made the movement more threatening, not less. This was not a riot that could be dispersed with cavalry. It was a constitutional force asking Parliament to admit that monetary policy had distributional consequences.

The political success was spectacular. Earl Grey thanked Attwood for maintaining popular support outside the Commons; Lord Durham declared that "the country owed Reform to Birmingham, and its salvation from revolution." The 1832 Reform Act passed, and Attwood was elected MP for Birmingham. But the monetary reform failed. MPs grew "soon bored" of his currency theories. Disraeli dismissed him as "a provincial banker labouring under a financial monomania." Working-class members wanted the vote more than currency expansion. Attwood had built a movement large enough to help win parliamentary reform and not large enough to make Parliament rethink money.

#### The Chartist Petition

By 1839, depression had deepened across industrial Britain. Unemployment persisted, wages remained suppressed, and the recovery promised after the 1821 return to gold had never arrived for working people. Attwood helped draft the first Chartist petition, which demanded not only political reform but monetary expansion to relieve economic distress. It carried 1,280,958 signatures, perhaps 10 percent of Britain's adult population. When assembled, the petition was nearly three miles long if the sheets were joined end to end. It required multiple men to carry it into Parliament.

On July 12, 1839, Parliament rejected it 235 votes to 46. The vote was not close because the monetary argument was not allowed to become close. There was no serious parliamentary reckoning with whether monetary contraction had caused unemployment, whether paper money could function without gold backing, or whether taxes could create demand for state money. Ideas that had been visible in Adam Smith's own discussion of tax receivability had become unthinkable in respectable monetary debate.

Attwood resigned from Parliament and left politics permanently. The decade had cost him social standing, political capital, and much of his public authority. More important for the history of money, it left him as a warning about what defeat can do to an idea. He had watched skilled workers lose livelihoods through monetary contraction, named the mechanism, and organized more than a million signatures behind a remedy. The establishment did not mainly refute him. It diagnosed him. "Financial monomania" did the work an argument would otherwise have had to do.

The forgetting was not total, and it was not conspiratorial. Banking School writers, radicals, and practical bankers kept pieces of the critique alive. But Attwood's specific argument — that money quantity mattered for employment, that contraction could cause real suffering, and that paper money could work without gold — lost the right to count as respectable economics. The lesson was brutal: popular mobilization could achieve parliamentary reform, but monetary architecture was decided in Bank of England committee rooms, Treasury consultations, and the channels through which a theory became administratively usable.

#### The Paired Vindication

The historiography has been generous to Attwood in retrospect, sometimes too generous. A century after his death, some economic historians described the Birmingham School's intuitions as anticipations of Keynesian thought. Asa Briggs noted "undoubted similarities of approach" between the Birmingham men and later demand-management economics, while warning that "it would be unhistorical to call Attwood's analysis 'quasi-Keynesian.'" Henry Miller's later reassessment described the Birmingham currency men more accurately as "popular radicals" whose monetary program was a political project rather than a theoretical breakthrough. Attwood had identified the mechanism — monetary contraction could cause unemployment rather than merely reflect it — and grasped that paper money could function without gold backing. His vindication was real. It was also smaller than the laurel implies.

That is why he matters to this book. Attwood is not useful because he secretly solved twentieth-century macroeconomics in advance. He is useful because he saw the question that later monetary politics would keep disguising: when a regime chooses price credibility, external confidence, or creditor protection, whose unused capacity and unemployment become the price of that choice?

The harder thing to say is that Peel and Overstone were also directionally vindicated — vindicated about a worry, not an answer. They were not simply running interference for bondholders. They had identified a danger every fiat regime has to face: an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master can over-issue. Inconvertible paper had been allowed during the Restriction because Britain was at war and no alternative was available. The post-war doctrine tying money to gold reflected the fear that without an anchor, political pressure would push the Bank and Treasury toward debasement.

That fear had democratic force as well as creditor force. Inflation can destroy wages between paydays, pensions between quarterly payments, savings accumulated over years, and the ability of ordinary families to know what tomorrow's money will buy. A politics that can solve conflicts by issuing more paper may avoid open taxation while imposing a hidden one.

The tragedy is that the Currency School treated one hidden tax as intolerable and another as discipline. Inflation can tax creditors and wage-earners through depreciation. Deflation can tax debtors and workers through unemployment and rising real burdens. The Currency School saw the first danger with moral clarity and the second through a haze of necessity.

The long record since 1844 has vindicated the worry repeatedly: Weimar, Hungary, Argentina, Brazil, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Turkey. In each case a state facing fiscal pressure it could not meet through taxation or non-monetary borrowing used the central bank to bridge the gap. In each case the eventual cost fell on wage-earners, fixed-pension retirees, and holders of currency-denominated contracts. The worry was real then. It is real now.

What the Currency School got wrong was the form of the answer. A hard rule binding note issuance to gold flows did not anticipate deposits, did not provide lender-of-last-resort flexibility, and did not survive the suspensions of 1847, 1857, and 1866. Attwood was directionally right that monetary contraction destroys real lives and that paper money can hold its value without gold. Peel and Overstone were directionally right that discretionary issuance requires institutional constraint. The contest of 1820–1850 was not over which side possessed all the truth. It was over which real danger the system would be built to prevent. The framework that won architected against over-issue while treating deflation's casualties as the cost of discipline.

But being right about either worry was insufficient on its own to win the contest. The Currency School won not because its theoretical case was stronger than the Banking School's, but because the institutional contest was decided through channels where intellectual argument was only one of several inputs. The rest of the chapter follows Attwood's defeat through those channels: Parliament, banker correspondence, administrative usefulness, and the slow conversion of one theory into law.

### How the Forgetting Happened: Distributional Consequences and Institutional Power

The return to gold in 1821 was more than a technical monetary decision; it was the consolidation of a long political and intellectual contest about what money was for. Between 1800 and 1850, the older language of money creation as a governance tool was pushed out of respectable policy argument by the doctrine that taxes must fund government spending and that paper should be tied tightly to metal. The new doctrine tracked the material interests of those who held government debt — deflation raised the real value of their bond holdings — and the historiography has been arguing for a century about how much of that alignment was coincidence and how much was design. The distributional record itself is clearer: creditors gained while debtors, wage-earners, and producers bore the deflation.

#### The Mechanics of Wealth Transfer

The distributional consequences of gold standard deflation followed a pattern so systematic it was almost mathematical. Consider the arithmetic from a creditor's perspective. A bondholder who lent £1,000 at 5 percent interest in 1820 expected to receive £1,050 in 1821—the principal plus contracted interest. Under normal circumstances, this would represent a 5 percent real return. But under the gold standard's deflationary pressure, something remarkable happened.

Between 1821 and 1824, the general price index fell approximately 20 percent. The bondholder still received £1,050, but that money could now purchase what £1,260 would have bought in 1820. The real return wasn't 5 percent—it was approximately 26 percent. The creditor gained an additional 21 percentage points through deflation alone, a windfall created not by productive activity but by monetary policy.

For debtors, the mechanics ran in brutal reverse. Consider Farmer Gigney, who borrowed £100 in 1820 when wheat sold for 74 shillings per quarter. He had expected to repay the debt by selling 27 quarters of wheat. By 1823 wheat had collapsed to 53 shillings per quarter, and the same £100 now required 38 quarters — a 41 percent increase in real burden, on a contract that had not changed by a word. The creditor, receiving £100 in 1823, could buy 41 percent more wheat than the day the loan was made. The transfer was not of money but of real purchasing power — of Gigney's labor, his land's productivity, his family's security. Workers faced a parallel squeeze: nominal wages proved sticky downward, so employers laid off rather than cut, and those who lost jobs found no unemployment insurance and inadequate charity behind them. Manufacturers around Attwood watched the squeeze close from both sides — selling prices fell, rents and debt service did not.

The scale of the wealth transfer was substantial. Britain's government debt stood at roughly £800 million in 1820. As a share of GDP the figure depends on which contemporary nominal-output series is used — Mitchell's *British Historical Statistics* and the Bank of England's reconstructed series yield ratios ranging from roughly 150 to 220 percent of GDP, with 200 percent toward the high end. On the central estimate, the 20 percent deflation between 1821 and 1824 increased the real value of the debt by something on the order of £160 million, transferred in real terms from those who worked to those who held paper claims on the state. The order of magnitude is robust across estimates; the precise figure is sensitive to which GDP series one trusts. Orthodox economics called the deflation sound money. Attwood called it theft through monetary policy. The arithmetic of debtor distress and creditor windfall favored his reading.

#### The Alternative They Rejected

Attwood and the Birmingham School proposed the obvious alternative: maintain the paper currency established during the Bank Restriction, stabilize prices near their wartime level, and adjust the money supply to economic need rather than gold flows. Existing debts would be repaid at contracted rates without an added deflationary transfer. Creditors would receive what they had contracted for, not a windfall created by policy.

The orthodoxy's rhetoric obscured that distributional choice. Ricardo's Quantity Theory framed inflation as universally harmful, while Say's Law denied that monetary contraction could cause systemic unemployment. The Currency School's language of automatic gold adjustment made the wealth transfer look like market correction rather than policy.

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 embedded this framework in law, automating gold constraints and removing them from political contestation. The marginalization of Attwood and the Birmingham School cannot be reduced to technical economics — their analysis proved more accurate than Ricardo's in predicting depression and unemployment — and it cannot be reduced to power alone, either. It was a victory in a contest that combined intellectual conviction with institutional position, and the framework that won settled the direction of the wealth transfer in ways the historical record has spent a century and a half cataloguing.

#### The Pattern

Attwood's defeat illustrates how an economic orthodoxy consolidates when its conclusions track concentrated interests. The Napoleonic Wars had left Britain with £850 million in government debt. Bondholders held the claims, and the political question was straightforward: repay in paper at wartime prices, or in gold at pre-war parity? The first answer favored debtors and producers; the second favored creditors. The choice was framed not as distributional but as technical — restoration of monetary order, automatic adjustment, neutral market forces.

The human cost entered the public record in Hansard, the Insolvent Debtor lists, and Cobbett's *Rural Rides*, but not the dominant economic narrative. When dissenters pressed the point, they were treated not as wrong but as unserious: "financial monomania," "currency crank," "crude inflationist." James Wilson's *Economist*, Tooke, Fullarton, and Hawes all objected; the contest was real. But the decisive channel was Parliament, the Treasury, and the Bank. There the Currency School's premise became administratively real.

The framework was self-reinforcing. The 1825–26 banking crisis, the 1847 suspension of the Bank Charter Act, and the recurring depressions of the late nineteenth century were read not as evidence against gold but as evidence that gold's discipline had not been applied rigorously enough. Attwood was directionally right about the mechanism and squarely right about the distribution. Being right was not enough.

---

The framework Attwood lost to has outlived the gold standard that justified it. Modern political discourse across rich democracies still runs on household-budget language — *we're broke*, *the national debt is out of control*, *governments must balance the budget like a family* — even after decades of pure fiat money. What persists is not gold itself, but the habit gold helped train: the assumption that public money must be made to feel scarce before political choices can be treated as responsible.

Attwood's afterlife is therefore not a matter of intellectual genealogy alone. He is the book's first modern witness to the capacity question. The Birmingham workers he defended had not lost their skills; the factories had not vanished; the community had not run out of useful things to do. The monetary regime had made coordination fail and then called the resulting unemployment discipline. That pattern will return in Churchill's coalfields, in Volcker's farm belt, and in Japan's employment ice age. Each later case differs in cause and institution. The recurring question is Attwood's: when a society has the people and tools to produce more, what kind of monetary order turns that capacity into work, and what kind strands it idle?

### The Parliamentary Triumph: The Bank Charter Act of 1844

#### Peel's Doctrine: Defining Money as Gold

Prime Minister Sir Robert Peel opened the debate on May 6, 1844, defining the pound as "a certain definite quantity of gold"—not an abstract unit, not a promise backed by taxation, but physical metal. This Currency School doctrine narrowed the field of respectable argument. What William Paterson stated in 1694 — that the Bank creates money "out of nothing" — could no longer be said plainly in Parliament. What Adam Smith wrote in 1776 about taxation giving value to paper money remained in the literature, but it no longer supplied the operative vocabulary of policy.

Peel's system removed monetary policy from political contestation. The Bank would split into two departments. Note issuance above £14 million would be "governed in amount by the fluctuations in the stock of bullion" — automatically contracting when gold flowed out. No parliamentary debate, no democratic choice, no employment considerations. The deflationary mechanism delivered rising real bond values to creditors and squeezing nominal incomes to debtors and wage-earners; the architecture treated those distributional consequences as the automatic working of natural economic law rather than as political choices the framework had made on Britain's behalf.

The Act prohibited new banks from issuing notes, froze existing country banks' issuance at 1844 levels, and eliminated note-issuing rights upon merger. By economic attrition, private note issuance would disappear. Peel framed this as completing work begun in 1819, establishing continuity with the Resumption Act that had triggered devastating 1821 deflation. His political authority gave Currency School theory the weight of statesmanship.

#### The Banking School Opposition: Prophetic Warnings

In September 1843, James Wilson had founded *The Economist* magazine to attack the Currency School's rigid doctrines week after week, publishing sophisticated critiques of the proposed Act and warning that it would fail to control credit creation. Thomas Tooke and John Fullarton — the Banking School's leading theorists — made the same case in books and pamphlets. When Robert Torrens, a Currency School defender, eventually published his rebuttal in 1848, the title told the story: *The Principles and Practical Operation of Sir Robert Peel's Bill of 1844 Explained, and Defended Against the Objections of Tooke, Fullarton, and Wilson*. The intellectual opposition was real, prominent, and entirely unsuccessful.

Benjamin Hawes, Member of Parliament for Lambeth, led the parliamentary opposition on June 13, 1844, arguing there was no evidence to justify interfering with banks' note issuance. He challenged the Currency School's central claim head-on: where was the proof that convertible paper money caused high prices? After searching committee evidence and testimony, Hawes found no examples actually linking convertible bank notes to inflation. The Currency School was legislating based on theory, not evidence.

Most remarkably, Hawes predicted exactly what would happen — and was ignored. Restrict the legal currency below what the economy actually needs, he warned, and people will find alternatives: "the necessity for a circulating medium of some kind will prove too strong for your legislation, and will lead to a system of dealing in small bills and acceptances." This prediction proved devastatingly accurate. The Act was suspended during crises in 1847, 1857, and 1866. Credit found alternative forms — through bills of exchange and, most importantly, through deposits the Currency School failed to recognize as money.

Mr. Hastie warned of catastrophic collapse. Charles Newdegate spoke for agricultural interests: price reductions from currency contraction hit workers hardest — "a sheer loss without the shadow of an equivalent."

#### What Wasn't Debated

Remarkably, no MP cited Adam Smith's 1776 insight about taxation giving value to paper money. No one referenced the Bank Restriction period (1797–1821) as evidence paper money worked without gold — except Hawes, who was promptly ignored. The idea Paterson articulated in 1694 — that the Bank creates money "out of nothing" — was now unspeakable. Even the Banking School accepted the Currency School's premise that gold backing was necessary. The chartalist understanding had been so thoroughly erased it didn't appear as an option to reject.

Though no division lists survive, the debates make clear the bill enjoyed overwhelming support. Opposition speakers acknowledged their minority status. Chancellor Henry Goulburn noted the measure "had consequently met with the concurrence" of those who heard all objections. On July 19, 1844, the Bank Charter Act received Royal Assent. The Currency School had won not by silencing opposition but by controlling the institution that translated theory into law.

### How the Currency School Won

The Currency School did not win by controlling every room in which monetary ideas were debated. That would be too simple and, on the evidence, wrong. James Wilson founded *The Economist* in 1843 and used it to attack the Bank Charter Act; Tooke and Fullarton published the Banking School case; Hawes made the parliamentary critique in language that now looks prophetic. The opposition was real, literate, and visible.

The Currency School won because it controlled enough of the decisive channel. Samuel Jones Loyd — Lord Overstone after 1850 — never sat in the Commons, but his 1837, 1840, and 1844 pamphlets supplied the analytical scaffolding for the Act, and later parliamentary debate acknowledged him as one of its chief promoters and supposed authors. Peel sat through five of Loyd's six days of testimony before the 1840 Select Committee on Banks of Issue. George Warde Norman, Overstone's closest friend, served as Bank of England Director for fifty-one years and sat on the Committee of Treasury. Chancellor Sir Charles Wood wrote to Overstone in May 1847 that he was "the person to whom we are really indebted for the Act of 1844."

The precision matters. The Currency School did not capture the commercial press; James Wilson's *Economist* was born as a free-trade paper and became one of the most visible critics of Peel's Act. It did not silence the Banking School; Tooke, Fullarton, Hawes, and Wilson left a thick printed record of objection. It did not monopolize all academic teaching; the evidence for deliberate curriculum capture at Oxford and Cambridge is thinner than polemic would like. What it captured was more practical: the confidence of the men who could write a bill, defend it as statesmanship, and make the Bank's operating problem look soluble through a rule.

This was how institutional power often works: a theory becomes useful to the people positioned to legislate, administer, and defend it; the theory's opponents remain audible but not decisive; and the resulting law then trains future debate by making one answer the baseline. The Banking School was not silenced. It lost the channel that mattered most. Once the Act was law, each later crisis could be interpreted as a reason to suspend the rule temporarily while leaving the rule's authority intact.

The Act also gave the Currency School a retrospective advantage. Before 1844, the argument could still be fought as theory against theory: what counts as money, what causes crises, how much discretion the Bank should retain. After 1844, the argument had a statute behind it. Officials, journalists, bankers, and later economists had to begin from the law's categories even when they criticized them. Issue and banking departments; notes and deposits; bullion rule and emergency suspension — the vocabulary of the Act became the vocabulary of the debate.

### Peel's Error: Deposits

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 represents one of history's great unintended consequences. Peel crafted legislation to discipline money by controlling note issue. He succeeded in destroying private note issuance. He failed to control credit creation.

The Act prohibited new banks from issuing notes, froze existing country banks' issuance at 1844 levels, and mandated banks lose note-issuing rights upon merger. In 1844, 277 country banks issued £8.6 million in notes. By 1921, Fox, Fowler and Company issued the last private English banknote — a 77-year transition to Bank of England monopoly.

The Currency School's error was categorical: it treated paper notes as money and deposits as mere accounts. Banks adapted. As monetary historian Charles Goodhart later emphasized, restricting discretionary note issue encouraged banks to expand through deposits instead. Fractional reserve banking did not end; it shifted into demand deposits. By 1900, English payments by value were overwhelmingly settled through bank deposits, checks, and bills rather than notes or coin; in the London banking system, physical currency had been pushed to the margins of high-value exchange.

A note looked like money because it moved hand to hand. A deposit looked like an account entry because it sat in a ledger. But if a buyer could settle a debt by transferring the deposit, the economic function was money-like even without paper changing hands. The Act gave Parliament the appearance of having disciplined money creation while leaving the channel that modern capitalism was becoming outside the frame.

Even brilliant economists missed this. John Stuart Mill treated credit as unable to create purchasing power from nothing. Walter Bagehot analyzed deposit banking with unmatched institutional acuity, but still treated banks chiefly as intermediaries rather than creators of money. Not until Keynes's *Treatise on Money* was the truth stated explicitly: banks could safely create money provided they moved forward together. The full articulation awaited Post-Keynesian economists: loans create deposits, not the reverse. The reason it took so long is the reason Peel missed it. Deposit creation was invisible.

The United States followed the same trajectory after the National Banking Acts of 1863–1864 made state bank-note issuance uneconomical. State bank notes collapsed, deposits expanded, and checking accounts became the dominant private money form. On both sides of the Atlantic, the modern two-tier system emerged: central-bank base money at the foundation, commercial-bank deposit money above it.

#### Samuel Gurney's October crisis

Samuel Gurney sat before the Parliamentary Committee in late 1847, his Quaker dress marking him as a member of Norfolk's most prominent banking family. The October crisis had tested the Bank Charter Act's iron restrictions in ways Robert Peel never anticipated. When panic swept London, depositors at Overend, Gurney & Company — the "bankers' banker" serving country banks across England — demanded their money. The Act's rigid cap on note issuance meant Gurney couldn't expand circulation to meet the panic, even with £8 million in deposits backing his operations. The crisis peaked on October 25th. Then the government suspended the Act.

"Those who had sent notice for their money in the morning," Gurney testified, "sent us word that they did not [need it]." The panic that the Act's restrictions had inflamed vanished the moment those restrictions lifted. Gurney understood what Peel's elegant theory had missed: in moments of crisis, confidence mattered more than currency ratios.

Gurney's sentence is one of the cleanest pieces of evidence in the whole controversy. The money did not need to be physically issued in great quantity for the panic to ease. The knowledge that it could be issued changed behavior. That is lender-of-last-resort logic before it had acquired its later textbook form: credible elasticity can prevent the very demand for cash that rigid rules are supposed to discipline.

### Colonial coda: taxation and force

While nineteenth-century orthodoxy pushed tax-receivability to the margins of theory, colonial administrators used it brutally in practice. British colonial hut and poll taxes in Kenya, Uganda, Rhodesia, and elsewhere were payable only in colonial currency. Kenya's 1901 Hut Tax Ordinance required 2–3 rupees per hut annually. Communities that had used cattle wealth and barter now had to obtain rupees by selling to settlers or entering wage labor on colonial plantations.

The point is not that coercion makes money legitimate. It is that tax obligation can create currency demand, and imperial states knew how to use that fact even while metropolitan theory often denied or minimized it. The chartalist mechanism appeared here in its ugliest form: not as democratic public finance, but as forced monetization.

---

The 1821 deflation had caused unnecessary suffering, destroyed livelihoods, and demonstrated that returning to gold at the old parity imposed arbitrary costs on debtors. Yet this lesson would be forgotten. A century later, Britain would repeat the mistake with even more devastating consequences when Winston Churchill returned to gold at an overvalued parity.

The gold standard had reached its intellectual apex. What William Paterson had stated plainly in 1694 — that the Bank of England created money out of nothing — was now unthinkable in respectable economics. The institutional reality of money creation had been buried beneath the surface of gold orthodoxy. But that success contained the seeds of its own destruction.

For eighty years after 1844 the system seemed to vindicate itself. Trade expanded, empires grew, London became the financial capital of the world. The mechanism appeared automatic. In fact it worked because the Bank of England exercised discretionary management behind the Act's rigid rules, because no major war tested the system, because South African and Alaskan gold discoveries kept the supply growing, and because Britain extracted from its colonies what its trade balances could not provide. When war came in August 1914 every combatant abandoned the gold standard within days, and the system that had seemed eternal collapsed in a week.

The orthodoxy that survived would face its real test in the 1920s and 1930s — when Churchill's return to gold, France's gold hoarding, and the Federal Reserve's adherence to Real Bills Doctrine turned financial pressure into mass unemployment, foreclosure, hunger, and political catastrophe. The human cost of those choices would be measured not in thousands but in millions.
