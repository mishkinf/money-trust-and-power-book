# Chapter 06 Sources & Summary

### **Chapter Summary**

Chapter 6 now treats Newton's 1717 report as a hinge rather than a single-cause origin story. It restores the physical and administrative reality of the Great Recoinage: the Tower Mint, provincial mints, rolling mills, screw presses, assay work, counterfeit enforcement, and the public shortage created by remaking a degraded coinage. Newton understood the bimetallic arbitrage problem and sought to preserve bimetallism by reducing the guinea's value; the Treasury adopted a partial reduction of six pence rather than the larger reduction his analysis implied and never followed through with further adjustment. Britain's drift toward gold therefore emerged from under-correction plus wider forces: Brazilian gold inflows, Asian demand for silver, English institutional inertia, commercial habit, and the growing credibility of Bank of England paper. Britain moved gradually from de facto gold dominance toward formal legal adoption in 1816/1821, while Germany's 1871-1873 adoption and silver sales helped globalize the gold standard after 1870.

The chapter also foregrounds the Bank Restriction period (1797-1821) as evidence that Britain's currency could retain value without gold convertibility when supported by productive capacity, taxation, parliamentary credibility, Bank reputation, and expectations of eventual resumption. The classical gold standard is presented as an institutionally managed rule, not an automatic machine. The restoration pass adds a stronger steelman of gold's real coordination advantages before turning to the distributional ledger.

The system carried distributional consequences. Its deflationary bias raised the real value of debts and bond payments while squeezing borrowers and wage-earners during contractions. Germany, Hamburg, the United States, Japan, Russia, India, Argentina, and Brazil appear as examples of how gold adoption mixed credibility, trade access, imperial pressure, and domestic political conflict. Whether those consequences were inherent costs of monetary discipline or design choices about whose interests the system would protect is a question that would dominate British politics from 1815 to 1925, and that the chapters on the Bullion Controversy, the Bank Charter Act, and Churchill's 1925 return to gold engage in detail.

3. The "fiat always fails" narrative overlooks that hyperinflation results from productive capacity destruction and foreign currency traps, not money creation per se. Venezuela's oil production collapsed 77% before hyperinflation peaked. Weimar's mark stabilized at 320:1 through mid-1922 despite continued printing, collapsing only after France occupied the Ruhr (eliminating 80% of coal/steel production). Zimbabwe's agricultural output fell 50% seven years before 2007-2008 hyperinflation. Counter-examples prove the opposite: Japan sustains 250% debt-to-GDP with deflation, the US created $8 trillion (2008-2022) with minimal inflation, and Britain's 1797-1821 Bank Restriction (24 years, 226% debt-to-GDP) produced only 22.3% total inflation. The constraint is real resources and institutional capacity, not nominal money supply. Japan can sustain unprecedented debt because it's yen-denominated; Argentina defaults at 75% because it borrows dollars it cannot print. See Epilogue and Appendix A for detailed analysis.
