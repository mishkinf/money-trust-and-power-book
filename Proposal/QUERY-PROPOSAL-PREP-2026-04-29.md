## Core Positioning

*Money, Trust, and Power* is a serious trade history of money that explains why the household-budget metaphor is wrong, why that does not make public money limitless, and why every monetary regime is ultimately a political choice about trust, power, capacity, and who pays when promises fail.

The book's distinctive move is not simply "gold bad, fiat good." Its governing frame is paired vindication: monetary conflicts usually contain two real fears. Gold-standard defenders were right to fear discretionary over-issue and inflationary abuse; their opponents were right to see that monetary contraction could destroy workers, debtors, and productive capacity. The history of money is the history of institutions choosing which danger to prevent and which population to expose.

## One-Sentence Pitch

A five-thousand-year monetary history showing that money has always been institutional credit, not household budgeting, and that the real limits on public money are productive capacity, credibility, governance, and the distribution of loss.

## Short Pitch

From a barley loan in ancient Larsa to Edward Backwell's ruined goldsmith bank, from Thomas Attwood's forgotten Birmingham movement to Churchill's return to gold, Keynes's defeat at Bretton Woods, Volcker's recession, quantitative easing, pandemic inflation, and cryptocurrency, *Money, Trust, and Power* tells the story of money as institutions powerful enough to make promises stick. It gives readers a replacement for the household-budget metaphor: governments that issue their own currencies do not run out of money like households, but they can run out of real capacity, institutional trust, political legitimacy, and credible adjustment mechanisms.

## Why This Book Now

The public argument over money is trapped between two inadequate slogans. One says governments must balance budgets like families. The other says sovereign currency makes money easy. The last fifteen years have shown why both are wrong: the 2008 crisis proved central banks could create vast sums without consumer inflation when the economy had slack; the 2021-22 inflation proved money hitting damaged capacity can become painfully real; crypto proved that trying to escape institutions usually recreates them in less accountable forms.

This book gives readers a historically grounded way to think about deficits, inflation, central banks, bailouts, debt ceilings, industrial policy, and crypto without requiring them to become economists.
