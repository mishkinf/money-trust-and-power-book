**Title:** *Money, Trust, and Power*
**Subtitle:** *The Five-Thousand-Year Fight Over Who Gets to Create Money*
**Length:** Approximately 62,000 words

**Category:** Serious trade nonfiction / economic history / political economy

## Overview

*Money, Trust, and Power: The Five-Thousand-Year Fight Over Who Gets to Create Money* is a serious trade history of money that replaces the household-budget metaphor with a more accurate and more useful one: money is institutional credit, sustained by enforceable obligation, productive capacity, credible governance, and public trust.

The book moves from ancient barley debts and medieval merchant bills to the Bank of England, the gold standard, Churchill's disastrous return to gold, Keynes's defeat at Bretton Woods, Nixon's closure of the gold window, Volcker's disinflation, quantitative easing, pandemic inflation, and cryptocurrency. Across five thousand years, the same question keeps returning: what makes a monetary promise trusted, and who pays when that trust fails?

The argument is not that gold was fake and fiat is free. It is that every monetary system chooses which danger to fear most: inflation, unemployment, creditor loss, debtor ruin, democratic overreach, elite capture, institutional breakdown, or external constraint. Gold-standard defenders were right to fear discretionary over-issue and inflationary abuse. Their opponents were right that monetary contraction could destroy workers, debtors, and productive capacity. The book's central frame is paired vindication: monetary history is full of arguments in which both sides saw a real danger, but institutions chose which danger to prevent and which population to expose.

That frame gives readers a way to understand today's debates over deficits, inflation, central-bank independence, debt ceilings, bailouts, industrial policy, and crypto without requiring them to become economists. Governments that issue their own currencies do not run out of money like households. But they can run out of real capacity, institutional credibility, political legitimacy, and credible mechanisms for distributing loss.

## Why This Book Now

The nearly two decades since 2008 have made ordinary citizens fluent in monetary events without giving them a coherent monetary education. The 2008 crisis showed central banks creating trillions to rescue the financial system. The pandemic response showed governments spending at a scale once described as impossible. The 2021-22 inflation, and the harder last mile of the 2026 inflation fight, showed that real capacity constraints still bite. Crypto promised escape from institutions and then recreated banks, runs, leverage, fraud, custody failures, and finally court-administered recoveries in less accountable form.

Public debate still reaches reflexively for the household-budget metaphor: government must live within its means, debt burdens our grandchildren, money printing always causes inflation, and "sound money" means discipline. The opposing slogan, that sovereign currency makes money easy, is equally inadequate. *Money, Trust, and Power* gives readers the historical grammar needed to reject both simplifications.

## Audience

Primary readers include:

- Serious trade nonfiction readers interested in economic history, power, crisis, and institutions.
- Readers who followed the 2008 crisis, the pandemic inflation debate, central-bank rescues, or crypto collapse and want the longer story.
- Policy-interested readers frustrated by debt-ceiling, deficit, inflation, and central-bank debates.
- Readers of Liaquat Ahamed, Adam Tooze, Barry Eichengreen, David Graeber, Stephanie Kelton, Christine Desan, and financial-crisis nonfiction.

The book is not personal finance, not a textbook, not a narrow MMT manifesto, and not a simple gold-standard debunking tract. It is a narrative institutional history with a civic payoff.

## Comparable Titles

**Liaquat Ahamed, *Lords of Finance***
Comparable for narrative monetary history with human stakes and central-bank personalities. *Money, Trust, and Power* has a broader historical sweep and a clearer replacement for household-budget thinking.

**Adam Tooze, *Crashed***
Comparable for monetary architecture as political power. This manuscript is more accessible to general readers and traces the architecture back through centuries of institutional design.

**David Graeber, *Debt***
Comparable for the long history of credit and obligation. This manuscript is more disciplined around monetary institutions, central banking, and policy tradeoffs.

**Stephanie Kelton, *The Deficit Myth***
Comparable for public-facing challenge to household-budget politics. This manuscript is less programmatic and more historical, with stronger emphasis on inflation, institutional credibility, and real-resource limits.

**Christine Desan, *Making Money***
Comparable for money as legal and institutional design. This manuscript translates adjacent insights into a more narrative trade frame.

**Barry Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters* and *Exorbitant Privilege***
Comparable for gold-standard and dollar-system history. This manuscript is more explicitly organized around what non-specialist readers should now understand about public money.

## Chapter-by-Chapter Synopsis

### Preface

The Preface introduces the book's central inversion: money is not a thing but a relationship, a social technology created by institutions and sustained by trust. The household-budget metaphor is not merely incomplete; it is a historical forgetting of how monetary systems actually work.

### Chapter 1: The Birth of Credit

Money begins not in barter but in institutional accounting. Mesopotamian temples and palaces recorded obligations in units of account long before coinage dominated exchange. Taxation and enforceable obligation created demand for money independent of intrinsic metal value. The chapter establishes the book's first principle: money derives power from credible institutions.

### Chapter 2: Merchants of Trust

Medieval merchants built international credit networks through bills of exchange, reputation, and double-entry bookkeeping. Gold did not need to move every time trade crossed a border; promises moved instead. But when Edward III suspended payments and confidence in Italian banking houses broke, private credit revealed its public foundation: sophisticated private finance cannot survive when sovereign promises fail.

### Chapter 3: The Sovereign's Dilemma

Monarchs could create domestic credit through tally sticks and tax-backed obligations, but foreign war required gold and silver that creditors could demand. Charles II's 1672 Stop of the Exchequer destroyed trust in personal sovereignty and ruined goldsmith-bankers like Edward Backwell. The lesson was political: credible money required institutions stronger than a single ruler's need.

### Chapter 4: When Private Money Failed

Goldsmith banking turned private deposits into a proto-banking system, creating money by lending multiples of reserves. It worked until public trust failed. The Stop of the Exchequer exposed the system's fragility and anticipates later shadow-banking crises: private credit can expand brilliantly, but when confidence breaks it needs a public backstop.

### Chapter 5: The Bank of England Solution

The Bank of England solved the credibility problem by marrying private banking capacity to parliamentary taxation. It did not simply intermediate existing savings; it created present purchasing power against future public revenue. The chapter explains how tax-backed public credit made Bank money credible without making gold the true source of value.

### Chapter 6: Newton's Accident

Isaac Newton's 1717 adjustment to England's bimetallic currency helped push Britain toward gold by overvaluing gold and undervaluing silver. What later looked like natural monetary law began as institutional accident, then survived through Bank of England management, parliamentary credibility, and global British power.

### Chapter 7: Paper Proves Itself

The Bank Restriction period from 1797 to 1821 proved that paper money could function for decades without gold convertibility when institutions were credible. But the return to gold imposed brutal deflation on farmers and debtors. Ricardo and the Bullionists defended gold discipline; Henry Thornton and later Thomas Attwood saw the human cost of contraction.

### Chapter 8: The Great Forgetting

The Currency School converted a defensible fear of unconstrained paper into doctrine. Thomas Attwood and the Birmingham School identified unemployment as a monetary wound, but the Bank Charter Act of 1844 embedded gold orthodoxy into law. The tragedy was conceptual: the law constrained notes while deposits became the real engine of modern bank money.

### Chapter 9: The Interwar Catastrophe

Churchill's 1925 return to gold at the old parity imposed adjustment on workers and coal communities. Gold hoarding by France and the United States helped transmit depression. Roosevelt's break from gold in 1933 showed that credible institutions could use monetary flexibility to revive output. The chapter turns gold from abstraction into lived unemployment, foreclosure, and political breakdown.

### Chapter 10: Bretton Woods and the Bancor That Wasn't

At Bretton Woods, Keynes's bancor proposal lost to Harry Dexter White's dollar-centered system. The result worked for a generation because American power and gold reserves were real, but it contained the Triffin dilemma: the world needed dollars, and the supply of dollars gradually undermined confidence in dollar convertibility.

### Chapter 11: The Breaking of Bretton Woods

De Gaulle's gold offensive, Britain's devaluation, the London Gold Pool collapse, and Nixon's 1971 closure of the gold window revealed that the postwar gold-dollar compromise could not survive its own success. Nixon called the suspension temporary. It became permanent, inaugurating the age of pure fiat.

### Chapter 12: The Tests

Pure fiat did not remove monetary constraint; it changed its location. The 2008 crisis showed that money creation in a slack economy need not produce consumer inflation. The 2021-22 inflation showed that money meeting damaged capacity can become painfully real. Volcker's disinflation, Japan's stagnation, the Hagedorn farm, quantitative easing, and crypto all test the same principle: the limit is not metal but capacity, credibility, governance, and distributional legitimacy.

### Epilogue: From Gold to Governance

The Epilogue distills the book's civic payoff. Money began in institutional accounting, taxation creates baseline demand, domestic and foreign monetary promises pose different problems, gold worked only through credible institutions, and fiat flexibility requires discipline. The book ends by returning to Edward Backwell: private credit fails without enforceable public obligation, and monetary freedom without governance becomes another form of power without accountability.
