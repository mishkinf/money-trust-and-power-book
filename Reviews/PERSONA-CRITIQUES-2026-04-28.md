## 1. NYT Book Review staff reviewer

In the opening pages of "Money, Trust, and Power," a Sumerian scribe presses wedge-shaped marks into damp clay; on page two, Isaac Newton fumbles a calculation; by the epilogue, a Lehman attorney watches $200,000 in stock evaporate over a weekend. The author — a first-time trade-nonfiction writer working without an obvious academic perch — has clearly studied the books that succeeded in this category, and the bones of "Money, Trust, and Power" are those of a serious, well-built manuscript: 67,000 words, twelve chapters, a single throughline (money is institutional credit, not metal), and a willingness to take the strongest objections seriously rather than steamroll them.

The strongest single set piece in the book is Chapter 4, "When Private Money Failed." Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn on Lombard Street — owed £295,994 by Charles II on the morning of January 2, 1672, walking into the Treasury meeting on Sunday, January 7, and dying in exile in the Netherlands eleven years later — is the kind of character work Liaquat Ahamed gave us with Norman, Strong, Schacht, and Moreau. The dramatic clock works ("the news travelled the way bad news travelled in seventeenth-century London — from clerk to clerk, by foot, faster than any printing press"), the arithmetic is plain, the consequences are personal, and the connection to 2008 ("Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn would have recognized the dynamics on Lombard Street in 1672 instantly") earns its place. Chapter 9's Harry Todd — a Boldon Colliery miner whose seventeen-month lockout is set against Churchill's $4.86 versus $4.77 decision — is similarly load-bearing. The Todd family eating better under wartime rationing than under the gold standard's "supposed peace and prosperity" lands.

When the manuscript is working, this is what it does: it pins macroeconomic abstraction to one named human being whose ledger sheet you can read. Amat-Sîn and her daughter Šāt-Sîn at the temple of Gula in 1801 BCE; Francesco Datini's December 12, 1399, bill of exchange; Aunt Berta in 1923 trading the principal of a family mortgage for a single salt herring; Maureen McKenzie in Kirkwood, Missouri, watching her option-ARM principal balloon to its cap. These are the moments that suggest the author understands what Lewis and Ahamed understand — that the reader has to feel the cost.

The trouble is what happens between them.

Too often the manuscript reaches for an effect and takes three paragraphs to do what one would do better. The preface's framing — "Money is not a thing but a relationship" — is bolded and then restated in the opening of Chapter 1, the closing of Chapter 1, the framing of Chapter 5, and again in the epilogue. Graeber said it once, on page 21 of "Debt," and trusted the reader for the next 500 pages. The author of this book does not yet trust the reader. The thesis is delivered, redelivered, and gift-wrapped. By Chapter 8, when we are told that the Currency School "made alternatives literally unthinkable" and that "what William Paterson stated in 1694 — that the Bank creates money 'out of nothing' — was now conceptually impossible," we have read some version of that sentence ten times.

The prose, similarly, oscillates. There are sentences with real cadence — "He had built a banking empire that served royalty and merchants; he had accumulated, by the standards of any age, real wealth" — and then there are clunkers like "money had been created from nothing" set in its own bolded paragraph as if the reader might miss it. Bolded didactic asides ("**Money had been created from nothing.**" / "**They abandoned the miners.**") work in a Substack post; in a 67,000-word trade book they grow tiresome. They signal that the author is anxious you might not get it.

The book is also overstuffed with explanatory glosses set in parentheticals — "(an institution that can provide emergency liquidity to banks during crises)," "(physical coins made of precious metal like gold or silver)," "(the practice of profiting from price differences between markets by buying low in one place and selling high in another)." These are written as if for a reader less sophisticated than the one who will actually buy this book. Kelton trusted her readers; this manuscript condescends to them.

Chapter 12 is where the manuscript's structural weakness shows. The 2008-versus-2021 inflation comparison is genuinely interesting and the productive-capacity framework is well-defended. But the chapter then races through Weimar, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Japan, the 1970s, hyperinflation, deflation, hikikomori Michinao Kono, Eva and Victor Klemperer, Maureen McKenzie, Lehman pension holders Charles Kwalwasser and Renee Spero, and Boulder small-business owner Barbara Kantor. Any one of these is a strong scene; together they crowd each other out. The Klemperer salt-herring scene and the Kono employment ice-age set piece are both excellent reporting, and they would be devastating if they had room to breathe. They do not. Tooze, in "Crashed," would have given Klemperer five pages and trusted the silence around him. Here he gets a paragraph, then a table.

The argument's fairness is real and, in places, distinctive. The Currency School, the Buchanan-Wagner public-choice critique, the Sargent-Wallace fiscal-dominance scenario, the Hayek-Selgin-White hard-money tradition — all are taken seriously rather than waved off, and the epilogue's "open questions" section is unusually candid for a book with this thesis. That's a virtue. But it also reveals the manuscript's central craft problem: the author has done the homework of a scholar and the scene-work of a journalist, but has not yet figured out how to make those two modes serve each other paragraph by paragraph. Ahamed's discipline was that the argument emerged from the characters; here, the characters arrive to illustrate an argument that has already been stated three times.

There are also unforced errors. The Bretton Woods chapter's depiction of Harry Dexter White as a procedural villain reads thinner than Benn Steil's coverage of the same conference; the line "We will try to produce something which Your Highness can understand" is good, but the framing around it ("The insult was calculated") tells the reader how to feel rather than letting the line do its own work. The recurring "this is exactly how X works today" interjections — modern stablecoins like USDC, central bank digital currencies, SWIFT codes — are useful once and then become a tic.

What the book has, that matters: a real argument, a real range, a real instinct for the scene that converts a date and a number into a person whose Tuesday morning was ruined. What it does not yet have: the discipline to leave the reader alone with that scene.

**VERDICT: QUALIFIED RECOMMEND.**

*Pull quote:* "When 'Money, Trust, and Power' lets a single named human being carry the weight of an argument — Backwell at the Unicorn, Harry Todd at Boldon Colliery, Aunt Berta and her salt herring — it reaches the standard its comp titles set; when it explains the argument a fourth time in bold type, it sells itself short."

**On bestseller-tier craft:** No — not yet, and the gap is identifiable and fixable. Ahamed, Lewis, Tooze, Kelton, and Graeber each trust the reader to keep up; this manuscript trusts a less sophisticated reader than the one who actually buys 67,000-word monetary histories. The scene work is genuinely close to Ahamed-grade — Backwell, the Todd family, the Klemperer salt-herring vignette — but the connective tissue overexplains, the thesis is restated half a dozen times in bolded sentences, and didactic glosses ("convertibility — the ability to exchange paper money for precious metal at a fixed rate") interrupt scenes that should be doing the explaining themselves. A disciplined revision — cutting roughly 15 percent, removing the bolded thesis-repetitions, trusting the parentheticals to disappear, and giving the strongest set pieces (Backwell, Todd, Klemperer, Kono) the room their counterparts get in "Lords of Finance" — would close most of the gap. The argument is right-sized for the moment; the prose is not yet at the level that turns a strong argument into a book that sells 200,000 copies.

## 2. Skeptical mainstream economist

### On the steelmen

Let me start with what is actually here, because it matters: this manuscript is not a Kelton imitation. There is a serious effort, particularly in the second half of Chapter 12, to engage what I would recognize as the orthodox case. The Volcker section has been substantively reworked. Chapter 7 includes a paragraph on Friedman-Schwartz and Bernanke 2002 that does not flinch ("Money does matter for nominal variables; sustained inflation is, in the long run, a monetary phenomenon... the argument of this book is not against Friedman on these points"). Chapter 12's public-choice section names Buchanan, Wagner, Niskanen, Sargent-Wallace, and Cochrane, and concedes — in the author's own voice — that the productive-capacity framework "owes the public-choice tradition an institutional architecture, not a wave of the hand." That is the right concession. It is a real one.

But the steelmen are uneven, and the unevenness goes one direction.

#### 1. Volcker (Ch. 12, ~lines 269–311)

The Volcker treatment is the strongest part of the book on the orthodox side. It explicitly endorses the conventional account ("the chapter does not dispute it") and runs a counterfactual based on Bernanke's 2005 essay on Latin American indexation. The Argentina/Brazil section — the de-anchoring counterfactual, the figure on negative real returns to passbook savers from 1973–81, the reframing as "two patterns of cruelty" rather than cruelty versus gentleness — engages the expectations channel at roughly the level a thoughtful policy reader (not a graduate macro student, but close) would recognize. The Hagedorn farm vignette is given disproportionate emotional weight, and the framing of Volcker's choice as "redistributing the cost from price-paying creditors to wage-earning workers" reappears in the appendix and the 1970s section in language the rest of the chapter formally retracts. But within the Volcker section itself: this is fair. **Verdict on Volcker specifically: SERIOUS, with residual editorial inconsistency elsewhere.**

#### 2. The 2021–22 inflation (Ch. 12, ~lines 9–42)

This is the weakest part of the case, and the unevenness shows. The opening framing sentence calls 2021–22 the "hardest case" for the productive-capacity framework, which is generous to itself: it is in fact the case that orthodox demand-side accounts predicted in real time and the productive-capacity framework would not have predicted absent the supply-shock overlay. To his credit, the author cites Blanchard's and Summers's February 2021 warnings, the New York Fed's "roughly two-thirds to aggregate demand shocks" finding, and Bernanke-Blanchard 2023. The honest synthesis paragraph — "supply factors likely account for around half... demand factors for roughly a third to a half" — is closer to defensible than the chapter's earlier "supply chain disruptions accounted for approximately 60% of above-trend inflation" framing, which selectively cites the supply-favorable end of the literature.

The deeper problem: the chapter never engages the *ex ante* question. If the productive-capacity framework "predicted exactly this asymmetry," why did its leading public proponents (Kelton, Wray, the MMT camp the appendix cites approvingly) not predict the 2021 inflation? Blanchard and Summers did. The chapter retroactively absorbs Blanchard's prediction into its own framework — "their warnings were the productive-capacity framework's prediction in a public-policy register" — which is a rhetorical move, not an analytical one. Blanchard's case rested on aggregate demand exceeding potential output by a multiple, not on sectoral capacity bottlenecks. The appendix at line 96 still says outright: "the 2021-2022 inflation came from supply chain disruptions and profit margin expansion during COVID recovery, **not from 2020 fiscal stimulus**." That is the camp's actual position, baldly stated; the chapter's "honest synthesis" is the camp's strategic retreat from it. **Verdict on 2021–22: PARTIAL. The chapter has been edited toward fairness; the appendix has not.**

#### 3. Public-choice / Buchanan-Wagner (Ch. 12, lines 323–329)

Two paragraphs. Buchanan, Wagner, Niskanen named. The reply: independent fiscal councils, symmetric fiscal rules, sunset clauses, automatic stabilizers triggered by inflation thresholds. This is a genuine institutional answer rather than a wave-away, and the author concedes "political incentives matter at least as much as productive capacity." But the section is brief, the proposed institutional architecture is named rather than defended, and the post-1971 advanced-economy deficit record is acknowledged in the Preface and Epilogue without being analytically engaged — the chronic-deficit pattern Buchanan predicted, and the Reinhart-Rogoff debt-sustainability literature, are simply absent. The Epilogue concedes the critique "has not been refuted," which is honest, but it is conceded in a paragraph rather than answered in a chapter. **Verdict: PARTIAL. The critique is named, the rebuttal is gestured at, the empirical reckoning is deferred.**

#### 4. Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane FTPL

Sargent-Wallace gets two sentences. Cochrane gets one. Both are correctly characterized at the level of the bumper-sticker — "if a fiscal authority runs persistent primary deficits without credible future surpluses, monetary restraint cannot ultimately prevent inflation" — and the Epilogue concedes the framework "does not exclude" the FTPL scenario. The 2021–22 episode, the chapter says, could be read through that lens: "alternative readings are not absurd." But the unpleasant arithmetic and the fiscal theory are technical claims with specific empirical content — about the joint determination of the price level and the public debt, about the role of expected primary surpluses — and the manuscript engages neither. A graduate macro student would not recognize Cochrane in two sentences. The Chapter 7 footnote explicitly bracketing chartalism from FTPL ("Cochrane's FTPL is a distinct claim... Chapter 12 distinguishes them") is good throat-clearing; Chapter 12 does not in fact distinguish them at the level the throat-clearing promised. **Verdict: NAME-CHECK rather than engagement.**

#### 5. Currency School in Chapter 8

This is the clearest case of a steelman set up to lose. The chapter premise opens by calling the Currency School "defensible on its own terms," and Chapter 7's Bullionist section is genuinely sympathetic to Ricardo, Overstone, and Torrens — the Restriction-era inflation was real; the assignat collapse was real; the temptation for a captive issuing bank to over-issue was real. That paragraph is good. But Chapter 8 then runs a 250-line indictment of Currency School orthodoxy as institutional capture by bondholders — Overstone's £2 million fortune, the "deliberate institutional networking," the Norman family's 123-year continuity — interleaved with the Attwood biography, the Birmingham rallies as proto-democracy, and the deflation-as-wealth-transfer arithmetic. Attwood is described as having been "right about the cause and right about the cure." The Currency School's "tragic error" was missing deposit creation; its surviving program is described as "creditors' interests dressed as natural law." This is not steelmanning; this is the prosecution case. **Verdict: PERFORMATIVE.** The Chapter 7 concession is the steelman; Chapter 8 is the verdict the steelman was set up to deliver.

#### 6. Productive capacity vs. inflation acceleration

The framework's central claim — that productive capacity is the binding constraint — is repeatedly stated in a way that conflates *slack* with *potential* and ignores the standard NAIRU/expectations-acceleration framework. The "concert hall with 100 seats" metaphor (line 125) is exactly the model that produces wrong predictions: it has no expectations, no Phillips curve, no acceleration mechanism, no role for the monetary aggregate other than as a ticket count. The chapter never engages the question of *when* inflation begins to accelerate before measured slack is exhausted — which is the central operational question for any working central bank. Japan's deflation-despite-QE is treated as confirming evidence; it is at least equally consistent with credit-channel and balance-sheet-recession accounts that don't require the productive-capacity framing. **Verdict: PARTIAL conflation. The framework works as a heuristic; it is presented as theory.**

### OVERALL VERDICT: PARTIAL

This is a meaningfully edited manuscript that has moved a substantial distance toward fairness on Volcker, on Friedman-Schwartz, on the Hayek-Selgin-Ammous tradition, and on the public-choice critique. The Chapter 12 voicing of the Volcker disinflation is the work of someone who took the criticism seriously. But the steelmanning is uneven across chapters, the appendix flatly contradicts the chapter's "honest synthesis" on 2021–22, Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane are named rather than engaged at any technical depth, and Chapter 8 is structurally a takedown of the Currency School with a paragraph of prefatory respect attached. The framework's central claim — productive capacity as binding constraint — is presented without engaging the inflation-acceleration mechanics that constitute the orthodox objection. A reader who comes to the book holding the orthodox view will find their position named, occasionally credited, sometimes engaged seriously, and ultimately not refuted on its own terms.

### Bestseller paragraph

Setting aside platform: at the craft level, this is closer than I expected to the category it wants to join. The prose has the Ahamed register — long set-piece scenes (Klemperer's coffee, the Hagedorn diary entry, Kantor's PBS interview, Lehman's 401(k) holders) that carry the institutional argument through individual lives. The thesis is legible, the structure holds across twelve chapters, the human-cost vignettes do real argumentative work rather than ornamenting a thesis. It is better written than Kelton, more institutionally serious than Graeber on the modern material, less cinematic than Lewis, less synthetic than Tooze. What holds it back from clear bestseller craft is two things: first, the analytical unevenness this critique has documented — sophisticated readers will notice the steelmen wobbling, and the trade press's most influential reviewers (Krugman, Wolf, the *FT* Alphaville crowd) are exactly those readers. Second, the manuscript is still resolving its register: parts read like a serious institutional history, parts read like advocacy. *Lords of Finance* never sounds like advocacy. Tighten the analytical unevenness, particularly Chapter 8 and the MMT appendix, and the craft is there.

## 3. Trade nonfiction acquisitions editor (Big 5)

**INTERNAL ACQUISITIONS MEMO**
**RE: *Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work***
**Status: Unsolicited / Unagented MS, ~67k words, 12 chapters + preface/epilogue/glossary/MMT appendix**

**Book in one paragraph.** A 67,000-word narrative history of money built around a single load-bearing thesis: money has never been a thing but a relationship — a record of obligation backed by institutions strong enough to enforce it. The author runs the argument from a 1801 BCE Larsa barley loan through Datini's Bruges, the Stop of the Exchequer, Newton's bimetallic miscalculation, Attwood's Birmingham, Churchill's 1925 return to gold, Bretton Woods, Volcker, 2008, and 2021's CPI surge — landing on a productive-capacity framework that engages Hayek, Ammous, Buchanan-Wagner, and Cochrane on their own terms.

**Comps.**
- *Lords of Finance* (Ahamed, 2009; ~125k words; Pulitzer) — **aspirational.** This MS reaches for Ahamed's set-piece method and structurally invites the comparison; it does not yet have Ahamed's character oxygen.
- *The Deficit Myth* (Kelton, 2020; NYT bestseller) — **realistic on argument, not on platform.** This is the book Kelton's readers will reach for next, but it gets there via history rather than polemic, which softens the political hook.
- *The Lords of Easy Money* (Leonard, 2022) — **realistic.** Same skeptic-of-orthodoxy register, same Fed-and-distribution preoccupation, same trade-narrative grain. Solid mid-five-figure category.
- *Money: The True Story of a Made-Up Thing* (Goldstein, 2020) — **realistic and possibly the closest fit.** Same five-thousand-year arc, similar institutional thesis, lighter on argument and shorter (but Goldstein had Planet Money behind him).
- *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (Graeber, 2011) — **aspirational.** The MS shares Graeber's chartalist instincts and explicitly walks the same temporal arc; it lacks Graeber's anthropological cross-cultural reach and his prose vinegar.

**Target reader.** The Planet-Money/Odd-Lots/Macro-Voices listener; finance-curious knowledge workers who already buy Tooze, Kelton, Leonard, Levitin; econ-adjacent academics looking for assignable trade nonfiction; the small but durable Bitcoin-skeptic and Bitcoin-curious adjacent audience (the MS is unusually fair to Hayek/Selgin/Ammous, which matters). Realistic ceiling without a platform: 15–25k hardcover. With a strong platform and a podcast tour: 40–60k. Bestseller list is reachable only with op-ed real estate and a name attached.

**Marketing hooks.** The catalog sentence writes itself: *Why governments can never "run out of money," and why every generation has to learn it again.* Strong op-ed angles: the household-budget metaphor as the gold standard's longest-living ghost; what 1797 tells us about 2020's $3T expansion; the Birmingham School as proto-Keynesians. The Newton chapter (the gold standard as a 2.33% Treasury under-correction) is a *Planet Money* segment fully formed. The Backwell/Stop of the Exchequer set piece is podcast gold. The Hagedorn Iowa-farm and Harry Todd Durham-miner sections are the book's emotional anchors and would carry galley pull-quotes.

**Structural concerns.** The 67,000-word length is the central problem. Successful comps run 95k–160k. At this length the book reads as either *deep* or *thin* depending on what's in front of you, and what's in front of you for the first three chapters is the thinner version: the Mesopotamia–medieval material is competent but moves at textbook pace and front-loads exactly the ancient throat-clearing that loses commercial readers before the modern payoff. Chapter 12 is 13,400 words — twice any other chapter — and is doing the work of two chapters compressed (2008, 2021, crypto, public choice, Volcker, distribution). It's the strongest chapter argumentatively and the most overstuffed structurally. The thesis lands, but it lands four times: in the Preface, in Ch.5, in Ch.8, and in the Epilogue, in nearly identical language. The set pieces are real (Backwell at the Unicorn; Newton's lab; Attwood at Newhall Hill; the Mount Washington Hotel; the Hagedorn diary) — but they are surrounded by argumentative connective tissue that a developmental editor would cut by a third.

**Author platform requirements.** Unsigned, this MS needs one of: (a) an academic post at a recognizable economics or history department with prior trade publication; (b) a Substack/podcast presence in the 50k+ subscriber range in the macro/finance/history space; (c) a working journalist's masthead at *FT Alphaville*, *Bloomberg Opinion*, *The Atlantic*, *NYT*, or equivalent; (d) a credentialing co-author (Kelton, Tooze, Wray, Goodhart) willing to put a name on the cover. Without one of these, the book is a six-figure-advance category competing as a five-figure-advance acquisition. The MS itself does not name the author or signal a platform, which is itself diagnostic.

**Verdict: YES-WITH-REVISIONS** — contingent on (1) author platform meeting one of the four bars above and (2) a developmental edit that cuts Part I by 30%, splits Ch.12, expands Ch.10–11 (Bretton Woods is undercooked given how much weight it carries), and de-duplicates the thesis statement.

**On the craft question.** Setting platform aside: this is not yet at Ahamed–Lewis–Tooze–Graeber level on the page, and the gap is specific rather than diffuse. Ahamed wins through four central bankers rendered as full human beings across 500 pages; Lewis wins through dialogue and scene; Tooze wins through analytical density carried by a singular voice; Graeber wins through vinegar and cross-cultural scope. This MS has the historical instinct and the set-piece selection of that league — Backwell, Attwood, Hagedorn, Todd, the Klemperer hyperinflation pages, the Keynes-at-Bretton-Woods sequence are genuinely strong — but the prose modulates between sharp narrative and textbook exposition more than the comps do, the same thesis sentence recurs too often, and there is no single sustained character whose interior life carries the reader the way Ahamed's Norman or Lewis's Burry does. What holds it back is craft-level rather than conceptual: voice consistency, length, and a developmental editor's hand on the thesis-restating tic. With a 90k-word expansion that deepens three or four central figures into Ahamed-grade portraits, and with a Kelton-tier platform behind it, this clears the bar. As-is, with a strong platform, it's a respected mid-list title that gets reviewed in the *FT*, the *NYRB*, and *Foreign Affairs* and sells 20–30k. The bestseller outcome is reachable; the manuscript is not yet there.

## 4. Conservative / sound-money critic

# Review: *Money, Trust, and Power* — Mishkin Faustini's House of Mirrors

*A pre-publication assessment for the sound-money tradition*

There is an old trick in academic publishing whereby a book announces, in its preface, that it will steelman its opponents — and then, six hundred pages later, the opponents are found face-down on the floor with the author's fingerprints on a candlestick. Mishkin Faustini's *Money, Trust, and Power* is a sophisticated specimen of the genre. The author is too well-read, too fluent in the literature, and too punctilious in his footnotes for the steelman to be absent. It is genuinely there. It is also, in too many places, hollow.

Let us begin where the author's tradition begins: with discipline.

The book's central claim — laid down at Preface, p. 1 — is that "money is not a thing but a relationship," and that "the binding constraint on what governments can spend in their own currency is the productive capacity of the economy, not the metal in their vaults or the balances on their accounts." Fair enough as a description of the post-1971 dispensation. But the framing is not neutral. By Chapter 6 ("Newton's Accident") the gold standard has already been demoted from monetary order to clerical mishap — a "Treasury under-correction in 1717, layered over a Brazilian gold boom and an Asian silver pull… and then, by the late nineteenth century, an orthodoxy whose origins almost no one could name." The metaphor is recurring: gold as accident, gold as nostalgia, gold as the architecture whose deflationary bias "was the architecture, working as the architecture worked." The passive voice is doing heavy work. The reader is invited to admire the framework's even-handedness while gold is escorted out the back.

Did the gold standard receive a fair hearing as a *discipline mechanism*? Not really. Faustini concedes (Ch. 6) that "Selgin, White, and Bordo have made it carefully" — and then promptly moves on to distributional ledgers, Indian taxpayers, and Birmingham deflation. The Hayekian point — that gold's discipline is valuable precisely *because* it does not consult productive-capacity econometricians — is acknowledged only as the worry of "the simpler version of the story." That is not steelmanning. That is condescension dressed in courtesy.

Chapter 8 ("The Great Forgetting") is more troubling. It is a beautifully written chapter — Attwood and the Birmingham hillside crowds are vividly drawn — but it is structured as a melodrama in which the Currency School wins through "deliberate institutional networking," Lord Overstone's Political Economy Club purges, and bondholder self-interest. The Currency School is not engaged on its merits; it is sociologized. Peel's argument that note issue should be governed by gold flows is reduced to "the deflationary mechanism delivered rising real bond values to creditors and squeezing nominal incomes to debtors and wage-earners; the architecture treated those distributional consequences as the automatic working of natural economic law rather than as political choices the framework had made on Britain's behalf" (Ch. 8). The Currency School was wrong in its diagnosis of *deposits as money* — fair enough — but the deeper point, that issuers with discretionary power and a fiscal master eventually over-issue, is conceded once in the Preface and then abandoned. The reader is left with the impression that the men who built the 1844 Act were Rothschild proxies who happened to write good prose.

The Volcker chapter (Ch. 12) is the book at its best and its most revealing. Credit where due: Faustini does name Volcker as the man who broke the inflation, does engage Bernanke's 2005 Latin America essay, and does construct a counterfactual using Argentina (1975–1990, 5,000 percent peak) and Brazil to argue that the Hagedorn farm and the Argentine pensioner were two faces of the same coin. The line — "Volcker's choice was therefore not between cruelty and gentleness. It was between two patterns of cruelty" — is honestly earned and tells the truth. This is how a steelman reads. The chapter does *not* dismiss Volcker as the bondholder's enforcer. It treats him as a man making a hard institutional judgment under uncertainty, and gives him his due: "the institutional foundation for what later became known as the Great Moderation… was real."

But the productive-capacity framing then recovers itself, and the same chapter casts the 1970s inflation as essentially OPEC's fault — "This wasn't 'too much money chasing goods'" — which is the standard cost-push apologia for Burns. The reader of *Capitalism and Freedom* will spot the move. The unpleasant arithmetic of Sargent and Wallace is named (Ch. 12) and Cochrane's FTPL is cited — but the public-choice critique gets a single section, three institutional fixes ("symmetric fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils"), and a pat on the head. *Democracy in Deficit* is summarized accurately and then asked to wait outside.

On Bitcoin, the book performs better than I expected. Ammous is named, *The Bitcoin Standard*'s "hardness" framework is quoted accurately, Hayek's *Denationalisation* is engaged ("the root and source of all monetary evil"), Selgin's productivity norm is described correctly, and the empirical record (volatility, El Salvador, Mt. Gox, Terra) is laid out without condescension to easy targets like FTX (which gets one sentence). The conclusion — that crypto institutions have recreated bank-like institutional risk — is fair. But the deepest Hayekian point, the *knowledge problem* in central planning of money, is nowhere addressed. Faustini believes a fiscal council can read the output gap. Hayek's whole career stands as a warning that it cannot.

The MMT appendix is where the steelman finally collapses. It presents MMT as "a descriptive framework — like Newton describing gravity," denies it is "a political ideology," and waves away the inflation tax as a "more stringent constraint, not a looser one." The author of these pages has read Buchanan but has not absorbed him.

**Verdict:** A serious, learned, beautifully sourced book that gives the sound-money tradition more daylight than most of its progressive cousins — and still asks it to lose. The Volcker chapter is honest. The crypto chapter engages real authors. Chapter 8 and the MMT appendix are not steelmen but ventriloquism. **Three stars where five were possible.** Send it to the publisher. Send it also to a reader who will demand that gold's discipline be defended, not contextualized.

**On the bestseller question:** At the craft level, this manuscript is genuinely close to *Lords of Finance* and *Debt* — the human vignettes (Attwood's Birmingham, the Hagedorn farm, Eva Klemperer's coffee, Aunt Berta's herring, Maureen McKenzie's ARM) are extraordinary, and the prose has the rhythmic confidence trade nonfiction requires. It is more disciplined than Kelton, more granular than Tooze, less performative than Lewis. What holds it back from the top tier is not voice but architecture: the productive-capacity thesis is *announced* in the Preface and then *protected* through twelve chapters, and the seams show. Ahamed and Graeber wrote books in which the thesis emerges from the material; Faustini's emerges from the introduction and conscripts the material. Trim 8,000 words, let the gold standard win one round on points, and an editor at Penguin Press could move this. Platform-wise, it needs a Klein or Krugman essay, a Cowen *Conversations*, and one heterodox right-of-center endorsement (Selgin himself, ideally) to cross over. The craft is there. The political courage to risk a fairer Chapter 8 is what would unlock the bestseller.

## 5. Working economic historian

# Essay-Review: *Money, Trust, and Power*

**Essay-Review for *Foreign Affairs* / *JEL***

The genre of the long-arc monetary history — the trade-press telling that runs from cuneiform to the Fed in three hundred pages — is by now a crowded shelf, and crowded for a reason: the underlying scholarly literatures have matured to the point where a synthesis is genuinely possible, and the post-2008 audience is genuinely interested. *Money, Trust, and Power* enters that shelf as a serious entrant. Its central thesis — that money is institutional credit anchored by taxation rather than a commodity-derived medium of exchange — is by now the consensus position of the credit-theory tradition (Innes, Knapp, Goodhart, Wray, Ingham, Hudson, Graeber), and the manuscript honestly says so in the Preface (p. 18): "It is not a recovery of suppressed knowledge; it is a translation of a settled scholarly tradition." That self-positioning is correct, and it should be held to throughout. Where the manuscript holds itself to it, the result is an unusually intelligent piece of synthesis. Where it slips into the populist register that has overtaken much MMT-adjacent writing, the synthesis frays.

**On the early material.** Chapter 1's Mesopotamia is mostly within the consensus, but the consensus is not as stable as the chapter implies. Hudson's "palatial balance-sheet economies" framing is accurately attributed; the Schmandt-Besserat token argument is fairly handled; the Ur III *bala* figures (188 million liters of grain, 500 administrators, "half a million non-food-producing workers") are presented with confidence that exceeds what Steinkeller, Garfinkle, and the recent prosopographic literature would license — the half-million figure in particular is contested. The "amargi" / *andurarum* presentation is closer to Hudson's interpretation than to Van De Mieroop's more cautious reading; that's a defensible choice but it is a *choice*, and the chapter does not flag it as one. The Larsa loan tablet (Kress 83 / P370944) is a genuine document, used appropriately, though the prosopographic confidence ("Year 21 of Rīm-Sîn I") is greater than the surviving damage strictly supports. Most importantly: the Song dynasty *jiaozi* claim that "by 1204, over 4 million notes circulated backed by only 700,000 coins in reserves" — this is a specific number whose provenance I cannot reconstruct from the standard literature (Von Glahn, *Fountain of Fortune*, would be the natural source and gives a more textured account), and absent a footnote, it has the feel of a Wikipedia-grade summary statistic doing rhetorical work the underlying scholarship would not bear.

**Chapter 2 is the manuscript's weakest link in source quality.** The Datini set-piece (December 12, 1399) is the kind of beautifully rendered scene the genre demands, and the Datini archive is real — but the manuscript's engagement with the secondary literature on Italian banking and the bill of exchange is thin. De Roover's *The Rise and Decline of the Medici Bank* is the foundational work; Goldthwaite's *The Economy of Renaissance Florence* is the standard recent reference; Mueller's two-volume *The Venetian Money Market* and Bell, Brooks & Moore's revisionist work on *cambium*-as-credit should all be in conversation here. None of them appear to be. The *cambium non est mutuum* discussion is present but compressed into a folk version; the Bardi/Peruzzi collapse is rendered using figures (400,000 / 600,000 florins; "many times the annual revenue of the English crown") that broadly track Hunt's *The Medieval Super-Companies* but elide the more recent revisionist literature (Hunt himself, Sapori's revised arithmetic) that has questioned whether Edward III's default was the proximate cause as opposed to the trigger of an already-deteriorating position. The chapter's treatment is competent journalism; it is not a competent engagement with the historiography.

**Chapter 5 (Bank of England founding) and Chapter 6 (Newton's 1717 ratio) are stronger.** The Paterson voicing is one of the manuscript's set-piece achievements — the Cameronian-Covenanter background, the Darien disaster, the projector ecology around Defoe — and the chapter is honest about the contested attribution of the "out of nothing" formulation, which is exactly right (the line has been debated since Macleod). The £1.2 million / phased-call structure (£720,000 actually paid in) is correctly reported. The 1697/1708/1742/1800 charter renewals are given accurately. The Newton chapter's framing as "accident" is more rhetorical than historical — Feavearyear, Craig, and the more recent Kleer literature treat the 1717 adjustment as a more deliberate response to the global silver-gold price differential than the chapter's "miscalculation" narrative allows — but the basic arithmetic and timeline are right. The Godeffroy / Hamburg 1872 set-piece is the manuscript's most original piece of historical writing. It is also the one most exposed to the problem the manuscript is alert to: the surviving correspondence does not establish the connection between Godeffroy's resignation and the currency vote, and the chapter says so explicitly ("the timing is striking but the link cannot be made on the public record"). That epistemic discipline is admirable. Hold on to it.

**Chapter 8 (1844 Bank Charter Act) is intellectually ambitious and partly succeeds.** The Currency vs. Banking School reconstruction is broadly defensible: the manuscript correctly identifies Loyd/Overstone as the architect, Norman as the multi-generational institutional carrier, the Tooke/Fullarton/Wilson opposition as substantive, and the post-1844 transition to deposit money as the doctrine's central practical failure. This tracks Wood's *A History of Central Banking*, Schwartz, and Clapham's *Bank of England* tolerably well. The Attwood rehabilitation is more polemical than the Wood/Schwartz tradition would write it, and the "Birmingham School as proto-Keynesian" framing leans on Asa Briggs and the older Birmingham historiography rather than on Daunton or O'Brien — defensible, but the chapter overclaims when it asserts Attwood was "right about the cause and right about the cure." He was directionally right about money's non-neutrality; he was not right in the analytical detail later macroeconomics required, and the modern vindication claim is generous. The treatment of the 1839 Chartist petition rejection (235-46) as evidence that "chartalist understanding had been erased" reads the parliamentary debate more politically than the record bears.

**Chapter 9 (interwar) is squarely within the Eichengreen-Temin-Ahamed consensus.** The Churchill 1925 reconstruction, the McKenna "it will be hell" line, the Keynes *Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* analysis, the Irwin gold-hoarding figures (40 percent of 1928–32 deflation attributable to U.S./French sterilization) all track the standard literature. The Todd family vignette and the Iowa "Farmer A" reconstruction are well-executed and properly sourced (Murray's 1933 study). The "Lebesque on the métro benches" set-piece is more impressionistic. No fresh claim is being made that the consensus literature would dispute.

**Chapter 10 (Bretton Woods) tilts toward the Steil reading over the Skidelsky/Helleiner reading**, which is a defensible interpretive choice but should be acknowledged. The Robbins/Vinson "we know we will be beaten" line, the Morgenthau "seize the hotel" threat, Keynes's "unhouselled, disappointed, unaneled" letter, the Bareau Savannah quotation — these are all real, all properly used, and the chapter handles them with care. The "bancor died in preliminary negotiations" reading is closer to Steil's *The Battle of Bretton Woods* than to Helleiner's more institutionally textured account in *States and the Reemergence of Global Finance*. The Triffin dilemma framing is standard. **Chapter 12 (Volcker)** sits in the Meltzer / Silber / Greider triangle with Silber's *Volcker* the dominant influence; the Hagedorn farm vignette is well-rendered; the explicit acknowledgment of the South American counterfactual (Bernanke 2005 on Latin American hyperinflation) is the kind of analytical balance specialist endorsers will respect.

**On craft as historiography.** The manuscript's strongest move is its consistent willingness to steelman the metallist, monetarist, and public-choice traditions on their own terms — the engagements with Mises's regression theorem, Friedman-Schwartz, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, and Hayek-Selgin-Ammous are handled with intellectual seriousness uncommon in this genre. Its weakest move is the recurring "modern parallels" passage at the end of nearly every chapter (Greece 2010, China 2025, COVID 2020, FTX 2022) which pull the prose toward op-ed register and away from the historiographic discipline the chapter has just demonstrated. The footnote architecture is uneven: the early chapters use a superscript apparatus (¹, ²ᵃ, ⁹ᵃ) implying citations that the manuscript file does not actually contain — I cannot evaluate citation rigor because the citations themselves are not present in the chapter files I read. This is a significant gap. The book cannot go to specialist endorsers without footnotes the endorsers can verify.

**Verdict: COMPETENT SYNTHESIS** — with two reservations and one upside. The reservations: Chapter 2's engagement with the Italian-banking historiography is too thin to send to a de Roover-trained reader without revision; and the citation apparatus appears to exist in superscript but not in fact, which must be remedied before any specialist sees the manuscript. The upside: where the book engages contested scholarship honestly (Chapters 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12), it does so at a level that compares favorably to Ahamed and Steil and exceeds the level of MMT-adjacent trade writing on the same material.

**On the bestseller question.** Set publishing aside: is the prose, the structure, the intellectual achievement at the level of *Lords of Finance*, *The Big Short*, *Crashed*, *Deficit Myth*, *Debt*? Honestly: not yet, but closer than the author probably fears. Ahamed's gift was a small cast of characters carried across hundreds of pages — Norman, Strong, Schacht, Moreau — and the manuscript's set-pieces (Backwell, Paterson, Godeffroy, Attwood, Keynes-at-Bretton-Woods, Volcker) suggest the author can do that work. What holds it back at the craft level is *structural*: the book is currently a thematic argument illustrated by chapters, where the bestsellers in this category are narratives that *generate* an argument as a byproduct. *Crashed* is held together by the 2008 calendar; *Lords of Finance* by four men over twenty years. *Money, Trust, and Power* is held together by an ideological position the author cares about, and the "modern parallel" tic at chapter ends keeps reminding the reader of that. Cut the modern parallels, let the historical material make the case, and the book moves a full level closer to the shelf it wants to be on. Platform-and-marketing alone won't get it there. The cuts will.

# Synthesis

## Where the personas converge

These five readers do not share a politics, a methodology, or a generation. They converge on three findings — and convergence across that spread is the strongest signal in this exercise.

**1. The set-piece scenes are the manuscript's craft achievement, and the connective tissue is the craft problem.** Every persona — even the hostile ones — names specific scenes as working at or near comp-title level: Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn (Ch.4); Paterson and the founding (Ch.5); Godeffroy and Hamburg 1872 (Ch.6); Attwood at Newhall Hill (Ch.8); the Todd family at Boldon Colliery and Churchill at $4.86 (Ch.9); Keynes at Bretton Woods (Ch.10); the Hagedorn Iowa farm and the Klemperer salt herring (Ch.12). What the personas fault, in nearly identical language, is what happens *between* the set pieces: the thesis is restated 4–6 times in nearly identical wording (NYT, editor, historian); bolded didactic asides ("Money had been created from nothing.") read as Substack tics in a trade book (NYT); parenthetical glosses condescend to readers more sophisticated than the glosses assume (NYT); "modern parallel" interjections at chapter ends pull the prose into op-ed register (historian, NYT); and the chapters are structured as a thesis illustrated by material, not a narrative that generates a thesis (historian, editor, conservative critic).

**2. Chapter 8 is the structural weak point — a prosecution dressed as a steelman.** The skeptical economist names this most directly ("the prosecution case"), the conservative names it ("ventriloquism"), and the historian flags the analytical overclaim on Attwood ("right about the cause and right about the cure" is generous). This is the single chapter where three of five personas independently flag the same problem. The Currency School is conceded once in Chapter 7 and then convicted in Chapter 8 by sociologizing its proponents (Overstone's £2 million, Norman family continuity, Political Economy Club purges) rather than answering its strongest claim — that discretionary issuers with a fiscal master eventually over-issue.

**3. Length, structure, and the Chapter 12 overload.** The editor and the NYT reviewer both flag that 67k is short for the comps (95k–160k) and that the front-loaded ancient material risks losing commercial readers before the modern payoff. Both also flag that Ch.12 is overstuffed — doing the work of two chapters compressed (2008, 2021, crypto, public choice, Volcker, distribution) — and that the strongest scenes inside it (Klemperer, Kono, the Hagedorn farm) are crowded out of the room they need.

## Where the personas diverge

**On the Volcker chapter.** The skeptical economist and the conservative critic — who came in expecting the manuscript's weakest point — both call the Volcker treatment fair, even strong. The economist: "this is fair." The conservative: "this is how a steelman reads." The "two patterns of cruelty" framing, the Bernanke-2005 Latin American counterfactual, and the Argentina/Brazil section earned credit from both sides. The recent voicing work in Ch.12 is paying off, and the personas notice.

**On the MMT appendix.** The economist and the conservative both single out the appendix as where the steelman collapses — and specifically as where the appendix contradicts the main chapter's "honest synthesis." The appendix's flat claim that 2021 inflation came from supply chains and margins, "not from 2020 fiscal stimulus," is at odds with Ch.12's negotiated middle position. This is the cleanest fix: the appendix has not been edited in step with the chapter.

**On craft level vs. comp titles.** All five personas converge on "not yet, but the gap is identifiable and fixable." None says it is at Ahamed/Lewis/Tooze/Graeber/Kelton level on the page; none says the gap is a void. The diagnoses cluster:
- *Reader-trust* gap (NYT): cut the bolded thesis-restating, the parenthetical glosses, trust the scenes.
- *Architectural* gap (historian, editor, conservative): the thesis is announced and then protected, rather than emerging from the material — let the historical material make the case.
- *Length* gap (editor): 67k is the wrong length for the category; 90k-with-deeper-character-portraits is the right length.
- *Analytical-fairness* gap (economist): Ch.8 and the appendix wobble; sophisticated reviewers (Krugman, Wolf, FT Alphaville) will spot it.

**On the citation apparatus** (historian only, but a hard requirement): the superscript apparatus implies citations that the chapter files do not contain. This must be resolved before the manuscript goes to any specialist endorser.

## Highest-priority issues to address before sending out

In order of severity:

1. **Fix the citation apparatus.** Superscripts without footnotes is a non-starter for any historian-blurber. Either deliver complete notes or remove the superscripts. (Historian.)
2. **Reconcile the MMT appendix with Ch.12's negotiated position on 2021–22 inflation.** The appendix currently says one thing the chapter has formally retracted. Edit the appendix to match. (Economist, conservative.)
3. **Rebalance Chapter 8.** The Currency School needs a real defense before it gets the takedown — not "defensible on its own terms" as a one-paragraph concession. Engage the discretionary-issuer-with-fiscal-master concern on its merits, not by sociologizing Overstone. (Economist, conservative, historian.)
4. **Cut the bolded thesis-restating throughout.** The thesis is delivered four to six times in near-identical language; cut to one. Remove the bolded didactic asides ("Money had been created from nothing."). (NYT, editor.)
5. **Cut or thin the "modern parallel" passages at chapter ends.** They pull the prose into op-ed register and undercut the historiographic work the chapters have just done. (Historian, NYT, editor.)
6. **Split or restructure Chapter 12.** It is doing the work of two chapters compressed; the Klemperer and Kono set pieces deserve room to breathe. (Editor, NYT.)
7. **Strengthen Chapter 2 against the Italian-banking historiography.** De Roover, Goldthwaite, Mueller, the *cambium*-as-credit revisionist work — these need to be in the conversation before a de Roover-trained reader sees this chapter. (Historian.)
8. **Decide on length and direction.** Either accept the editor's diagnosis that 67k is short for the comps and expand into deeper character portraits, or accept the NYT/historian diagnosis that the right move is to cut 15% and tighten. The current length serves neither commercial nor literary aims fully. (Editor, NYT, historian.)

## On the bestseller question — convergent answer

All five personas, asked independently whether this is at the craft level of *Lords of Finance*, *The Big Short*, *Crashed*, *Deficit Myth*, or *Debt*, returned the same answer: **not yet, but the gap is specific and closable.** The diagnosis is consistent across personas:

- The set-piece selection and rendering is at or near comp-title level.
- The thesis-announcement and thesis-protection structure puts the book in a different mode from the comps, which generate their thesis from the material.
- The reader-trust gap (bolded restating, parenthetical glosses, modern-parallel tics) is the most fixable item.
- The analytical unevenness (Ch.8, MMT appendix) is the second most fixable.
- The length question is the structural decision the author has to make: tighten to 57k as a sharp argument-led book, or expand to 90k as character-led narrative history. The current 67k is the worst of both choices, per the editor.

Platform-wise, the personas converge on: a Klein/Krugman essay or *Conversations with Tyler*, a Kelton-tier endorsement, and one heterodox right-of-center endorsement (Selgin is named twice). Without one of those, the editor's realistic ceiling is 15–25k hardcover; with them, 40–60k; bestseller list reachable only with op-ed real estate and a name attached.

The headline finding: this is a manuscript that *can* reach its category's top shelf with a disciplined revision. The revision targets are concrete and small in number. The hardest of them — Chapter 8's structural rebalance and the MMT appendix's reconciliation with Ch.12 — are intellectual rather than prosodic, and the author has shown (in the Volcker section especially) that he can do that work when he chooses to.
