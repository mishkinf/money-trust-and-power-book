#### AFTER

> Two predictions of the inflation deserve credit at the outset. In February 2021, Olivier Blanchard at the Peterson Institute calculated that the $1.9 trillion American Rescue Plan plus the prior December package totaled roughly "$2.8 trillion, or nearly 3 times an overly generous estimate of the output gap of $900 billion," with multiplier effects that "could set off inflationary pressures of a kind we have not seen in a generation." Inflation arrived. Their directional call was right.
>

Blanchard's argument turned on aggregate demand exceeding a fixed productive capacity — what the standard output-gap arithmetic measures. But productive capacity in 2021 was not fixed. It was actively being destroyed and reorganized by COVID. Between February and April 2020, three percentage points of the U.S. labor force exited — roughly eight million workers; participation was still about a percentage point below trend through late 2021. Permanent business closures across hospitality, retail, and live entertainment ran into the hundreds of thousands. The supply disruptions that drove headline inflation higher were not a separate story from the demand surge; they were the same story, in which capacity was a moving target rather than the fixed concert hall the standard output-gap arithmetic assumed.

Eurozone inflation peaked at 10.6 percent in October 2022 — close to the U.S. peak — but reached that level in a fiscal context very different from the American Rescue Plan. European fiscal stimulus during 2020–21 was a fraction of the U.S. magnitude relative to its output gap, and Europe's inflation surge tracked closely with energy prices: heating gas, electricity, and the secondary effects of the Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Take energy out of the European inflation accounting and the residual is small. Take fiscal stimulus out of the U.S. accounting and the residual is much larger. Large fiscal expansions during periods of constrained supply translate disproportionately into prices — which is what the framework predicts and what Blanchard and Summers warned about in February 2021. The orthodox demand-overshoot frame got the direction right and the dominant mechanism only partly right. The sanguine consensus — establishment and heterodox alike — got the magnitude wrong while getting the supply-driven character of the inflation more nearly right. **Word delta:** +500 words (replaces ~80, net +420)

### BEFORE (line 327)

> Modern restatements sharpen the worry rather than soften it. Sargent and Wallace's "unpleasant monetarist arithmetic" (1981) showed that if a fiscal authority runs persistent primary deficits without credible future surpluses, monetary restraint cannot ultimately prevent inflation; central-bank independence becomes nominal. John Cochrane's *Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* (2023) generalizes this: large deficits unbacked by credible expected surpluses must be offset either by explicit default or by inflation. From a public-choice standpoint, a regime that normalizes deficits and declares solvency a non-constraint is precisely a regime in which the fiscal-theory channel becomes more, not less, salient.

### AFTER

> ### Fiscal dominance: a deeper objection
>
> The strongest restatements of this concern run through monetary economics rather than public choice, and they cut close to the bone of the framework defended in this book. Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace's 1981 "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic" showed that under what they called fiscal dominance — when the fiscal authority sets primary deficits independently and the central bank is left to finance whatever surpluses do not appear — monetary restraint cannot anchor the price level on its own. "Tighter money now," they wrote, "can mean higher inflation eventually," because the debt arithmetic has to balance, and if expected primary surpluses do not arrive on the schedule the government has implicitly promised, the price level itself becomes the variable that adjusts. John Cochrane's 2023 *The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* generalizes the same intuition into a statement about how price levels are determined. "The fiscal theory says that the price level adjusts so that the real value of nominal debt is equal to the present value of primary surpluses." If credible expected surpluses do not exist, the adjustment runs through inflation rather than through repayment.
>
> Both arguments name a constraint the productive-capacity framework does not, by itself, address. The framework explains *when* nominal spending meets real bottlenecks — when an economy at or near full utilization absorbs additional demand into prices rather than into output. Sargent-Wallace and the fiscal theory explain *why a nominal anchor can fail in the first place* — why the central bank's interest-rate rule, no matter how disciplined, cannot determine long-run inflation when fiscal policy is not providing the primary surpluses the rule presupposes. They are not the same claim, and it is a category error to treat them as one. They answer different questions. A complete account of fiat money has to answer both.
>
> The historical record contains episodes that look like fiscal dominance. The United Kingdom's postwar policy of holding gilt yields below the inflation rate while running large deficits — the financial repression of 1945–1951 — produced an effective inflation tax that resolved much of the wartime debt overhang at savers' expense. Brazil's 1980s and early-1990s indexation regime, in which the price level rose roughly with the velocity of fiscal-monetary accommodation, was the textbook case of monetary policy chasing fiscal facts. Turkey's 2021–2024 episode, in which a politicized central bank pursued unorthodox cuts while deficits ran wide, produced annual inflation above 70 percent and a currency that lost three-quarters of its dollar value in three years. None of these episodes is consistent with a simple "productive capacity is the only constraint" reading. Each looks more like Sargent-Wallace or the fiscal theory in concrete form: a state issuing currency without the credible future surpluses to back the debt path it was running, and a price level that adjusted accordingly.
>
> What the productive-capacity framework adds, when set alongside Sargent-Wallace and the fiscal theory, is the conditional under which fiscal-monetary coordination is *not* dominant: a regime in which the central bank operates with credible independence, the fiscal authority commits to long-run primary surpluses sufficient to back the debt path it is running, and the institutions enforcing both commitments are politically robust enough to survive election cycles. Under those conditions, productive capacity becomes the binding constraint, because the nominal anchor is intact. Outside those conditions — and the conditions are not automatic — the binding constraint is fiscal-monetary credibility itself, and Sargent-Wallace's arithmetic asserts itself. The institutional architecture proposed in the next section is, in part, an attempt to specify what those conditions look like and what would have to be true for them to hold. It is a thinner answer than the public-choice critique probably wanted. It is a fairer one than name-checking Sargent and Wallace and moving on.

**Justification:** The audit found the existing two-sentence treatment was "name-check rather than engagement" and that "a graduate macro student would not recognize Cochrane in two sentences." This expansion meets the criticism on its own terms: distinguishes capacity-constraint from nominal-anchor claims, gives Sargent-Wallace and FTPL their own separate analytical content, and grounds both in identifiable historical episodes (UK 1945–51, Brazil 1980s–90s, Turkey 2021–24). It also positions the institutional-architecture paragraph that follows as an honest answer to a real problem rather than a pre-emptive deflection.

### NEW SUBSECTION

> ### The knowledge problem
>
> There is a deeper Hayekian objection that does not fit neatly under public choice or fiscal dominance, and the chapter is honestly stronger for naming it. Hayek's 1945 article "The Use of Knowledge in Society" argued that the central problem of economic order is that the knowledge relevant to the allocation of resources — what is scarce, what is wanted, what can be substituted, what is being produced where — is not given to anyone in its totality. It is dispersed among millions of producers, consumers, and traders, much of it tacit, all of it changing. The price system works, on Hayek's reading, because it condenses that dispersed knowledge into signals that decentralized actors can act on without needing to grasp the full underlying picture. A central planner, no matter how well-intentioned or institutionally robust, cannot substitute for that mechanism, because the planner does not have access to the underlying knowledge. The argument is what Hayek made in 1945, and it is what *Denationalisation of Money* (1976) extended into a critique of central-bank monopoly: the state's control over money removes the disciplinary function of competition and substitutes the discretion of agents who, on the knowledge-problem reading, cannot have what they would need to know to exercise that discretion well.
>
> Apply that argument to the productive-capacity framework. The framework treats sectoral capacity as the binding constraint and proposes that fiscal councils, independent central banks, and inflation-threshold triggers can read sectoral slack in real time. Hayek's worry is precisely that they cannot — that the chip shortage in 2021 looked like a localized supply story until it wasn't, that the labor-market tightening of 2022 was visible to the participants months before it appeared in the aggregate data, that any institution charged with reading the output gap from the center is doing the kind of computation Hayek argued is impossible in principle. The Buchanan-Wagner critique attacks political incentives. Sargent-Wallace attacks debt sustainability. Hayek's knowledge-problem critique attacks the underlying epistemic premise: that anyone, sitting at the center of an economy, can know well enough and quickly enough what the binding constraint is at any given moment. The chapter does not have a clean answer to this objection.
