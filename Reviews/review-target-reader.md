# A Reader's Report on *Money, Trust, and Power*

I read this over a long weekend on the back porch with a pot of coffee and a yellow highlighter, the way I read *Lords of Finance* the summer my father got laid off and I started actually reading the business section. So that's the lens. Not an economist. Not an editor. Just a guy who got curious in 2008 and never quite stopped.

Here's what I noticed.

---

## 1. THE CHAPTER-BY-CHAPTER EXPERIENCE

**Preface.** The opening hooked me. The Bank of England suspending gold for "one year" and lasting twenty-four was the kind of fact I'll repeat at dinner parties. The Yorkshire-farmer-needs-Bank-notes-because-of-taxes paragraph clicked something into place I'd been circling for years. By the time I got to "money is not a thing but a relationship" I had already sent a screenshot to my brother. But the section "Money and Power" started laying out the thesis in a way that felt a little like the author wanted to make sure I knew what side he was on before I'd even bought into the history. I didn't mind it, exactly. I just noticed it.

**Chapter 1: The Birth of Credit.** The Mesopotamian temple stuff is genuinely wonderful. The Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn loan tablet — the actual two women, the actual rate, the actual goddess Gula as creditor — is the moment I stopped resisting. That's where the book stopped being an argument and started being a story. I slowed way down here, in a good way. The metallist counter-section was harder going. I appreciated that the author included it (and that the author admitted Mises has never been "fully refuted on its own terms"), but the prose tightened up and got more academic. I almost skipped the Palley/Semenova paragraph. A day later, what I remembered: clay tablets, the goddess Gula, jiaozi notes in Song China, and the Massachusetts soldiers paid in tax-receivable bills of credit in 1690. Those four things, in that order.

**Chapter 2: Merchants of Trust.** Datini's morning in Bruges was the best chapter opening in the book up to that point. The rendering of the bill of exchange — Orlandini, the parchment, the wax seal, the Pyrenees, Francesco da Prato & Co. making a book entry — gave me the satisfaction of finally understanding what a "bill of exchange" actually is. I had read the phrase in twenty books. This is the first time I could draw it. The Wisselbank section was mechanical but rewarding, and the Bardi/Peruzzi collapse landed because of how carefully the author had set up Datini five pages earlier. Where I slowed: the moralized-credit and Pacioli paragraphs. Necessary, probably, but they read like the author had pulled the bookkeeping textbook off the shelf for a moment. A day later: Datini's wax seal, the Wisselbank's standardized banco guilders, the moment in 1345 when "meticulously drafted, properly sealed, precisely dated" bills became worthless paper. That last sentence hit.

**Chapter 3: The Sovereign's Dilemma.** Greece-as-Edward-III is a genuinely useful framing and I will steal it. The chapter as a whole was solid but felt like the author was setting up Chapter 4 the whole time. The Stop of the Exchequer is told twice — once briefly here, once in detail in Chapter 4 — and the second time hit harder, which made me wonder whether this chapter needed the first telling at all. The Backwell-Vyner paragraph on page nine of my printout (the one ending "the City of London learned a lesson it would not forget") is one of the best paragraphs in the book. A day later: Edward III ruining the Florentines, the Stop, Spain defaulting seven times in ninety years, the Genoese learning to demand silver collateral.

**Chapter 4: When Private Money Failed.** This is where the book really started cooking for me. The Backwell narrative — the Sign of the Unicorn, the cold morning, the arithmetic he did the way any banker does — was as good as anything in *Lords of Finance*. The 2008 echo is laid on a little thick (and gets laid on again in Chapter 13), but I forgive it because the goldsmith story itself is told with such physical specificity. The "Discovery: How Bankers Realized Gold Could Multiply" section was the cleanest explanation of fractional reserve banking I have ever read. I made my wife read it. She is a pediatrician and she read all of it. That's the test. A day later: Backwell dying in exile in the Netherlands. Vyner reduced to petitioning Parliament while serving as Lord Mayor. The 0.01 percent annual return calculated over thirty-four years.

**Chapter 5: The Bank of England Solution.** Strong and clear. The "Bank didn't have £1.2 million in a vault" passage is doing real work — it's the central conceptual move of the book and it lands. Thomas Fletcher the cloth merchant is a clean example. The "two-tier system" diagram description (country bank notes pyramiding on Bank of England notes pyramiding on gold) finally made me understand what people mean when they talk about base money versus broad money. Where I slowed: the digression on what taxation is "actually for." Three functions, neatly bulleted, but it read like an MMT pamphlet had been dropped into the chapter. I agreed with most of it. I just felt the seams. A day later: Paterson's £1.2 million subscribed in eleven days. The "permanent" debt that was never closed out and remained at £11,015,100 when nationalized in 1946. France paying 7-11% while Britain paid 3-5%.

**Chapter 6: Newton's Accident.** The image of Newton in his Tower laboratory weighing foreign coins is the kind of detail this book lives on. The 2.38% adjustment that took fifty-seven years to globalize a monetary regime — that's the one I'll be telling people about for months. Gustav Godeffroy's resignation one month before the Hamburg Senate's capitulation was a marvelous piece of narrative reconstruction. Where I slowed: the gold-points mechanism subsection. Necessary but dry. The Baring Crisis paragraph saved it. A day later: Newton, the 2.38%, Brazilian gold doubling between 1694 and 1724, and Bismarck's five billion gold francs of French reparations becoming the substrate of the global gold standard.

**Chapter 7: Paper Proves Itself.** The Bank Restriction Act is the book's secret weapon and the author knows it. Twenty-four years of fiat money during the Napoleonic Wars is exactly the kind of fact that could change a reader's mind about modern monetary policy, and it did mine, at least partially. James Woodforde the Norfolk rector calmly receiving his tithe in Bank notes was a great touch. Where I disagreed with myself: the Real Bills Doctrine sections. The "movie tickets" analogy is good but the author keeps explaining it three different ways and I started wanting him to trust me. The 1821 deflation aftermath — Samuel Gigney of Latchington, Robert Leach of Halberton, the forty Devon farmers — is devastating and I think also a little overplayed. By the time I got to Leach's "fall from farmer to — what?" I had already mentally enlisted in the author's cause and the paragraph was pushing on a door I'd opened. A day later: the Bank Restriction's twenty-four years, Henry Thornton's prescient 1802 book, the wheat-price collapse from 110 shillings to 45.

**Chapter 8: The Great Forgetting.** This is where I started writing in the margins. Thomas Attwood is the hero this book has been building toward, and he earns it. The Birmingham rally of 200,000 people on Newhall Hill, the 1.28 million signatures, Disraeli's "financial monomania," and Attwood dying in 1856 as "an obscure figure with few obituaries" — that's the saddest sequence in the book. Lord Overstone's institutional capture (his £2 million fortune, George Warde Norman's fifty-one years on the Bank's board, Montagu Norman as grandson) was the moment I stopped thinking of monetary history as accidents and started thinking of it as a class project. That said: this is also the chapter where the book's politics show through most clearly, and I went back and forth on how I felt about it. The arithmetic of wealth transfer — bondholders getting a 26% real return while a farmer's debt burden grew 41% — is brutal and clearly correct. But the chapter sometimes reads like a closing argument rather than a chapter. A day later: Attwood, "monomania," the Norman family's 123 years embedded in British monetary institutions.

**Chapter 9: Manufacturing Orthodoxy.** Shorter, tighter, and more focused than Chapter 8. The Banking School/Currency School fight was new to me and the author makes it stick. Hawes's prediction that money creation would simply "find alternatives" if you closed off note issuance — and the eventual quiet triumph of deposit banking — was a beautiful piece of historical irony. Harold Heslop's story (1898-1983, scholarship to Central Labour College, unemployed in 1927, novelist documenting the experience) connected the 1844 Act to the 1925 disaster better than any direct argument could. Where I slowed: the Scottish "free banking" footnote-in-the-text and the colonial hut-tax passage. Both relevant, both feeling shoehorned. The Rodger Cantwell / Tipperary eviction story was powerful but I wasn't sure why it was in *this* chapter. A day later: Hawes being right and ignored. Goodhart's "fare-thee-well" line. Heslop's autobiography titled *Out of the Old Earth*.

**Chapter 10: The Interwar Catastrophe.** This is the chapter that broke me. Harry Todd at 21 Rosebery Terrace, Boldon Colliery — his wife Edith pregnant, baby Henry born during the lockout, the family teaching the children to snare birds and make shoes from coal sacks, eating better under WWII rationing than under "the supposed peace and prosperity of the 1920s monetary orthodoxy" — I read that paragraph twice and then I read it to my wife. The Iowa farmer ("Farmer A") and his $90,000 in conservative debt is a perfect counterpart. Morvan Lebesque sleeping in métro entrances at twenty-two. Three different countries, three different mechanisms, the same monetary error. The Keynes pamphlet quote — "He who wills the end wills the means" — is going on the wall over my desk. Where I slowed: I didn't really. This was the chapter where I forgot I was reading a book about economics. A day later: Harry Todd, the children, snared birds, "2s. in the £," Lebesque's "trying not to die."

**Chapter 11: Bretton Woods and the Bancor That Wasn't.** Compelling but long. The setup — Mount Washington Hotel, the calculated chaos, White's procedural control — was masterfully done. The Keynes-versus-White material I knew partly from *Lords of Finance* and Benn Steil's book, but the author found a fresh angle: not "Keynes lost a debate" but "the conference was a piece of theater designed to ratify a decision already made." The "designated driver at a party" Triffin metaphor is the cleanest version I've encountered. Where I slowed: the bullet-point comparison of bancor versus dollar standard ran on for several pages and felt like a PowerPoint slide had escaped into the prose. I started skimming. The chapter recovers in the closing pages with Keynes collapsing on the hotel stairs, the Hamlet quotation ("unhouselled, disappointed, unaneled"), the Belgian delegate Theunis saying "you are on your knees to them." That ending was magnificent. A day later: the dotted line, "The kid who owns the ball is usually captain," Keynes dying eight months later at 62.

**Chapter 12: The Breaking of Bretton Woods.** Workmanlike. The 1967 sterling devaluation is well told, especially Wilson's "the pound in your pocket" line being reframed as a defining piece of governmental cynicism. De Gaulle systematically converting dollars to gold, the London Gold Pool hemorrhaging $3 billion in five months, the 12.5% backing ratio by August 1971 — the arithmetic of impossibility was vivid. Where I slowed: the "primer on international payments" felt like it belonged in a textbook. The repetition of the bancor-vs-dollar comparison, after Chapter 11 just did it, was unnecessary. The "designated driver" metaphor reappears here too and by this point I was tired of it. A day later: Volcker's "the game was indeed over," the British asking for $3 billion four days before Nixon, the Queen declaring a bank holiday on March 14, 1968.

**Chapter 13: The Age of Pure Fiat.** The most ambitious chapter and also the most uneven. The opening framing of 2008 versus 2021 — same monetary tool, opposite results, depending on the output gap — is genuinely clarifying and I will think about it for a long time. Maureen McKenzie's mortgage and Barbara Kantor's small-business credit line being cut by 90% are well chosen. The systematic comparison of failed (Weimar/Zimbabwe/Venezuela) versus successful (US/Japan/Eurozone) fiat systems is the cleanest argument I've read against the "fiat always fails" narrative. Eva Klemperer's coffee doubling in price while she drank it was a perfect detail. Michinao Kono in Takarazuka, 1,815 applicants for three jobs, "I want to get up and let them feel secure about me while they're alive" — that's a sentence that will stay with me. Where I slowed and started disagreeing: the 1970s/Volcker section reads as more openly partisan than the rest of the book. The author wants Volcker to be a villain — and he makes a real case — but I kept thinking of *Lords of Easy Money* and wanting more nuance about what would have happened if inflation had been allowed to run. The Hagedorn farm story is shattering, but the "better tools existed" sentence pulls a punch the chapter has been throwing for ten pages. The cryptocurrency section feels rushed and a little grumpy. A day later: 2008 versus 2021 as a controlled experiment. Kono. The Hagedorns losing their farm despite doing everything right. The Klemperers' coffee.

**Epilogue.** Strong. The six lessons are useful and the prose finally relaxes. The CBDC discussion is the most current-feeling part of the book and also where I felt most unresolved — the author flags surveillance and programmability risks but the framing is "good institutions can use them well," and I'm not sure I bought that as the only lens. The Brazil-Argentina contrast was efficient and illuminating. The closing image — money as relationships endured "only when they are tended with care, wisdom, and commitment to justice" — is sentimental but earned. A day later: "Build institutions worthy of trust." That's the line.

## 2. THE CHARACTERS WHO STUCK WITH ME

The ones who came alive on the page:

**Edward Backwell** at the Sign of the Unicorn. The arithmetic he did, the decade he fought, the bankruptcy ten years to the month after the Stop, the death in exile. He's a Dickens character somehow misplaced into an economics book.

**Thomas Attwood.** The whole arc — the button manufacturer, the Birmingham Political Union, the 200,000 people on Newhall Hill, "monomania," the obscure death in 1856, the modern vindication. He is the moral center of the middle of the book. I would tell his story at dinner.

**Harry Todd** of Boldon Colliery. The Todds will haunt me. The detail about eating better under WWII rationing than under the 1920s gold standard is the kind of fact that rewires your sense of what monetary policy *is*. Edith giving birth in February 1926. Tom and Vera and baby Henry Urwin. The seventeen months.

**John Maynard Keynes** at Bretton Woods. I've read about Keynes a hundred times but the rendering here — exhausted, ill, watching his vision die in subcommittee, collapsing on the stairs, choosing the Hamlet quotation about a man dying without last rites to describe what was done to Britain — was the most affecting Keynes I've encountered.

**Michinao Kono** in Takarazuka. A 45-year-old man who never participated in *shushoku katsudo* and spent two decades in his parents' house, taking a dishwashing job in November 2019 to get out of the house and let his elderly parents "feel secure about me while they're alive." That's a person, not a statistic.

**Isaac Newton** in his Tower laboratory weighing coins. I had no idea Newton spent decades as Master of the Mint and personally supervised counterfeiter executions. The 2.38% adjustment as the most consequential accident in financial history is the kind of fact that earns its keep.

**Gustav Godeffroy** of Hamburg, resigning one month before the Senate's capitulation. A small story but precisely told.

**Charles de Gaulle** demanding gold in 1965. He's almost a side character but the narrative gravity of him saying enough is enough is striking.

**Eva and Victor Klemperer** in Dresden, 1923, the coffee doubling while she drank it.

**The Hagedorns** of Spencer, Iowa. Dean's diary entry — "I've never been so terrified in my life" — is unforgettable.

The ones who blurred together:

**Lord Overstone, Samuel Jones Loyd, George Warde Norman, Sir Robert Peel, Henry Goulburn, Charles Wood, Gladstone, Russell, Francis Jeffrey.** The Currency School villains all slid into one another for me. I get that the author is making a point about institutional capture, but I came out of Chapter 8 unable to remember which of these men did what, except Overstone (because the £2 million fortune anchored him).

**Henry Thornton.** I know I should care about him more than I do. He keeps appearing as a kind of prescient genius, but he never gets a scene the way Backwell or Attwood does. He's an attribute, not a person.

**Harry Dexter White** versus **Edward Bernstein** versus **Dennis Robertson** versus **Lionel Robbins** versus **Fred Vinson.** The Bretton Woods supporting cast. Some shaping of who exactly was sneering at Keynes when would have helped.

**The Banking School theorists** — **Tooke, Fullarton, Wilson** — get name-checked but never characterized.

The story I would tell at dinner: Harry Todd, snared birds, baby Henry, eating better under WWII rationing, all because Churchill chose $4.86 instead of $4.77. Three or four people will be quiet for a moment.

## 3. THE ARGUMENT AS I UNDERSTOOD IT

In my own words: Money is not a thing — not gold, not paper, not bitcoin — it's a *relationship* between people and the institutions they trust. From the Mesopotamian temples through the medieval bills of exchange through the Bank of England through the modern Federal Reserve, the same trick has been getting performed: a credible institution creates purchasing power out of nothing, and that purchasing power circulates because people need to use it (most importantly, to pay taxes). The "gold standard" was an accident — Newton's miscalculation, Brazilian geology, Britain's institutional luck — that got dressed up as economic law and then weaponized to protect the wealth of bondholders at the expense of workers and debtors. The collapse of the gold standard wasn't a tragedy but a return to monetary honesty, and the real constraint on money creation is and has always been the productive capacity of the real economy, not commodity reserves. The orthodoxy that claims otherwise (the household budget metaphor, "we can't afford it," "where does the money come from") was manufactured between 1820 and 1850 to serve creditor interests and has outlived the gold standard that supposedly justified it.

I found it persuasive. More persuasive than I wanted to. I came in skeptical of MMT, partly out of a sense that anything that sounds too good to be true probably is, and partly because *Lords of Easy Money* had me worried about central bank overreach. I came out persuaded of about 70% of the author's case. Specifically:

- I'm fully persuaded that credit preceded commodity money. The Mesopotamian evidence is overwhelming and the author handles the Mises/Menger counterargument fairly.
- I'm persuaded that the 1821 deflation and the 1925 return to gold were political choices, not technical necessities, and that they redistributed wealth from workers and debtors to bondholders. The arithmetic is clean and the human stories are not selectively chosen — they reflect a documented pattern.
- I'm persuaded that the productive-capacity principle (2008 vs 2021) explains a lot of what happened in the last fifteen years.

Where I disagreed:

- The book treats fiat money's flexibility as more straightforwardly good than I think the evidence supports. *Lords of Easy Money* showed me how easily QE can become a permanent gift to asset-holders, and the author admits this in passing but doesn't fully wrestle with it. The "build institutions worthy of trust" closing line is true but also a little glib — the book has just spent 400 pages showing how often institutions get captured by concentrated interests, and then concludes with "build better institutions." How?
- The Volcker chapter is the place where the politics most plainly outran the analysis for me. The author wants Volcker's recession to be unambiguously a class crime. I think it's more complicated than that. The author barely engages with what the alternative would have been.
- The crypto section is dismissive in a way I found cheap. Bitcoin and stablecoins have real problems (the author identifies them well) but the broader question of "do we want the central bank to have this much discretion?" deserves more than a scoffing paragraph.
- The CBDC section in the epilogue raises the surveillance issue and then mostly bats it away. I kept thinking: if the lesson of the book is that institutions get captured, why am I supposed to be sanguine about giving them programmable real-time visibility into every transaction?

Where my mind changed: the Bank Restriction Act stuff. Twenty-four years of pure fiat during the Napoleonic Wars, and the world didn't end. I'd never thought about that case. It's a much more powerful argument against gold orthodoxy than abstract MMT theorizing. Also, the bond-holder-as-class-actor framing of the 1820s-1840s. I had treated economic orthodoxies as roughly neutral attempts to figure out how the world works. The author convinced me that's at least partly naive.

## 4. THE PACING

Drag spots:

- The metallist counterargument in Chapter 1 (Palley, Semenova). Important but academic.
- Some of the Real Bills Doctrine explanation in Chapter 7. The movie ticket analogy is good; we don't need it three times.
- The bullet-point comparison of bancor versus dollar in Chapter 11. It runs for pages and reads like a slide deck.
- The repeated Chapter 11/12 setup of the Triffin dilemma. The "designated driver at a party" metaphor was good once.
- The systematic comparison table in Chapter 13 of failed versus successful fiat systems. The point is sharp; the table is overlong.

Rushed:

- The cryptocurrency section in Chapter 13 felt drive-by. Bitcoin, stablecoins, Terra/Luna, FTX in a couple of pages. Given how much of the book's contemporary relevance turns on the crypto challenge, I expected more.
- The CBDC section in the Epilogue. The questions raised are the most consequential in the book for the next twenty years and they get treated in five pages.
- The eurozone material is scattered across multiple chapters and never gets a chapter of its own. Greece deserves more.

Did I consider giving up? No. I came close to skimming during the bancor bullet points in Chapter 11, and during the Real Bills Doctrine repetitions in Chapter 7. But the human-story chapters (4, 6, 10, 13) kept pulling me back. The book is well structured: every time my attention started to wander, a Backwell or a Harry Todd or an Eva Klemperer arrived to remind me why I cared.

## 5. THE VOICE

I trusted the narrator. The footnotes-by-superscript suggested a real archive (I didn't read them but I noticed how granular they got — Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative catalog numbers, *Kress 83*, the Datini archive folio number, the Heslop papers reference at Durham). When the author quoted something specific, the source seemed to exist.

The writing is alive in roughly 70% of the book. The narrative chapters (Backwell, Attwood, Todd, the Klemperers, Kono, the Hagedorns) are as good as anything in the genre. The technical sections (Real Bills, gold points mechanism, the bancor bullet lists) read like a smart professor explaining something he's tired of explaining. The connective tissue — the "this is exactly what happened in 2008" / "this is exactly what's happening in modern China" passages — is sometimes overdone. There must be twenty places where the book interrupts itself to say "and this is the same mechanism as today." Once or twice would be enough; the reader can do that work.

Did the politics show? Yes, especially in Chapters 8, 10, and 13. The author is unmistakably on the side of debtors over creditors, workers over bondholders, employment over price stability, fiscal flexibility over gold-standard discipline. He's not cagey about it. The Preface signals it openly ("monetary systems are never neutral"), so it's not a bait-and-switch. Did it bother me? Sometimes. I think the book would have been more powerful, not less, if the author had let some passages breathe without underlining them. The 1821 deflation and the 1925 return to gold are damning enough on the evidence; the editorializing occasionally distracts from the case the facts are making.

The bigger problem with the politics isn't bias — it's that the author keeps presenting his interpretation as the obvious reading once you "see through" the orthodoxy. There are moments when an alternative interpretation of the same facts is plausible and the book doesn't engage with it. The Volcker chapter is the strongest example.

That said: I trusted him. He showed his work, he attributed his sources, he gave the metallists their best argument before refuting it, and he never patronized me. I'd read another book by this author tomorrow.

## 6. THE FIVE SENTENCES THAT STAYED WITH ME

1. **"Money has always been more than a medium of exchange. It is a mirror of power, law, and collective belief."**  
That's the epigraph. By the end of the book it had earned itself.

2. **"He who wills the end wills the means. What now faces the Government is the ticklish task of carrying out their own dangerous and unnecessary decision."**  
Keynes on Churchill in 1925, quoted in Chapter 10. It's not the author's sentence but the author chose it. It's about Churchill, but it's also about every politician who ever insisted there was no alternative.

3. **"The decision was hailed as the restoration of British honour, the symbol that the disruptions of the Great War had been temporary and could now be put aside."**  
And then the next paragraphs are Harry Todd's family snaring birds for food. That juxtaposition — "honour" set against the actual cost — is the book's central moral move and it does it without sermonizing.

4. **"He had been Chancellor of the Exchequer for four months and was meant to be making his decision now... He saw the contradiction. He wrote it down."**  
About Churchill, March 1925. There's something terrible in "he saw the contradiction. He wrote it down." And then he did it anyway. That sentence does in twelve words what some history books take chapters to do.

5. **"The lesson of history is direct: trust the institutions, not the metal. But more than that: build institutions worthy of trust."**  
The closing. I don't fully buy it as a prescription — the book itself shows how often institutions get captured — but as a *summary* of what the five thousand years are teaching us, it works. It's the line I'll quote.

What they did for me: They were the moments where the book's argument and its prose were doing the same work at the same time. Most of the book is either argument or prose; in those sentences, the two snapped together.

---

## 7. WOULD I RECOMMEND IT?

Yes. With caveats.

**To whom:** People who liked *Lords of Finance* and want to go deeper into the *why* rather than just the *what*. People who read Graeber's *Debt* and wished it had a more disciplined narrative spine. People who are confused by MMT and want to see the case made through history rather than through theory. Frankly, I'd put it in the hands of my politically conservative father-in-law, because the historical material is so concrete that even where he'd disagree with the author's politics, he'd come away knowing things he didn't know.

Who I would *not* hand it to: Someone with no prior interest in economic history. The book is generous to the lay reader — the analogies (movie tickets, designated drivers, two water tanks) try hard, sometimes too hard — but it presupposes a reader willing to sit with technical material for stretches. Also: someone allergic to the political framing. Plenty of conservatives could read this book profitably, but if you bristle the moment a book mentions "creditors versus debtors" as a power relationship, you're going to bristle a lot.

**Would I buy the next book this author writes?** Yes. Without thinking about it. The author has the two things that matter most: he can find the human story in a balance sheet, and he respects the reader enough to do the work. The technical chapters are uneven, the politics are sometimes too plainly displayed, the metaphors get re-used past their welcome. But the book taught me things I didn't know and changed my mind about things I thought I'd settled. That's all I really ask of nonfiction. That, and the chapter where Harry Todd's family eats better under WWII rationing than under the gold standard. That paragraph alone was worth the hardcover price.

I finished the book Sunday night and I've been turning bits of it over in my head all week. That's the test. I'm still thinking about it. I'll be thinking about it for a while.
