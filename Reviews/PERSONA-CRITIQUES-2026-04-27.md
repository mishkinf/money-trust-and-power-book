## 1. NYT Book Review staff reviewer

# A Five-Thousand-Year Argument, in Search of a Story

In *Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work*, an unnamed author sets out to do for monetary history what David Graeber attempted for debt and Liaquat Ahamed achieved for the interwar central bankers — to take a subject most readers consider technical and reveal it, instead, as a story about power. The thesis is bracing and largely correct: money is not a commodity but a relationship; taxation, not gold, is what compels its acceptance; productive capacity, not metal in vaults, is the real constraint on what societies can afford. From the clay tablets of Uruk to the keystrokes of the Federal Reserve, the book argues, the underlying mechanism has not changed.

This is a serious, ambitious book by an author who has read widely and thought carefully. It is also a book that the New York Times reader of nonfiction — the reader who will buy Tooze, Lewis, and Ahamed — will close, in many places, with respect for the argument and frustration with the prose.

Let me start with what works. The book's spine — twelve chapters tracing money from Mesopotamian temple credits through the Bank of England's 1694 founding, the accidental gold standard born of Isaac Newton's 1717 miscalculation, Churchill's catastrophic 1925 return to gold, Bretton Woods, and the Nixon shock — is genuinely illuminating. The author has a real eye for the load-bearing detail. The 1672 Stop of the Exchequer, in which Charles II destroyed England's first private banking system by defaulting on £1.3 million in goldsmith debts, is recovered here as a hinge moment, the failure that made the Bank of England necessary. Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn becomes a figure of genuine pathos — a banker whose business model was sound, whose lending was prudent, whose ruin came down to a king's choice between fleet and creditors. The chapter pairs beautifully, two centuries on, with Lehman Brothers in 2008, and the author makes the connection without forcing it.

The Newton chapter is the book's most original. The argument that the entire two-century reign of the gold standard rested on a 2.33 percent miscalculation by an aging Master of the Mint who was trying to *preserve* bimetallism — that "history's greatest scientific mind" produced the unintended monetary regime that would dominate the world until 1971 — is the kind of insight that justifies a popular history. Combined with the chapter on the Bank Restriction of 1797–1821, when Britain fought and won the Napoleonic Wars on inconvertible paper, it makes a powerful case that gold was a historical accident dressed up as natural law.

Chapter 8, on what the author calls "the Great Forgetting," is where the book's political argument bites hardest. Thomas Attwood, the Birmingham manufacturer who watched his workers starve after the 1821 return to gold and organized 200,000 people on Newhall Hill in 1832 demanding monetary reform, is vivid and important, and his dismissal as a "provincial banker labouring under financial monomania" by Disraeli is devastating context for current debates. Lord Overstone's institutional capture of British monetary policy — the Political Economy Club, the *Edinburgh Review*, fifty-one years of one Bank of England director, three generations of Norman family influence — is documented with care. This is the chapter Graeber would have written had he been a better archive researcher.

And there are individual passages of real power. The Todd family in Boldon Colliery during the 1926 lockout, eating better under wartime rationing than under Churchill's gold-standard prestige. Aunt Berta in Guben in 1923, weeping into her brother's drugstore with the entire principal of a family mortgage in her arms, exchanging it for a single salt herring. Dean and Kaye Hagedorn's Iowa farm, lost in 1986 not to bad weather or imprudence but to a Federal Reserve that had decided the cost of breaking inflation expectations was worth paying with somebody else's land. These are the moments that justify the book's ambition. They show what monetary policy actually looks like at the level where it lands.

So why is the book, as a reading experience, so often frustrating?

The first problem is structural. Trade nonfiction lives or dies on momentum, and this manuscript carries the weight of a textbook. Chapters open with characteristic NPR-podcast scene-setting — "The smell of barley dust filled the air inside the temple granary of Uruk" — and then routinely interrupt themselves to explain, in bold and parentheses, what a "unit of account" or "fractional reserve banking" or "convertibility" means. The bolded glossary terms accumulate, page after page, until the reader feels they are highlighting their own homework. Ahamed in *Lords of Finance* trusted his readers; this author does not, and the suspicion makes the prose itchy.

Second, the book is repetitive in a way that suggests an author falling in love with his thesis. The argument that taxation creates currency demand is sound and important. It is also stated, with minor variations, in every chapter. The Mesopotamian temples, the Chinese jiaozi, Massachusetts colonial bills of credit, English tally sticks, Bank of England notes, colonial hut taxes — each is presented as fresh proof of a principle the reader has already accepted by page eighty. By the time we reach the modern fiat era, the rhetorical structure of the chapters has hardened into a formula: vivid opening, central thesis restated, secondary characters introduced to illustrate the thesis, closing paragraph drawing a contemporary parallel (Greece, China, COVID, Bitcoin). Tooze in *Crashed* trusted the reader to track an argument across nine hundred pages without restating its premises every fifty. This author does not.

Third, the character work is thinner than it looks. The book does the journalism of detail — names, dates, archival citations — but the human beings inside the details remain types: the ruined banker, the starved miner, the dispossessed farmer, the unemployed Japanese hikikomori. They appear, deliver the moral lesson the chapter requires, and disappear. Lewis in *The Big Short* could give you a hedge fund manager's voice in three sentences; here, Edward Backwell is "Backwell," a name attached to a balance sheet, even when the chapter is asking him to carry pages of narrative weight. Keynes at Bretton Woods, whose collapse on the hotel stairs is the book's most cinematic scene, never quite emerges as a person rather than a position.

Fourth, the politics. The author writes that the book "makes these patterns explicit without prescribing solutions." This is not quite true. The Currency School, Lord Overstone, Churchill, the 1971 Bretton Woods architects, the eurozone designers, the 2008 QE recipients — every villain in the book is a creditor, an asset-holder, or someone serving creditor interests. Every hero — Attwood, Keynes, Roosevelt, Bernanke — is a flexibility advocate. This is broadly defensible; the historical evidence does run that direction. But the book's tone of permanent indictment, its reflex of detecting "institutional capture" wherever orthodox economics has prevailed, will read to the *Times* reader who has lived through 2021's inflation as advocacy, not history. The qualifications that should have been load-bearing — that supply-side explanations of the post-COVID inflation are contested, that public-choice critiques of fiscal flexibility have force — are made, fairly, in late chapters, but they arrive after the reader has been told, repeatedly, that worry about deficits is "the lineal descendant of the Currency School's program."

A final, smaller complaint, but a telling one for a book this dependent on its archive: the apparatus is uneven. Footnotes proliferate to the point of distraction (¹⁷ᶜ ¹⁷ᵈ ¹⁷ᶠ in a single sentence), and the manuscript still carries internal cues — "(See Figure 1.1)," "(See Figure 7.2)" — for figures that may or may not survive into the finished book. A reader who has been promised "the cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, preserved in a private collection in Bochum, Germany" and "Archivio di Stato di Prato, Fondo Datini, pp. 1146" deserves a cleaner editorial finish than this.

What is the book worth, all told? More than its weaknesses suggest. The argument is correct in its essentials, the historical sweep is genuine, and the connections drawn — between 1672 and 2008, between Edward III's silver problem and Greece's 2010 euro crisis, between Newton's miscalculation and Bitcoin's 21-million coin cap — are the kind of pattern recognition that makes monetary history live. A reader who comes to it after Graeber's *Debt* will find a more institutionally serious account; a reader who comes to it after Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* will find a deeper historical foundation for arguments Kelton makes mostly from theory. A reader who comes to it after Ahamed will miss Ahamed's narrative grace.

**Verdict: Qualified recommend.** The book earns its scope on argument; it loses ground on prose. A serious editor with a sharp pencil, prepared to cut twenty percent of the bolded definitions and a third of the recapitulations, could turn a worthy manuscript into the trade book it wants to be.

**Pull quote:** *"A bracing, occasionally brilliant argument that money has always been a relationship rather than a thing — burdened by a textbook's instinct to explain what a popular history should trust its readers to grasp."*

## 2. Skeptical mainstream economist

# Reviewer Critique: *Money, Trust, and Power*

The manuscript advertises itself as steelmanning the orthodox tradition. After reading the framing chapters, the gold-era chapters, and the modern fiat chapters with that promise in mind, I find the steelmans uneven. Some are genuine improvements over the typical heterodox rendering. Others are gestures. A few are caricatures dressed up in citation. Let me work through the specific cases the author asked me to assess.

**The Currency School and the Bullionist case (Ch. 7).** This is the strongest performance in the book. Section "The Bullionist Controversy" (lines 60–67) actually concedes that Ricardo, Overstone, and Torrens "had a serious case" — wartime inflation was real, the assignat collapse was real, and the temptation to monetize war finance recurs whenever a sovereign has a captive issuer. The author even concedes that the Currency School error — equating note issue with the money supply — became visible only after 1844. This is intellectually honest, the prose I'd expect a careful Bullionist defender to write. The Keynes-on-Churchill move at line 155 — distinguishing the gold standard from the *parity* chosen — is exactly the right concession. So far, so fair.

The trouble is what immediately follows. By the time we reach the closing pages of Chapter 7 (lines 177–181), and continuously through Chapter 8, the concession is rhetorically retracted. The deflation is "designed" to "transfer real purchasing power from debtors to creditors" (Ch. 8, line 5; lines 113–144). The Currency School's victory becomes "institutional capture" by Overstone (Ch. 8, lines 91–103). The 1844 Act becomes "tragic error" full stop (line 162). Where Chapter 7 said the Bullionists had real targets, Chapter 8 reverts to the morality play in which they are creditor agents. The two chapters do not reconcile. The author wants credit for steelmanning while ultimately delivering the same distributional indictment Hudson and Wray have written for thirty years. A reader who notices the seam will conclude the steelman was performative.

**Volcker (Ch. 12).** This is the chapter the author should be most worried about. The Volcker treatment is two-faced. Lines 207–211 frame the disinflation as "a political choice to prioritize price stability over employment, protecting creditors at workers' expense." Lines 247–267 detail the Hagedorn farm bankruptcy and the Rust Belt collapse with novelistic intensity. Then, almost in apology, lines 249 and 267 add a paragraph conceding that wage-price expectations had de-anchored, that incomes policy and energy reform had "already declined" politically, and that "better choices required institutional cooperation that did not then exist." This concession is the right one — but it is fifteen lines against three pages of concentrated cruelty narration. The Hagedorn passage frames Volcker's choice as "men in Washington" deciding the distributional cost was acceptable. That is not a fair description of someone who watched 1979 inflation expectations and concluded that *not* breaking them was the larger cruelty. The Bernanke-style apology is given to the 1929–33 Fed (line 137 of Ch. 7) but never to the alternative — that Volcker prevented an Argentinean inflationary regime in the United States. There is no engagement with the literature on the Great Moderation, no reckoning with what the disinflation enabled for the next twenty-five years. The chapter cites no Goodfriend, no Clarida-Gali-Gertler, no Hetzel. The verdict ("the brutal one") is delivered without weighing the counterfactual seriously. This is a steelman in name only.

**The 2021–22 inflation (Ch. 12, lines 9–35).** Here the author has clearly responded to a previous reviewer — there is an explicit "honest synthesis" paragraph (line 31) that concedes supply explains roughly half, demand a third to a half, that Blanchard's and Summers's February 2021 warnings "were not vindicated as exclusive explanations… but they were not refuted either," and that "large fiscal expansions during periods of constrained supply translate disproportionately into prices." That is a real concession and I credit it. But the surrounding architecture undoes it. The chapter title still says fiat money "held"; the lead numbers (60% supply, 50% capacity constraints) come first and loudest; the European comparison gets one clause; the New York Fed two-thirds-demand finding is cited and then immediately framed as compatible with a "productive-capacity framework" that, by the author's own logic, should have predicted *exactly* the inflation that occurred when the ARP hit a constrained economy. If productive capacity is the binding constraint, and fiscal stimulus was three to five times the output gap (Blanchard's actual 2021 estimate, which the chapter does not quote), then a productive-capacity theorist should have been *first* to predict the inflation, not last to concede it. The framework is being asked to do the work of explaining both why 2008 didn't inflate *and* why 2021 did, and the author never confronts that this is precisely the orthodox monetarist-plus-Phillips-curve story with different vocabulary.

**Buchanan-Wagner and Sargent-Wallace (Ch. 12, lines 279–285).** This is the most surprising section. The "public-choice critique deserves an answer" passage is genuinely good. It accurately summarizes *Democracy in Deficit*, names Niskanen, cites unpleasant monetarist arithmetic, and cites Cochrane's FTPL. The proposed institutional architecture — symmetric fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils, automatic tighteners, sunset clauses — is responsive. I disagree that it is *adequate*; the political-economy literature suggests these rules are evaded systematically, which is Buchanan's whole point. But this is at least an argument rather than a dismissal. Credit where due.

**The FTPL treatment.** Cited (line 283) but not engaged. The chapter mentions Cochrane in one sentence and moves on. FTPL is a serious challenger to the productive-capacity story precisely because it says inflation can occur without exceeding capacity if expected surpluses don't back outstanding debt. The author never confronts this. Chapter 7 even invokes "the fiscal theory of the price level" approvingly at line 47 — as supporting *chartalism* — which is a misreading. Cochrane's FTPL is not a friendly cousin of the tax-drives-currency view; it is a price-level-determination theory that generates *inflation* from unbacked deficits, which is the opposite of the book's reassurance. Treating these as compatible is cartoonish.

**Empirical claims requiring scrutiny.**

- Ch. 7, line 45: claims the Restriction's 2.6× note expansion produced only 30–40% inflation "because" of taxation. That is a single-country pre-post comparison with no counterfactual. Britain also had massive real output growth, war-driven savings, and forced lending. The chartalist mechanism is asserted, not identified.
- Ch. 8, line 128: the £160 million wealth transfer figure assumes 200% debt-to-GDP. Mitchell's figures put British public debt at roughly £840M against nominal GDP estimates that vary considerably; calling this "200%" is the high end of the range and is not flagged as such.
- Ch. 12, line 161: the claim that 98% of German government spending in November 1923 "came from printing" is in the right neighborhood but is presented as if it proves hyperinflation is a state-capacity problem, not a monetary one. The Reichsbank's behavior was a monetary choice taken under fiscal pressure; that's what monetarists have always said.
- Epilogue, Brazil/Argentina: Brazil's debt is ~75% of GDP, not 90%, and Brazil's real has been one of the worst-performing EM currencies of the past decade. Citing Brazil as a sovereignty success while ignoring its 2015–16 inflation overshoot, its central-bank independence struggles, and its 2002 sovereign debt scare reads as cherry-picking.

**Does the book ever concede orthodoxy might be partly right?** Yes — in three places. The Friedman-Schwartz concession at Ch. 7 line 139 ("the core Friedman–Schwartz claim has become the operating premise of every major central bank, and it was the explicit doctrine Bernanke applied in 2008–2009") is excellent. The Austrian/monetarist concession at Ch. 12 line 67 ("the critics were not crazy") is good. The public-choice answer at Ch. 12 lines 279–285 is good. That is real. But these concessions are local; the framing chapters (Preface, Ch. 1) and the Epilogue treat the orthodox view as a creditor-serving ideology that "served powerful interests and was deliberately constructed through intellectual and political campaigns" (Preface, line 36). The book cannot have it both ways — orthodoxy as serious intellectual tradition with genuine warrant *and* orthodoxy as engineered creditor propaganda. The author needs to choose, or to write much harder transitions between the two registers.

**Verdict: partial.** The Currency School and public-choice steelmans are genuine; the Volcker and FTPL treatments are not; the 2021 inflation chapter has been improved under pressure but the framework still does not bear the weight the author asks of it. A serious orthodox reviewer will find the book's most-cited claims — that fiat "held" in 2008–2020, that 2021 was supply-driven, that the gold standard was creditor capture — defensible only if the orthodox response is straw-manned, and the book's better moments show the author can do better when he wants to.

## 3. Big 5 acquisitions editor — internal memo

**INTERNAL ACQUISITIONS MEMO**
**RE:** *Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work*
**Length:** ~67,000 words / 12 chapters + preface + epilogue

---

**1. The book in one paragraph**

A 5,000-year history of money organized around a single contrarian thesis: money is not a commodity but a credit relationship enforced by institutions, and taxation — not gold — is what gives currency its value. The author moves from Mesopotamian temple ledgers and English tally sticks through the Bank of England's 1694 founding, Newton's accidental gold standard, the Bank Restriction, Churchill's 1925 blunder, Bretton Woods, Nixon's 1971 closure of the gold window, 2008, COVID, and Bitcoin. The structure is chronological, didactic, and argument-driven. It is essentially a popular history written in the MMT/chartalist tradition — sympathetic to Keynes and Thornton, withering on the Currency School, with running parallels between gold-standard creditor capture and the modern "household budget" framing of fiscal policy.

**2. Comps**

Closest comp is Felix Martin's *Money: The Unauthorised Biography* (Knopf, 2014) — same thesis, same structural beats, sold modestly (50–80k hardcover globally, my guess). Then David Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (Melville House, 2011) — the obvious lodestar, 250k+ across editions, but Graeber had the anthropologist credentials and the Occupy moment. Stephanie Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* (PublicAffairs, 2020) is the live commercial proof point for chartalism — NYT bestseller, ~150k hardcover. Adjacent: Saifedean Ammous's *The Bitcoin Standard* (the book this manuscript is implicitly arguing against, ~500k+ copies); Mehrling's *The New Lombard Street* (academic, low sales); Eichengreen's *Globalizing Capital*; Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* (Pulitzer, 250k+, but that's narrative-driven). The honest read: this sits between Martin and Kelton with Graeber's structure. Realistic ceiling 40–80k hardcover with the right author platform; floor 8–15k without one.

**3. Target reader**

Educated economic-history reader — *Atlantic*/*New Yorker*/*FT Weekend* subscriber, the *Lords of Finance* and *Debt* buyer. Significant secondary market in MMT-curious progressives (the Kelton readership), crypto-skeptics, and finance-adjacent professionals who read for argument. Will get hostile reviews from Austrians, *WSJ* edit page, hard-money/Bitcoin Twitter — that's a feature for the core audience but bounds the trade-paper coverage. Not a general-reader breakout in the *Sapiens* sense; the chartalism-vs-metallism debate is too inside.

**4. Marketing hooks**

**Strong:** The Newton-as-accidental-architect-of-the-gold-standard frame in Chapter 6 is genuinely fresh and cover-line-ready. "Money is a relationship, not a thing" is the elevator pitch and it lands. The 1797–1821 Bank Restriction as the buried natural experiment that proves fiat works is the book's best single argument and historians will cite it. The set-piece scenes — Datini's 1399 bill of exchange, Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn, Harry Todd's 17-month lockout, the Hagedorn farm, the Klemperer salt herring — are vivid, well-sourced, and the Todd material in particular is the book's emotional core. The recurring gold-standard-to-eurozone-to-Bitcoin parallels are timely.

**Weak:** "5,000 Years of X" in the title cues Graeber and invites unfavorable comparison. The MMT framing is unannounced but obvious by Chapter 5 and will be weaponized in reviews — the manuscript doesn't earn the right to the conclusions it asserts because it doesn't seriously engage Rogoff, Reinhart, or the post-2021 inflation revisionism until very late. Chapter 12 partially fixes this with the Buchanan/Wagner/Sargent-Wallace concession, but it reads as bolted on. There is no marquee author quote to anchor jacket copy.

**5. Structural concerns**

The book is too long for the argument. The thesis is established by end of Chapter 5 and then re-litigated in every subsequent chapter. Chapters 1, 3, and 5 substantially overlap on the taxation-creates-demand mechanism. Chapter 8 (the Forgetting) is the analytical core but bloated — the Attwood biographical material and the Overstone institutional-capture section say the same thing twice. Chapters 10 and 11 could be merged: Bretton Woods + the breaking is one arc, not two. The case-study set pieces (Amat-Sîn, Datini, Godeffroy, Heslop, Cantwell, Lebesque, Kantor, Kono, the Klemperers, the Hagedorns, the Preußes) are the book's best feature *and* its biggest discipline problem — they accumulate beyond what the argument requires, and several feel like research the author couldn't bear to cut. The 2021 inflation discussion in Chapter 12 is the most intellectually honest section and should probably move earlier as a framing device. The Epilogue's CBDC material is underdeveloped relative to its prominence.

Also: the footnote density is academic-press, not trade. Many chapters carry 50+ notes with sub-letter suffixes (²⁷ʰ⁻¹⁰), suggesting heavy revision history bleeding into the final text. Needs a copy edit pass that rationalizes apparatus.

**6. Platform / author needs**

Submission does not specify the author. This matters enormously. With a tenured economic historian or a recognizable financial journalist (think Adam Tooze, Mehrling, Tett, Lewis), this is a viable mid-six-figure acquisition. With an unknown, the ceiling drops to low five figures and we'd need a co-author or a marquee foreword (Kelton, Tooze, Eichengreen) to position. The argument is contrarian enough that author authority is doing serious load-bearing work. Required minimum: a substantive op-ed footprint (FT, *NYT*, *Atlantic*), a podcast circuit ready to host (Odd Lots, Macro Musings, Ezra Klein), and ideally an institutional affiliation that gives reviewers something to cite. Without platform, this is a university-press book.

**7. Recommendation: yes-with-revisions, contingent on author platform**

The manuscript is real. The argument is coherent, the research is genuine, and the set pieces are the best I've read on this material since *Lords of Finance*. The Newton chapter alone justifies serious consideration. But it isn't ready, and it's the wrong length.

Conditional yes if: (a) author has a credible platform or we can attach one; (b) author will cut 12–15k words, primarily by consolidating Chapters 10–11, trimming the human case studies to the four or five strongest, and tightening Chapter 8; (c) author will engage the public-choice and post-2021-inflation critiques in the body rather than as a late concession; (d) we retitle to drop the "5,000 Years" formulation. Pass if author is unwilling to cut or lacks platform — at 67k words and this argumentative posture, an unknown author cannot carry it commercially, and we'd be publishing into a market where Kelton already owns the chartalist shelf and Graeber owns the long-history shelf.

My instinct: offer at the low end of the mid-list range with a development editor attached. The book that emerges from a serious revision could be a *Lords of Finance*-tier title. The book as submitted is a strong 7 looking for the editor who will make it an 8.

## 4. Conservative / sound-money critic

# Steelmen Made of Straw: A Manuscript That Tells On Itself

*A review of* Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work

There is a particular kind of book that announces its even-handedness in the preface and then spends sixty thousand words demonstrating it has none. *Money, Trust, and Power* is such a book. The author claims he will "steelman the gold standard, the Austrian school, and the Currency School." A steelman is the strongest version of an opponent's argument, faithfully reconstructed. What this manuscript delivers is a series of effigies, dressed plausibly enough to fool a reader who never met the originals, then methodically set on fire.

Begin with the Preface, because the author does. Money, we are told on page one, "is not a thing but a relationship," and gold "was never the foundation; it was merely a convenient fiction." This is the thesis. Notice that it is not argued; it is announced. By page three the household-budget framing — the idea that a government, like a family, must acquire money before spending it — is dismissed as ideological residue serving "powerful interests." By page four we have moved from monetary theory to the claim that monetary orthodoxy itself is an instrument of class warfare, "deliberately constructed through intellectual and political campaigns." This is not the language of a writer about to give Hayek and Mises a fair hearing. It is the language of a writer who has decided, before chapter one, who the villains are.

The gold standard chapters confirm it. Chapter 6 frames classical gold as "Newton's accident" — a Treasury under-correction in 1717 that congealed by historical inertia into a global system. Fine, as far as path-dependence stories go. But the chapter then catalogs gold's costs (deflationary bias, distributional regressivity, "hidden subsidy" to the metropole) without ever crediting the offsetting goods the Cato and Reason traditions have spent fifty years documenting. Where is the price-level stability of 1820–1914, in which a pound bought roughly the same basket at the end of the century as at the start? Where is the constraint on sovereign profligacy — the simple fact that under classical gold no major government ran the kinds of chronic peacetime deficits that have become routine since 1971? Where is Selgin's work on the productivity norm, or White's on free banking under gold? Absent. The "three convergent forces" section purports to explain why gold won and instead explains only why the author thinks it shouldn't have.

Chapter 7's treatment of the Bank Restriction is where the performance becomes embarrassing. The author wants us to read 1797–1821 as proof that "paper holds when the state's taxation authority is credible, regardless of the metal in any vault." He mentions the assignats and Continentals in a single defensive paragraph and moves on. He does not engage the obvious Austrian rejoinder: that Britain's wartime paper held *because everyone expected resumption*, and resumption is exactly what differentiated the pound from the assignat. The credibility was credibility *of the gold promise*, not credibility of taxation in the abstract. Strip out the expected return to metal and the experiment proves nothing about modern fiat. The author waves this away by asserting, in the chapter's closing passage, that the 2020 Fed expansion "delivered the same answer" — as though a year of suppressed measured CPI settled the question. By 2021 the answer had changed. The author knows this; Chapter 12 admits it; Chapter 7 pretends otherwise.

Chapter 8 is where the manuscript abandons the pretense altogether. Thomas Attwood is canonized as a proto-Keynesian martyr; Lord Overstone is presented as the architect of an "institutional capture" that "transferred wealth upward." The Currency School's actual argument — that an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will always over-issue, and that the assignat collapse was the empirical proof — is acknowledged in a single grudging paragraph and then drowned in the language of class war. Ricardo is treated not as the systematic theorist of *Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency* but as the front man for a creditor cabal. Anyone who has actually read the Bullionist literature will recognize this as caricature.

The Volcker chapter is worse. Chapter 11 calls the disinflation "demand suppression" that "redistributed the cost of the supply shock from price-paying creditors to wage-earning workers." This is technically true and morally inverted. The Great Inflation was destroying the savings of every wage-earner with a passbook account; Volcker's recession ended it. The author concedes, almost grudgingly, that "inflation expectations de-anchoring permanently" was the alternative — and then spends the rest of the chapter portraying the Hagedorn farm and the Youngstown mills as victims of a policy that had no good alternatives. *Of course* it had no good alternatives. That is the point conservatives have been making for forty years: once a fiat regime lets inflation run, the cure is brutal. The author treats this as an indictment of Volcker rather than of the regime that produced the disease.

Chapter 12 is the tell. Bitcoin is dispatched in two paragraphs: the 21-million cap is "gold's mathematical heir," Terra/Luna proves it, FTX proves it again, end of discussion. There is no engagement with Saifedean Ammous, no engagement with Selgin's work on rule-bound monetary regimes, no acknowledgment that the monetary critique embedded in the Bitcoin whitepaper — that discretionary central banks have a documented track record of debasement — is the *same critique* Hayek made in *Denationalisation of Money*. The author conflates "Bitcoin as currency today" with "the monetary critique Bitcoin embodies" and then treats the failure of the first as refutation of the second. It is a category error a sympathetic editor would catch.

The public-choice section deserves a separate verdict. To his credit, the author finally names Buchanan and Wagner near the end of Chapter 12, summarizes *Democracy in Deficit* with reasonable accuracy, gestures at Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane, and concedes that "the lesson the gold standard's defenders had partly right — that you cannot trust office-holders to stop printing when inflation arrives — survives the move to fiat." This is the one moment in 67,000 words where a steelman actually stands. Then he immediately re-buries it under proposals for "an independent fiscal council" and "constitutional fiscal rules with built-in symmetry" — the same rules-based discipline gold once provided, now reinvented as if discovered. A reader sympathetic to Buchanan will note the irony. The author does not.

Does the book ever concede that fiat has real failure modes? Sparsely. Chapter 12 names Weimar, Zimbabwe, Venezuela — and immediately defines them as "state capacity failures, not monetary failures," a distinction that does no work. Every fiat collapse is by definition a state-capacity failure; that is the Austrian point. The chronic peacetime deficits of the post-1971 West, the asset-price inflation that has hollowed out the saving class, the cumulative dollar debasement of roughly 87 percent since Nixon — these go unaddressed or are reframed as "distributional choices" that better institutions could fix.

**Verdict:** This is a competent piece of monetary history written by an author whose priors will not let him steelman his opponents. The historical research is real; the polemical framing is relentless. Send it back. Let the author rewrite the gold-standard chapters with a copy of Selgin's *Less Than Zero* on the desk, the crypto chapter with Ammous open, and the Volcker chapter with the question *what would have happened if he hadn't?* answered honestly. Until then, the steelmans are scarecrows, and any reader who has met the real thing will see straight through them.

## 5. Working economic historian — essay-review

**The Past as Polemic: A Five-Thousand-Year History with a Single Argument**

*Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work*. By the author. 67,000 words. 12 chapters.

This is a sweeping, briskly written, and frequently moving narrative history of money — from the temple of Gula at Larsa to Jerome Powell's keystrokes — that wants very badly to be the heir to Hudson, Graeber, Mehrling, and Tooze in a single volume. It is also, beneath the storytelling, a polemic in support of a particular reading of chartalism and its descendants in Modern Monetary Theory. The book's vices and virtues both flow from that double commitment.

Let me say what works. The set pieces are excellent and will carry general readers a long way. The Old Babylonian loan tablet of Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn (Ch. 1) is reconstructed with care and gives the chartalist case archaeological texture. The Datini bill of December 12, 1399, and its *recambium* (Ch. 2) is one of the cleanest expositions of the *cambium-non-est-mutuum* fiction I have read for a non-specialist audience. The Stop of the Exchequer set piece on Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn (Ch. 4) is genuinely fine narrative history. The Harry Todd family during the 1926 lockout (Ch. 9) and the Klemperer–Preuß diaries on Weimar hyperinflation (Ch. 12) lift the human cost off the page in a way that distinguishes this book from drier monetary histories. William Paterson's biography (Ch. 5), with its precise tracking of the projector's career from Hampstead Water through Darien, is a small triumph.

Now the harder things.

First, the historiographic posture. The author repeatedly presents as fresh discovery what is in fact the standard chartalist–credit-theory line that has been the dominant English-language scholarly tradition on the origins of money since Innes and Knapp, recovered by Goodhart, Wray, Hudson, and Ingham, and made famous by Graeber. The preface's repeated rhetoric of "systematic forgetting" and "buried truth" is overheated. Among working economic historians of money — as opposed to undergraduate textbooks — the credit-theory view has been *winning* for two decades. Treating it as suppressed knowledge is salesmanship. The book's footnotes, where I could spot-check them, lean heavily on Hudson, Wray, and Graeber for the ancient sections and on secondary syntheses (Frisby, popular Bernanke, Eichengreen) for the modern sections. There are very few archival citations one could not have found in the standard secondary literature.

Second, factual problems and oversimplifications I noted on a careful reading.

*Mesopotamia.* The Ur III *bala* figures in Ch. 1 (188 million liters annually, 500 administrators, Puzrish-Dagan) are presented with a confidence the underlying scholarship (Steinkeller, Garfinkle, Sharlach) does not warrant. The figure of "10,000 tablets" understates the corpus by an order of magnitude — the Drehem archive runs to tens of thousands. The *amargi/andurarum* distinction is collapsed into "jubilees" in a way Hudson would not endorse. Schmandt-Besserat's tokens are invoked twice on opposite sides of the chartalist–metallist argument without acknowledging the contradiction.

*Champagne fairs and bills of exchange.* The book mentions Champagne only obliquely (Ch. 2). For a chapter built on bills, this is a serious omission: the fairs were the institutional matrix in which bills generalized in the thirteenth century. The narrative jumps from Italian counting-houses to Bruges without explaining how Genoese and Sienese bankers integrated the Champagne cycle. Munro and Bell are absent from the apparatus.

*The Wisselbank.* The treatment (Ch. 2) is competent but compressed and slightly off the current scholarship. Quinn and Roberds — the standard reference now — would describe the Wisselbank's late-eighteenth-century failure not primarily as "internal corruption" but as the tension between the agio regime and unsecured advances to the VOC and Amsterdam municipality, with a critical inflection in 1781–82 when the agio collapsed. The book gets the direction right but the mechanism imprecise, and Quinn–Roberds are not cited.

*Paterson and 1694.* The Paterson chapter (Ch. 5) is, as I said, the strongest single piece of biographical writing in the book. But the famous "benefit of interest on all moneys which it creates out of nothing" line is a quotation Paterson himself disowned and whose attribution has been disputed since the nineteenth century — see Clapham. The book uses it as load-bearing without flagging the problem.

*Newton 1717.* The Ch. 6 account of Newton's adjustment as a "2.33% miscalculation" that "accidentally created the gold standard" is the kind of just-so story economic historians have been pushing back against for thirty years. Brazilian gold flows, the Asian silver pull, and the pre-1717 trajectory of bimetallism in England all matter, and the chapter does mention them — but the framing of one Treasury report as the fulcrum of two centuries of monetary history is the Carlyle–"great Newton" mode at its most seductive and least defensible. Redish, Selgin, and Velde have done the careful work; they are barely cited.

*Bullionist controversy and the Bank Charter Act.* Ch. 7 and Ch. 8 are the book's intellectual centerpieces and the most polemical. The treatment of Ricardo, Thornton, and the Bullion Report is fair; Thornton's stock has been rising for years and the book correctly elevates him. The treatment of Attwood and Birmingham is a deliberate rehabilitation, presented as long-overdue. It is not. Frank Fetter, Boyd Hilton, Anna Gambles, and Matthew Hale have all written sympathetically on Birmingham; the claim that Attwood was "erased" needs serious qualification. The Overstone capture narrative is plausible but oversold — an Overstone-as-Moriarty account that flattens the actual diversity of Currency-School and Banking-School arguments. The book repeatedly asserts that the Bank Charter Act 1844 "embedded gold orthodoxy in law" while simultaneously noting that the Act was suspended in 1847, 1857, and 1866. Both can be true, but the second fact is in some tension with the first claim's force.

*Interwar.* Ch. 9 on Churchill's 1925 return is by-the-numbers Keynes and Moggridge. The Norman–Strong–Schacht triangle that drove the late-1920s gold standard — Sayers, Ahamed, Eichengreen — is thinly handled. Schacht barely appears. France's *poincarés* and the legal 35% cover ratio are correctly identified, but Mouré's work on the Bank of France is unused. Irwin's quantification of the gold-sink hypothesis is invoked correctly.

*Bretton Woods.* Ch. 10's account of Keynes vs. White leans on Steil's *Battle of Bretton Woods* without saying so, and accepts Steil's procedural-domination thesis more uncritically than the recent literature (James, Helleiner, Boughton) would warrant. The Robbins quotations are accurate but Steil's framing of the conference as a fait accompli is contested. The Triffin dilemma is correctly summarized.

*Nixon shock and after.* Ch. 11 is competent. The British "$3 billion" request of August 11, 1971 is the standard story going back to Volcker's memoir; the book repeats it without engaging Garber's argument that the request was for cover, not bullion. Volcker disinflation in Ch. 12 is given a more honest hearing than I expected — the book lets Volcker make his case before judging him — and the Hagedorn farm vignette is genuinely powerful.

*2021–22 inflation.* Ch. 12's sectoral-bottlenecks discussion is the book at its most careful. The Shapiro, Bernanke–Blanchard, IMF, and New York Fed studies are correctly cited, the Blanchard–Summers warnings are correctly *not* dismissed, and the synthesis — supply roughly half, demand a third to a half, with sectoral bottlenecks doing the binding — is defensible. This is the chapter where the productive-capacity framework is stress-tested, and the author's willingness to qualify is admirable.

Third, two structural complaints. The book repeatedly stages contemporary reenactments — Greece in 2010 as Edward III, China's Treasuries as France's gold demands, Bitcoin as Ricardo — that work as rhetoric but flatten the differences that matter. And the prose has an unfortunate habit of declaring victories ("the verdict has been delivered," "settled the debate forever") that economic history rarely permits.

**Verdict: competent synthesis with polemical ambition.** This is not an original contribution to scholarship. It is not for specialists; the apparatus is too thin, the secondary literature too selectively deployed, and the argumentative framing too pre-decided. But it is a *good* synthesis — better than most popular monetary histories, with vivid set pieces and an ear for the human cost — and the chartalist tradition it represents deserves an articulate popularizer. With a more honest preface that acknowledges its place in an existing scholarly conversation rather than claiming to recover lost truth, and with tighter footnoting where the spot-checks above turned up gaps, this book could become a standard recommendation for the educated lay reader. As it stands, it will sell well and irritate the specialists it claims to be reaching past.

# Synthesis

## Verdict scoreboard

| Persona | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| NYT reviewer | Qualified recommend |
| Skeptical mainstream economist | Partial steelman |
| Big 5 acquisitions editor | Yes-with-revisions, contingent on platform |
| Conservative / sound-money critic | Send it back — steelmans are scarecrows |
| Economic historian | Competent synthesis with polemical ambition |

No one says strong recommend. No one says pass outright. The book lands in a wide middle: serious, real, not yet ready.

## Where the personas converge (high-signal)

These are the issues flagged by **three or more** independent reviewers. Address these first.

**1. The steelmans wobble between Ch. 7 and Ch. 8 — and elsewhere.** The economist, the conservative, and the NYT reviewer all hit the same seam: the framing chapters concede orthodoxy has a real case, then later chapters rhetorically retract the concession. Ch. 8's "Great Forgetting" is the worst offender. The economist quotes lines 60–67 (good) against lines 91–144 (relapse). The conservative reads the same arc as performance. The NYT reviewer phrases it as "the qualifications that should have been load-bearing arrive after the reader has been told repeatedly who the villains are." This is not a tone problem; it is a structural problem. Either the steelmans are honest and the morality-play language has to come down, or the morality-play language is what the book actually believes and the steelmans are decoration. **Pick one.**

**2. The Volcker chapter does not weigh the counterfactual.** The economist and the conservative independently land the same punch: the Hagedorn passage is novelistic and devastating, but the question *what would have happened without Volcker* is never seriously answered. No Goodfriend, no Clarida-Galí-Gertler, no engagement with the Great Moderation as Volcker's downstream achievement. Even the NYT reviewer notices the asymmetry — Bernanke's apology to 1929–33 is given; the parallel apology to the alternative-to-Volcker is not. The historian is the one outlier here, calling the Volcker treatment "more honest than I expected."

**3. The book is too long for its argument and repeats itself.** The NYT reviewer and the editor both flag this directly, and the historian flags it as "declaring victories" too often. Specific overlap zones: Chs. 1/3/5 on taxation-creates-demand; Chs. 10/11 on Bretton Woods; Ch. 8's Attwood/Overstone material doubled. The editor wants 12–15k cut. The NYT reviewer wants 20% of bolded definitions and a third of the recapitulations gone. Same diagnosis.

**4. The book is a chartalist tract that pretends not to know it.** The economist and the historian both call this out. The historian: the credit-theory view "has been *winning* for two decades" — treating it as suppressed knowledge is salesmanship. The economist: the Preface frames orthodoxy as "deliberately constructed creditor propaganda," which the steelmans cannot survive. The conservative reads the same tell from his side. **The Preface is doing damage** — it inoculates exactly the readers who would otherwise be persuaded by the historical chapters.

**5. Apparatus problems — footnotes, figures, attribution.** The NYT reviewer notices the dense suffixed footnotes and unresolved "(See Figure 1.1)" cues; the editor calls it academic-press density bleeding into a trade book; the historian spot-checks specific citations and turns up real gaps (Quinn–Roberds on Wisselbank; Munro/Bell on Champagne fairs; Mouré on Bank of France; the disputed Paterson "out of nothing" quote presented without flag; Redish/Selgin/Velde under-cited on Newton 1717). This is the cheapest fix on the list and the one most directly under the author's control.

## Where the personas diverge

- **The historian is the most generous on Volcker; the conservative and economist are harshest.** The historian reads the Hagedorn vignette as the chapter "letting Volcker make his case before judging him." The other two read it as the chapter spending three pages on cruelty narration against fifteen lines of concession.
- **The NYT reviewer cares about prose and character; nobody else does.** The "Backwell is just a name attached to a balance sheet" critique is real — but it's a popular-history concern, not a scholarly or commercial one. The editor, by contrast, calls the set pieces the book's best feature.
- **The conservative is the only one who reads the Bitcoin section as a category error.** The other reviewers don't engage Ch. 12's crypto pages much. If the goal is to land with conservative/libertarian readers at all, this is the section that loses them — two paragraphs is not enough.
- **The Newton chapter cuts both ways.** The NYT reviewer and the editor call it the book's fresh, cover-line-ready argument. The historian calls it a "Carlyle-mode just-so story" that flattens decades of careful work. Both can be true: it sells the book to general readers and exposes the author to specialist mockery.

## Highest-priority fixes before sending out

In rough order of return-on-effort:

1. **Rewrite the Preface.** Drop the "deliberately constructed by powerful interests" framing. Replace with a calmer claim: that the credit theory of money has been the scholarly mainstream for decades, but has not penetrated public discourse, which is what this book attempts. This single change neutralizes the conservative's strongest opening attack and the historian's "salesmanship" complaint, and lets every subsequent steelman read as honest rather than tactical.

2. **Fix the Volcker chapter.** Add a serious counterfactual passage — what does Argentina-style entrenched inflation look like if expectations had de-anchored in 1980? Engage Goodfriend and the Great Moderation literature. Keep the Hagedorn vignette; balance it with a passage on the savers Volcker rescued. Three reviewers flag this; it's the single biggest credibility hole.

3. **Reconcile Ch. 7 and Ch. 8.** The Bullionist concession in Ch. 7 must survive the move into Ch. 8. Either soften the "creditor capture" language in Ch. 8 to match Ch. 7's intellectual generosity, or strengthen the Ch. 7 caveats so the reader is prepared for the harder Ch. 8 verdict. As written, the seam is visible.

4. **Cut 12–15k words.** Editor's prescription, seconded by NYT reviewer. Specifically: consolidate Chs. 10–11 (Bretton Woods + the breaking is one arc); reduce overlap on the taxation-creates-demand mechanism in Chs. 1/3/5; trim the case-study set pieces to four or five strongest; tighten Ch. 8.

5. **Rebuild the crypto pages in Ch. 12.** Two paragraphs is not engagement. Even briefly engaging Ammous, Hayek's *Denationalisation of Money*, and Selgin's rule-bound regimes work would change the chapter's reception among the readership it most needs to disarm.

6. **Apparatus pass.** Resolve the figure cues. Flatten suffix footnotes (¹⁷ᶜ ¹⁷ᵈ ¹⁷ᶠ). Spot-check the historian's specific cites: Quinn–Roberds on the Wisselbank, Munro/Bell on Champagne, Redish/Selgin/Velde on Newton, Mouré on the Bank of France, Garber on the British 1971 request, Clapham on the disputed Paterson quote. Add or qualify.

7. **Title.** "Five Thousand Years of X" cues Graeber and invites unfavorable comparison. The editor's note on this is correct. Consider dropping it or changing the formulation.

## What you should NOT do

- Do not lengthen. Every reviewer who comments on length wants less, not more.
- Do not add another late-chapter "concession" passage. The economist's diagnosis is exact: bolted-on concessions make the framing chapters look worse, not better. Concessions need to live in the framing or not at all.
- Do not over-engage Bitcoin. Two paragraphs is not enough; thirty pages would be too many. Three to five pages of honest engagement is the target.

## One thing the personas missed (or weighted lightly)

The conservative and the historian both touched it but neither pressed: **the book wants Bitcoin to be a category error and gold to be a historical accident, but its own mechanism — that institutions powerful enough to enforce credit relationships are what make money work — is precisely the critique Bitcoin advocates make of central banks (rule-bound institutions vs. discretionary ones). The book never confronts that its own framework, taken seriously, is at least partially a sound-money argument.** That is the unwritten chapter the most sympathetic critics will write for you if you don't write it yourself.
