## The calibrated heuristic on modern-parallel passages

The execution plan's punch list says "trim modern-parallel coda if present" for most chapters. That rule needs **judgment, not mechanical application**. The right calibration emerged after over-cutting the Paris-café-clearinghouse close in Ch.2 and the Antwerp-clearinghouses interjection mid-Ch.2, both of which had to be restored.

**Rule: a modern parallel earns its place when it teaches the reader something they probably don't know.** A non-economist reader doesn't know how a credit-card swipe at a Paris café actually clears, doesn't know that a CBDC's value comes from tax acceptance not blockchain scarcity, doesn't know that Lehman failed for the same structural reason the Wisselbank did. Those connections are the *point* of writing monetary history for a general reader — they're how the historical material starts feeling load-bearing rather than ornamental.

**A modern parallel loses its place when it's pure thesis-restating dressed in a contemporary example.** "The lesson resonates today" leads, "this is exactly how X works today" sentences inserted between two scenes to break flow, single-paragraph mid-chapter interjections that just say "the same principle applies now" without teaching mechanism. Those are the tics the v1/v2 personas correctly flagged.

The conservative critic's tic complaint was about **cumulative pattern across the whole book**, not about any single instance. Cutting them all uniformly is the wrong response. Pick the ones that teach; cut the ones that only restate.

**What to cut in remaining chapters:**
- Bolded didactic asides (still real, still tics)
- Substack-tic full-sentence emphases
- Pure thesis-restating that adds no new content
- Modern parallels that only restate the chapter's argument without teaching mechanism

**What to keep:**
- Modern parallels that explain how the historical mechanism shows up in something the reader uses or has heard of
- Vivid chapter-end pull-quote closes that ground the material in a contemporary image
- Glossary-style concept introductions (bolded first-introduction terms)
- Structural list/section labels in scene-setting devices (the "What Newton expected/got" / "The observation/calculation/innovation" enumeration patterns)
