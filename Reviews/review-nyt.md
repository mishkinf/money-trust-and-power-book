# The Ledger and the Lash: A Five-Thousand-Year Brief Against Gold

**By Helena Marsh-Whitfield**

*"Money, Trust, and Power: A Five-Thousand-Year History" — 70,000 words*

There is a particular kind of book that arrives at a particular kind of moment. With Bitcoin partisans drafting white papers in the language of holy war, central bankers arguing in earnest about programmable money, and a generation of voters who have lived through quantitative easing, COVID stimulus, and the Greek crisis without anyone giving them a coherent story about what money actually *is*, the case for a sweeping popular history of money is overwhelming. The shelves are not empty — Niall Ferguson, William Goetzmann, David Graeber, and most recently Stephanie Kelton have all tried — but the synthesis we want, one that runs from the temples of Uruk to the Federal Reserve's COVID balance sheet without losing either the human texture or the analytic spine, has not yet been written. This new manuscript wants to be that book. It nearly is.

The author's argument can be stated in a sentence, and is, repeatedly: money is not a thing but a relationship, sustained by institutions powerful enough to enforce obligations, and given value chiefly by the state's demand that taxes be paid in it. From that thesis everything follows. Gold was an accident — Newton's 2.38 percent miscalculation in 1717, helped along by Brazilian mining and British power — that hardened into orthodoxy because it served creditors. Every subsequent monetary crisis, from the 1821 deflation that ruined English farmers to Churchill's 1925 return to gold to the Fed's 2008 Bear Stearns weekend, was a rerun of the same play with different costumes. The villains are the bondholders and their tame economists; the heroes are Henry Thornton, Thomas Attwood, John Maynard Keynes, and a parade of obscure clerks who knew that loans create deposits long before any textbook said so.

It is, in other words, an unapologetic chartalist tract — a history of money written from the perspective of Modern Monetary Theory, with the gold standard cast as a hundred-and-fifty-year hostage situation. The author never uses the term MMT until the closing pages, which is canny: the case is built brick by historical brick, from Mesopotamian *amargi* to Iowa farm foreclosures, before the theoretical label is finally hung on it. By then, many readers will have nodded their way into a worldview they would have rejected on the dust jacket.

What's most striking is how often it works. The book's strengths are real and worth naming. The author has done the archival labor: we get the Datini bill of December 12, 1399, with exchange rates and counterparties; the Larsa loan tablet Kress 83 with its 33.33 percent interest and joint-and-several liability between mother and daughter; Edward Backwell's 666-folio ledger at the Sign of the Unicorn. The Stop of the Exchequer in 1672 — usually a footnote — becomes a fully realized financial 9/11, complete with Sir Robert Vyner reduced to thirty pence on the pound and dying broken-hearted in 1688. This is the kind of texture Liaquat Ahamed brought to *Lords of Finance* and Ed Conway to *The Summit*, and it earns the author the right to argue.

Better still are the human cameos sprinkled across what could have been a dry institutional history. Harry Todd, the Boldon collier whose family ate worse in the 1925 lockout than they would under wartime rationing twenty years later, is the book's emotional ballast — and a sharper indictment of Churchill than Skidelsky ever managed in three volumes on Keynes. Farmer A in Story County, Iowa, losing 551 acres to deflation engineered in Washington and Paris. Maureen McKenzie of Kirkwood, Missouri, foreclosed in 2008 by an option-ARM that inflated her principal each month she paid the minimum. Michinao Kono, the Kyoto University dropout who applied with 1,814 others for one of three clerical jobs in Takarazuka, Japan's wasted ice-age generation in a single statistic. The author understands, as Michael Lewis does, that monetary policy lives or dies by whether you can put a face on a basis point.

And there are killer lines. Of Newton's 1717 adjustment: "the most consequential accident in financial history." Of the gold standard's "automatic" adjustment mechanism: "self-regulating in the sense that a car with power steering is self-driving: the mechanism worked, but someone had to grip the wheel." Of the 1925 Churchill choice: "Honour, it turned out, was an abstraction purchased with other people's ruin." That last sentence is the book in miniature — and it is exactly the kind of line you wish the author had been willing to write more often, instead of repeating, with the patience of a man explaining a card trick to children, that "money is not a thing but a relationship" thirty-seven times across nineteen chapters.

Which brings us to the weaknesses, and they are serious. The first is structural. Each chapter opens with a present-day "hook" — Bitcoin, the eurozone, COVID stimulus, China's dollar trap — that telegraphs the historical lesson before the history is told. By Chapter 6 this becomes a tic; by Chapter 10 it is a liturgy. The reader is not trusted to draw the parallel, so the parallel is delivered, captioned, italicized, and underlined. Worse, the same handful of arguments are restated in nearly identical paragraphs across hundreds of pages. We are told no fewer than five times that English tally sticks worked because they could pay taxes; that loans create deposits; that the 1797–1821 Bank Restriction proved fiat money worked. The author's editor appears to have been on sabbatical.

The second weakness is intellectual. For a book that prides itself on naming the bondholders' interests behind orthodox economics, it is curiously incurious about the interests behind its own preferred view. Modern Monetary Theory has detractors who are not stooges of the rentier class — Larry Summers and Olivier Blanchard come to mind — and the political economy of who benefits from open-ended deficit finance is not seriously examined. The author treats the 2021–22 inflation as a clean validation of the productive-capacity principle, supply-chain bottlenecks doing the analytical heavy lifting; readers who lived through it may feel the explanation is complete only if you squint. When Thomas Palley's metallist critique is briefly and fairly summarized in Chapter 1, it is the only moment in the book where a serious opponent is allowed to speak in his own voice. He is then quietly dropped.

The third weakness is moral. The author writes with palpable, deserved fury about miners' wages and farmers' foreclosures, but the same fury is never turned on the inflation that destroyed German savings in 1923 or Argentine pensions in 2002. Weimar, Zimbabwe, and Venezuela are dispatched in a single comparative table as "state capacity failures" — true enough, but a book this attentive to the human cost of deflation owes its readers an equivalent attentiveness to the human cost of currency collapse. Eva Klemperer in the Dresden café gets two paragraphs; Harry Todd gets ten pages. The asymmetry is not accidental. It is the one place where the polemic becomes self-serving.

How does it stand against the field? It is not as elegant as Ferguson's *The Ascent of Money*, which remains the genre's gold standard for compression and wit. It lacks Graeber's anthropological reach in *Debt*, and it does not match Tooze's analytic precision in *Crashed* on the 2008 crisis. But it is more honest about distributional politics than any of them. It is more historically detailed than Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* and far more readable than Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters*, whose argument about interwar gold hoarding it largely reproduces and humanizes. If you imagine Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* rewritten by someone who had read Hudson on Mesopotamia and Kelton on fiat, you have something close to this book — minus the prose discipline that made Ahamed a Pulitzer winner.

**The verdict: recommend with reservations.** This is a serious book, in places a brilliant one, and the chapters on the goldsmith catastrophe, the Bullionist controversy, and the breaking of Bretton Woods belong on the same shelf as the standard works in the field. The author has earned the right to a polemic. But the polemic has eaten the history in places it should not have, and a tighter edit — fifteen thousand words shorter, three fewer "and that is exactly what is happening today" pivots per chapter — would have produced a classic. As it stands, this is a very good book carrying a great one inside it. Read it for the human stories, the archival depth, and the unfashionable courage to argue that money has always been political. Argue with it where you must. The argument is the point.

## For the author, not for publication

You have a real book here, and I wanted to tell you so without the qualifications a review demands. Three things, in descending order of importance.

First, trust your readers. The contemporary "hook" at the start of every chapter is the structural decision most likely to date this book within five years and the one most likely to irritate a thoughtful reader on first reading. The history is more interesting than the analogy. Let the parallels emerge; don't deliver them. Cut every "this is exactly what happened in 2008" sentence and you will lose nothing and gain authority.

Second, your villain is too clean. The bondholder-versus-worker frame works beautifully for 1821 and 1925, less well for 1971, and barely at all for 2021. When you reach for it anyway, the argument thins. The strongest version of your book accepts that fiat money creates *new* distributional problems — asset inflation, financialization, the rentier class reborn through QE rather than gold — that your own framework has trouble naming because they don't fit the creditor-versus-debtor template. You gesture at this in the epilogue. Lead with it.

Third, give the inflation hawks their day in court. One serious chapter on Weimar, Zimbabwe, and the 1970s — written with the same human texture you give Harry Todd — would inoculate the book against its most predictable criticism and make the chartalist case stronger, not weaker. Right now your strongest readers will close the book half-convinced and slightly suspicious. They should close it convinced.

You have written something real. Make it tighter, fairer, and shorter. It will outlast you.
