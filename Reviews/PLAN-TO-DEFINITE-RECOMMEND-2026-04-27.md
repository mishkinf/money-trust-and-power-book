# Plan: From "Qualified Recommend" to "Definite Recommend"

**Source:** five persona critiques, 2026-04-27 (`Reviews/PERSONA-CRITIQUES-2026-04-27.md`).

**Audience scope (revised):** this plan optimizes for *strong approve* from the three open-minded reviewers — NYT staff reviewer, Big 5 acquisitions editor, working economic historian. Ideological opponents (Cato/Reason conservative, orthodox-monetarist economist) are explicitly out of scope: no edit short of changing the thesis flips them, and trying to flip them costs effort that would be better spent earning a strong recommendation from readers who can be won.

**Working principle:** address every issue flagged by ≥3 personas first. Ignore the orthodox-economist-specific and conservative-specific demands unless they also serve open-minded readers. Do not lengthen — every reviewer who comments on length wants less.

**Why Phase 2 still matters even when ignoring the opponents:** the NYT reviewer used the phrase "permanent indictment" and called the book "advocacy, not history." The editor said the manuscript "doesn't earn the right to the conclusions it asserts." The historian said "polemical ambition." All three open-minded readers smell a thumb on the scale, even though none of them shares the orthodox priors. So the steelmans must be honest — but they can be lighter than what an actual orthodox reader would demand.

## Phase 1 — Reframe the Preface and thesis arc *(highest-leverage single change)*

**Diagnosis:** the Preface tells the conservative reader on page 1 that orthodoxy is "deliberately constructed creditor propaganda." This destroys the steelmans before they begin and gives the historian the line "the credit theory has been *winning* for two decades — treating it as suppressed knowledge is salesmanship."

**Move:**
- Open instead with the lay/specialist gap: the credit theory has been the dominant scholarly tradition for decades (Innes, Knapp, Goodhart, Wray, Hudson, Ingham, Graeber); it has not penetrated the public discourse, which still treats money as a thing. That is the gap the book closes.
- Cut every appearance of "deliberately constructed," "buried truth," "systematic forgetting" used as moral indictment. Keep the Currency-School-as-institutional-victory historical claim — but as historical claim, not as conspiracy.
- Promise the steelmans explicitly and name the ones the book actually delivers: Friedman-Schwartz on 1929–33, Buchanan-Wagner on fiscal incentive, Sargent-Wallace on monetary-fiscal coordination, FTPL on price-level determination. Tell the reader what the book will concede before asking them to follow.
- Acknowledge fiat's real failure modes (Weimar, Zimbabwe, post-1971 chronic deficits, asset-price inflation) on the way in, not as defensive concession in Ch. 12.

**Cost:** ~2,000 words rewritten in the Preface, plus a calmer rephrase of the same claims wherever they appear in Ch. 1 and the Epilogue.

**What it buys:** disarms the conservative's strongest opening attack; converts the economist's reading of the steelmans from "performance" to "promise the book then keeps"; removes the historian's salesmanship complaint.

## Phase 1.5 — Manuscript-wide tone audit *(new — load-bearing for the open-minded-reader strategy)*

**Diagnosis:** Phase 1 fixes the Preface, but polemic is distributed across the manuscript, not concentrated. Every reviewer flagged it from their own angle: NYT ("permanent indictment"), editor ("MMT framing weaponized in reviews"), historian ("polemical ambition"). The book's strongest strategic position is *not being polemic*, and that position requires a systematic pass, not localized rewrites. The substance is defensible because it's correct; only the delivery is polemic. Strip the delivery, the substance lands harder.

**Sequencing:** runs immediately after Phase 1 so the new tonal North Star is set, before Phase 2 chapter rewrites inherit it. Phase 4's prose pass then polishes already-detoxed text rather than fighting two battles at once.

**Four moves:**

1. **Search-and-cull register.** Grep the manuscript for these phrases and patterns; each hit must pass the Tooze test (*would Tooze write this without qualification?*):
   - `designed to`, `orchestrated`, `engineered to`
   - `institutional capture` (used as default explanation for orthodox victories)
   - `served the interests of`, `served creditor interests`
   - `transferred upward`, `redistributed wealth from X to Y` *(when phrased as intent rather than effect)*
   - `buried`, `erased`, `forgotten` *(used as moral claim rather than historical observation)*
   - `the verdict has been delivered`, `settled the debate`, `the question is closed`
   - `lineal descendant`, `the latest version of`
   - `powerful interests`, `creditor cabal`

   Most won't survive the test. Many can be rewritten as the same factual claim in a calmer register: "designed to transfer wealth" → "had the effect of transferring wealth"; "buried truth" → "argument that fell out of public discourse"; "institutional capture" → "Overstone's network gradually dominated the institutions that mattered."

2. **Pattern fix on villain framing.** Every orthodox actor cast as villain gets at least one sentence of their best argument in their own voice before judgment lands. Specifically:
   - **Overstone** (Ch. 8) — currently has no voice. Use his correspondence (cited in O'Brien, ed., *The Correspondence of Lord Overstone*, 3 vols.) for one direct quote making his strongest case.
   - **Ricardo** (Ch. 7/8) — let his *Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency* argument stand on its own terms before the chapter situates it politically.
   - **Churchill** (Ch. 9) — already has the Keynes-on-Churchill move; verify it lands as policy disagreement rather than character indictment.
   - **Volcker** (Ch. 11/12) — folded into Phase 2b.
   - **1971 architects** (Ch. 11) — Connally, Burns, Volcker himself; let one of them make the case for closing the gold window before the chapter's political reading.

3. **Epilogue tone pass.** Currently a victory lap for the productive-capacity framework. Should be a calm landing:
   - Here is what the historical record shows.
   - Here are the open questions (FTPL, public-choice limits on fiscal rules, what fiat actually risks).
   - Here is what fiat enables and what it has not yet been tested against.

   Drop "the verdict" register entirely from the Epilogue. The tone should match the historian's "warm strong recommendation" target.

4. **Politics-by-evidence test.** For each major political claim in the manuscript, ask: *would Tooze let the evidence carry this without saying it aloud?* Where yes — cut the claim, trust the evidence. Where the claim must be made — qualify it. Tooze's *Crashed* is leftward but never says so; the politics emerge from which crises he covers, which actors he holds responsible, which institutions he interviews. The model.

**Cost:** ~1 week of focused editorial work. Mechanical for the grep-and-cull moves; judgment-heavy for the villain-voicing and Epilogue rewrite.

**What it buys:** the historian's verdict moves from "competent synthesis with polemical ambition" to "honest synthesis." The NYT reviewer's "permanent indictment" complaint vanishes. The editor's "doesn't earn the right to its conclusions" objection collapses, because conclusions earned through evidence don't need to be announced. The book's strategic position becomes: *clearly not polemic, therefore the substance gets to land on its merits.*

**Tradeoff to accept:** the book loses some of the Graeber-style punch that helped *Debt* sell to the activist-left audience. That audience is not the target. NYT/editor/historian readers are repelled by polemic, not energized by it. The math is unambiguous for this audience.

## Phase 2 — Chapter-level rewrites *(scoped down from original plan)*

The original Phase 2 was sized to satisfy a Cochrane-type orthodox reader. For open-minded readers we can lighten the load: the goal isn't to win an orthodox economist's "strong approve," it's to remove the "thumb on the scale" smell that NYT/editor/historian all detected.

### 2a. Reconcile the Ch. 7 / Ch. 8 steelman seam *(keep as planned — this is for the open-minded readers)*

**Diagnosis:** Ch. 7 (lines 60–67, 155) concedes the Bullionists had a real case, war-finance temptation is genuine, the Currency School error became visible only after 1844. Ch. 8 (lines 5, 91–144, 162) reverts to a morality play in which Overstone is the architect of creditor capture. The two chapters do not reconcile, and reviewers see the seam.

**Move:**
- Rewrite Ch. 8's framing so the institutional-victory narrative is *consistent with* (not a retraction of) Ch. 7's intellectual concession. Overstone won partly because he had a real case and partly through institutional capture — both can be true. The current chapter implies only the second.
- Cut "designed to transfer real purchasing power from debtors to creditors." Distributional consequences are real; *designed* is the conspiracy register the book cannot defend.
- Add one paragraph: under what conditions would the 1844 framework have been the right policy? (Answer: under a sovereign with a credible commitment device and no adequate substitute for the gold anchor — which is roughly what Britain had in 1844.) This is the move that makes Ch. 8 a serious historical chapter rather than a polemic.

### 2b. Rewrite the Volcker chapter with a real counterfactual *(trimmed from 3,000 to ~1,500 added words)*

**Diagnosis:** three pages of Hagedorn-farm cruelty against fifteen lines of "expectations had de-anchored." Even the NYT reviewer flagged the asymmetry: Bernanke gets an apology for 1929–33 in Ch. 7; the alternative-to-Volcker counterfactual gets none. Open-minded readers don't need academic citations; they need the chapter to honestly answer "what would have happened if he hadn't."

**Move (lighter):**
- Add a 1,500-word passage on the Argentina-style entrenched-inflation counterfactual. Use a specific household — a passbook saver in 1979 — as moral parallel to the Hagedorn farm. The chapter must show what Volcker prevented, not just what he caused. Skip the Goodfriend / Clarida-Galí-Gertler citations — those serve only the orthodox economist.
- Keep the Hagedorn vignette; it is the book's most powerful scene. But end the chapter with the moral choice honestly framed: *which* group of households was Volcker willing to let lose, and why was the answer defensible in 1979 even though it looks indefensible in retrospect.
- Drop the "men in Washington decided the distributional cost was acceptable" formulation. It is true and tendentious and the book gains nothing by saying it.

### 2c. Restructure the 2021 inflation architecture *(FTPL passage dropped; only the chartalism-FTPL conflation removed)*

**Diagnosis:** the 2021 discussion still reads as "fiat held, then conceded a few caveats" rather than "the framework predicts ARP-driven inflation, here's the confirmation." The dedicated FTPL engagement in the original plan was for Cochrane-type readers; open-minded readers don't need it.

**Move (lighter):**
- Remove the chartalism-FTPL conflation in Ch. 7 line 47 — it's a one-paragraph fix. No dedicated FTPL passage needed. Open-minded readers won't notice the absence.
- Restructure the 2021 inflation section so the framework's *prediction* of ARP-driven inflation comes first, the empirical synthesis (50% supply / 33–50% demand) comes second as confirmation, and the European comparison gets a paragraph rather than a clause. Quote Blanchard's 2021 estimate that the stimulus was 3–5× the output gap; that is the number the chapter currently does not name.
- Rephrase "fiat held" to something defensible. The fiat regime delivered an inflation episode in line with what the book's framework predicts under ARP-magnitude stimulus into a constrained economy; that is a *win* for the framework, not something the chapter should be defending against.

### 2d. Rebuild Bitcoin / Austrian engagement in Ch. 12 *(trimmed from 3–5 pages to ~2 pages)*

**Diagnosis:** two paragraphs on Bitcoin (21M cap, Terra/Luna, FTX) reads as dismissal even to the historian. Open-minded readers don't need Selgin and Ammous engaged at length, but they need the chapter to acknowledge the critique exists before dispatching it.

**Move (lighter):**
- Replace the two paragraphs with a ~2-page section. Two beats:
  1. **The critique Bitcoin embodies, named honestly** — discretionary central banks have a documented track record of debasement; rule-bound regimes constrain that. This is Hayek's argument in *Denationalisation of Money*. Take it seriously in one paragraph; don't engage at academic length.
  2. **The book's response, made openly** — the framework agrees that institutions matter; the disagreement is over which constraint is binding (capacity vs. anchor commitment). The book's own institutionalism is *partly* a sound-money argument under a different vocabulary, and saying so out loud strengthens the thesis. Then explain why Bitcoin-as-currency actually fails — volatility, settlement assurance, lender-of-last-resort absence. Keep Terra / Luna / FTX as evidence under "execution failure," not as the whole conversation.
- The Buchanan-parallel acknowledgment (independent fiscal council = gold anchor reinvented) belongs in this section too. One sentence, signaled but not over-developed.

## Phase 3 — Structural restructure for repetition; selective trim, possible modest growth *(reframed 2026-04-27)*

**Reframed diagnosis:** the editor persona's "cut 12–15k" was a *commercial* read that may have underestimated the category. Comparable monetary-history trade nonfiction sits at 80–95k (Felix Martin, Eichengreen, Ammous, Lewis); long-history trade nonfiction sits at 180–250k (Ahamed, Graeber, Tooze). Current manuscript at 67k is on the short end of the category, not long. The repetition complaint that drove the editor's cut prescription is real — the thesis is established by Ch. 5 and re-litigated thereafter — but it's a *structural* problem fixable by removing redundancy, not an absolute-length problem.

Three of five reviewers (historian, conservative, economist) implicitly wanted more substance, not less. The NYT reviewer's prose complaint was about *how* the book explains, not *how much*.

**Revised target:** roughly flat at 65–70k after additions and selective trims, with the option to grow toward 75–80k if author bandwidth and timeline permit. The shape of the work is what matters; absolute count is secondary.

**Specific moves:**

- **Remove repeated taxation-creates-demand exposition** across Chs. 1, 3, 5. Establish canonically in Ch. 1; later chapters refer back rather than re-derive. Estimated trim: 1,500–2,000 words.
- **Trim the weakest case-study set pieces.** Of the ~12 currently in the manuscript, keep the load-bearing ones:
  - Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn (Ch. 4)
  - Datini 1399 (Ch. 2)
  - Paterson (Ch. 5)
  - Harry Todd / Boldon Colliery 1926 (Ch. 9)
  - The Klemperers / Aunt Berta / 1923 (Ch. 12)
  - The Hagedorn farm (Ch. 12)

  Trim or cut the secondary set pieces (Godeffroy, Lebesque, Cantwell, Heslop, Kantor, Kono in their current depth). They're each fine; collectively they overload. Estimated trim: 3,000–4,000 words.
- **Tighten Ch. 8.** Attwood biographical material and the Overstone institutional-network section currently overlap. One pass through with the goal of saying each thing once. Estimated trim: 500–1,000 words.
- **Do NOT merge Chs. 10 and 11** as the original plan suggested. Keynes-vs-White at Bretton Woods and the breaking-of-Bretton-Woods are distinct narrative arcs that work better at chapter resolution than as one combined chapter. The original merge proposal was driven by the absolute-length cut target that this revision drops.

**Anticipated growth:**

- Volcker counterfactual (Phase 2b, already added): +1,500 words
- Bitcoin/Austrian engagement (Phase 2d, pending): +1,500–2,000 words
- Character voicing for anchor figures (Phase 4): +1,500–2,500 words
- European comparison expansion (Phase 2c, already added): +400 words

**Net likely outcome:** 67k current → ~67–72k after trims and additions. The manuscript stays focused-argument-book length rather than long-history length, but the substance does more work per word.

**If author wants to push toward genre median (85–95k):** add archival depth in 2–3 specific places where the historian persona flagged thinness — Norman/Strong/Schacht in the interwar (Ch. 9), the eurozone treatment (Ch. 12, currently 1 paragraph could become 4–5 pages), and the institutional response of 2008 (Ch. 12, more on the Fed mechanics, the AIG rescue, the global swap lines). Each of these is well-sourced in existing literature and would add substance without bloat. Decision is author's; not strictly required for strong-approve from the three open-minded readers.

## Phase 4 — Prose pass for NYT-grade trade nonfiction

**Diagnosis:** NYT reviewer is the only persona who pressed this hard, but the diagnosis is exact: bolded glossary terms accumulate ("the reader feels they are highlighting their own homework"), chapter openings follow an NPR-podcast formula, characters "deliver the moral lesson the chapter requires and disappear," and the prose has a habit of declared victories ("the verdict has been delivered," "settled the debate forever") that the historian also flags.

**Moves:**
- **Bolded glossary terms:** keep at most 8–10 across the entire book, used only at first introduction of a load-bearing technical term. Move the rest to a back-matter glossary (which already exists at `06-Glossary/glossary.md`).
- **Chapter openings:** rewrite the four worst NPR-formula openings. Identify them by reading just the first paragraphs of each chapter end-to-end; the formula is recognizable.
- **Character work on five anchors** — Backwell, Paterson, Keynes (at Bretton Woods), Attwood, Volcker. Give each two or three sentences of voice — what they sounded like, how they argued, what they were afraid of. Use letters and contemporaneous accounts where possible; the apparatus already cites several.
- **Declared victories:** search-and-cull "the verdict has been delivered," "settled the debate," "the question is closed" register. Economic history rarely permits these and the book is read as advocacy partly because it uses them.

## What NOT to do

(From the synthesis, restated because they are tempting and wrong.)

- **Do not lengthen.** Every reviewer who comments on length wants less.
- **Do not add another late-chapter "concession" passage.** The economist's diagnosis is exact: bolted-on concessions make the framing chapters look worse, not better. Concessions live in the framing or not at all — Phase 1 is where they live.
- **Do not over-engage Bitcoin.** Three to five pages is the target. Two paragraphs is too few; thirty pages would be too many.
- **Do not try to upgrade the book to "original contribution" by adding archival research.** That's a different book. The historian's "competent synthesis" verdict, made warm rather than grudging, is the realistic ceiling and is good enough — Felix Martin and Stephanie Kelton both shipped at that level.

# Appendix — Attributes of strong writing from comparable titles

The reviews named the comps repeatedly. Each comp embodies a writing virtue this manuscript currently lacks. Below: what each does, what to steal, and where in *Money, Trust, and Power* to deploy it.

## Liaquat Ahamed — *Lords of Finance* (2009, Pulitzer, ~250k+ copies)

**The virtue: character work that lets historical actors carry argument.**

Norman, Strong, Schacht, Moreau are not positions; they are people, with marriages, breakdowns, prejudices, and physical presence. Ahamed lets each man make his own case in his own voice before the reader is asked to judge the policy. The argument about the gold standard's interwar destruction is *carried* by reader sympathy with the four central bankers, not asserted around them.

**Steal these specific moves:**
- Open chapters with a person at a moment, not a thesis at a temple. The barley-dust-Uruk opening of Ch. 1 is exactly the formula Ahamed avoids.
- Give the antagonist their best argument in their own voice before the chapter judges. Overstone in Ch. 8 currently has no voice. Letters and contemporaneous writings exist; use them.
- Trust setting and circumstance to do mood work. Ahamed describes Norman's Threadneedle Street office, his clothes, his posture, his depression. The book gains historical weight from physical specificity.
- Don't repeat the thesis. The argument accretes through example; it isn't restated every fifty pages.

**Apply to:** Ch. 4 (Backwell), Ch. 5 (Paterson — already strong), Ch. 8 (Attwood AND Overstone — currently lopsided), Ch. 9 (Norman, Strong, Schacht — thinly handled per the historian), Ch. 10 (Keynes vs. White, especially the Bretton Woods scene), Ch. 11 (Volcker as a person, not a policy).

## Adam Tooze — *Crashed* (2018, ~100k+ copies, FT Book of the Year)

**The virtue: reader trust at scale.**

Tooze writes 700 pages of dense argument and *almost never* restates a premise. He trusts the reader to track. The argument is carried by sequence and selection — what's juxtaposed against what, what's brought back from chapter three to chapter eleven, which detail is reserved for the climax.

**Steal these specific moves:**
- Footnotes that signal scholarship without intruding on flow. Tooze's apparatus is heavy and invisible. The current manuscript's suffixed footnotes (¹⁷ᶜ ¹⁷ᵈ ¹⁷ᶠ) intrude even on the page.
- Politics visible but earned. Tooze's leftward read of 2008 is unmistakable but never said aloud — it emerges from which crises he covers, which actors he interviews, which institutions he holds responsible. The current manuscript's "deliberately constructed creditor propaganda" is the opposite of this technique.
- Long sentences when needed. Don't over-paragraph. The current manuscript paragraphs everything to short bursts; some arguments need a long sentence to land.
- No glossary intrusions. Tooze defines a technical term once, on first use, in the body. He doesn't bold it.

**Apply to:** Phase 1 (Preface tonal restraint), Phase 4 (cut bolded glossary, fix paragraphing in Chs. 7–11), Phase 5 (linearize footnotes).

## Michael Lewis — *The Big Short* (2010, ~1m+ copies)

**The virtue: voice in three sentences.**

Burry, Eisman, Lippmann, Bass — each is distinguishable from the next on the page after a single page of presence. Lewis builds a person through ticks, language, contradictions, and small physical details. Technical material is delivered through character explanation, not authorial exposition.

**Steal these specific moves:**
- Build characters through contradictions. Burry's glass eye and his medical license. Eisman's rudeness as ethical position. Each anchor figure in *Money, Trust, and Power* needs at least one defining contradiction surfaced.
- Use dialogue. The manuscript is almost entirely indirect. Even one quoted line per anchor figure changes the texture.
- Deliver technical content through character. Instead of "fractional reserve banking is the practice of…", Lewis would have a banker explain it badly to a colleague while afraid of being overheard.
- Comic timing. The manuscript is too earnest. Lewis is funny because the rhythm of his sentences sets up payoffs; the humor is structural, not joke-based.

**Apply to:** Phase 4. The five anchor figures (Backwell, Paterson, Keynes, Attwood, Volcker) each need a Lewis-grade introductory paragraph — voice, contradiction, physical detail, one quoted line.

## David Graeber — *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011, ~250k+ copies)

**The virtue: big-claim confidence without apology.**

Graeber states large unfashionable claims and earns them through erudition rather than hedging. The book reads as opinionated because it *is* opinionated, but the opinion is supported by anthropological detail no other writer has assembled. The opening (the conversation at the garden party with the lawyer) does work for the entire book: it establishes the question, the antagonist, and the author's voice in five pages.

**Steal these specific moves:**
- Write a Graeber-grade opening scene. The current Preface argues; it doesn't dramatize. A specific conversation, a specific moment of confusion or wrongness, that surfaces the book's question — that's the Graeber move.
- Don't qualify into safety. Graeber would not write "the productive-capacity framework is one way to think about money creation." He'd write "money is credit; this is what the evidence shows; the textbook is wrong." The current manuscript hedges in the wrong places (mid-chapter qualifications) and asserts in the wrong places (Preface as accusation).
- Cross-cultural sweep generates surprise. Graeber's Madagascar and Tiv chapters make the European story strange. The current manuscript's Mesopotamia, China, and colonial-Africa material does some of this work; lean into it.

**Caution:** the current Preface tries for Graeber's confidence and lands as Graeber's accusation. The model is Graeber's *erudition*, not his polemic.

**Apply to:** Phase 1 (Preface — try a scene-led opening), Phase 4 (don't hedge in the body if you've earned the claim).

## Felix Martin — *Money: An Unauthorised Biography* (2013, ~50–80k copies, closest comp)

**The virtue: argument-led, vignette-illustrated, ruthlessly tight chapters.**

Martin's chapters average 7,000–8,000 words. Each makes one argument, illustrated by one or two vignettes, and ends. He doesn't try to settle every debate; he makes a claim and moves on. This is the closest structural template for the manuscript.

**Steal these specific moves:**
- One argument per chapter, named in the chapter's first 500 words and not re-derived.
- Vignettes serve argument; they are not collected for their own sake. The current manuscript has ~12 vignettes; Martin would have four or five, and each would be argumentatively load-bearing.
- Tight chapters — target 5,500–7,000 words, not the current 8,000–10,000.

**Apply to:** Phase 3 (the cut). Use Martin's chapter-length norm as the budget.

## Stephanie Kelton — *The Deficit Myth* (2020, NYT bestseller, ~150k copies)

**The virtue: commercial proof that the chartalist argument can sell, when stated unapologetically and with concrete examples chosen for political resonance.**

Kelton is not a strong prose model — direct, conversational, second-person — but she demonstrates that the readership exists and what tone reaches it. Her examples are picked for stakes, not for illustration: a teacher's pension, a flooded town, a deferred infrastructure project.

**Steal:**
- Concrete examples chosen for resonance, not for completeness. The Hagedorn farm and the Klemperer salt herring are this. The Godeffroy / Heslop / Cantwell / Lebesque / Kantor / Kono / Preuß case studies mostly aren't — they're collected for completeness.
- Direct address where it strengthens the argument. The manuscript currently never uses "you." Sometimes it should.

**Don't steal:** Kelton's prose pace. She is a less interesting writer than Ahamed, Tooze, Lewis, or Martin, and the manuscript should aim higher than her bar.

## Saifedean Ammous — *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018, ~500k+ copies)

**The virtue: none worth importing — but worth understanding as the antagonist.**

Ammous's commercial success matters because it shows the conservative/sound-money readership is large and underserved by serious writing. The manuscript can't out-flank Ammous on his own terms, but it should be read because it shows what Phase 2d's Bitcoin section is actually responding to. Read the chapter on "Why Gold?" and the chapter on "The History of Money" — both are the strongest of the book — and write the response section with that text in front of you.

**Apply to:** Phase 2d preparation reading.

## Synthesis — the writing standards this book must meet

Pulled from the comps, in priority order:

1. **Trust the reader.** (Tooze) Cut the bolded glossary. Stop restating the thesis. Stop declaring victories.
2. **Voice the anchors.** (Ahamed, Lewis) Five named figures must each have voice, contradiction, and physical specificity in their first introduction.
3. **One argument per chapter.** (Martin) Named in the first 500 words; not re-derived.
4. **Politics visible but earned.** (Tooze) The leftward read is fine; the accusatory framing is not. Let the argument carry the politics.
5. **Concrete examples chosen for stakes.** (Kelton, Ahamed) Trim to 4–5 vignettes, each load-bearing.
6. **A scene-led opening for the Preface.** (Graeber) Drama, not announcement.
7. **Apparatus that signals scholarship without intruding.** (Tooze) Linear footnotes, no suffixes, no in-text figure cues that aren't yet figures.

These are the writing standards the manuscript will be judged against by the three open-minded reviewers. The argument is already at the level. The prose isn't yet.
