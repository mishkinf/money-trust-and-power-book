# Developmental Edit Letter: *Money, Trust, and Power*

**To:** The Author
**From:** [Editor]
**Re:** Manuscript submission, 70,000 words
**Date:** April 2026

---

## 1. Overall Assessment

This is a serious, ambitious, well-researched manuscript that wants to be the next *Lords of Finance* but currently reads like a hybrid of *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, a Kelton-style MMT polemic, and a graduate-level monetary survey course delivered in plain prose. There is a real book in here — the bones are good, the period research is strong, the contemporary stakes are clearly motivated, and the author has done unusually thorough archival work for a trade title (the Datini bill of December 12, 1399; the Gigney insolvency; Harry Todd at Boldon Colliery; Heslop's papers at Durham; Maureen McKenzie's mortgage; Michinao Kono and the *hikikomori* generation). That archival depth is the manuscript's biggest single asset and the strongest argument that this can be elevated.

It is not yet publishable. **My quality estimate is 73 out of 100** — a strong professional draft, one substantial revision short of being acquirable at a major trade house. At 73 it would acquire today at a serious university press (Princeton, Yale, Chicago). At 80, with the revisions below, it becomes acquirable at PublicAffairs, Basic, Norton, or Knopf — PublicAffairs being the natural home, given it published Leonard's *Lords of Easy Money* and Kelton's *The Deficit Myth*. An 85 — strong list title — requires the author to make a decision the manuscript currently refuses to make (see §2). Bestseller territory (90) would require either a sharper authorial voice (Ferguson, Ahamed) or a more disciplined polemic (Kelton); the prose is competent but rarely thrilling, and the argument is too internally hedged to function as polemic.

The shelf is **economic history / financial history**, top of the trade list, sitting next to Ahamed, Eichengreen, Kelton, Tooze, Silber, and Levy. Catalog comp: "*Lords of Finance* meets *The Deficit Myth*."

The reasons it is a 73 and not an 80, in declining order of severity: (1) it cannot decide whether it's a narrative history, an MMT manifesto, or a textbook; (2) it relentlessly tells the reader what the next chapter will prove and what the last chapter proved; (3) the contemporary parallels are imposed rather than earned; (4) a tic of analytical framing ("This wasn't accidental — it was engineered"; "the lesson was clear"; "the arithmetic was inexorable") flattens nuance; (5) the architecture is overweight in Parts III and IV. These are all fixable at revision, not rewrite, level.

## 2. Big-Picture Issues (Rank-Ordered)

### Issue 1: The book has not decided what it is.

This is the manuscript's central problem. Three books are fighting inside this draft:

- **Book A: A narrative history of money** — the Ahamed model. Bruges 1399, Lombard Street 1672, the dinner at 11 Downing Street, the Mount Washington Hotel, Harry Todd's seventeen months. These passages work.
- **Book B: A polemic for Modern Monetary Theory disguised as history** — the Preface ("This inversion... is not an accident. It served powerful interests and was deliberately constructed"); Chapter 8's "Great Forgetting"; Chapter 13's explicit invocation of MMT's core insight; the Appendix's MMT framework; the Reader's Guide's framing as a "detective story about how a fundamental misunderstanding became conventional wisdom." This is Kelton, not Ahamed.
- **Book C: A monetary economics textbook** — Chapter 5's bullet-pointed ledger entries; Chapter 13's "Failed Fiat Systems" comparison table; the bracketed in-text definitions ("**convertibility** — the ability to exchange paper money for precious metal at a fixed rate"); the "Why Smart People Believed a Fallacy for 150 Years" boxes; the "Imagine a rule that sounds perfectly reasonable" analogies.

Each is fine on its own; they cannot share a binding. The textbook scaffolding deflates the narrative; the narrative softens the polemic; the polemic's certainty conflicts with the historian's nuance.

**What to do.** Make Book A the spine and subordinate B and C to it. The Ahamed/Tooze/Silber template works because the historical narrative carries the argument by implication — the reader arrives at the conclusion without being told. The current draft tells the conclusion in the first paragraph of every chapter, re-tells it at the end, then re-re-tells it when the next chapter opens. That's textbook rhythm. Cut the "Why This Matters Now" section heads. Move the bracketed definitions to the existing glossary. Move the comparison tables to the existing supplementary materials. Trust the scenes.

### Issue 2: The contemporary parallels are imposed rather than earned.

Almost every chapter opens with a contemporary scene before the historical narrative: Chapter 1 Bitcoin; Chapter 2 a Paris café credit card; Chapter 3 Greece 2010; Chapter 4 Lehman 2008; Chapter 5 Fed 2020; Chapter 6 gold-standard nostalgia; Chapter 7 Fed 2020 again; Chapter 8 the household-budget metaphor; Chapter 10 Greece 2015; Chapter 11 China's dollar trap; Chapter 13 COVID stimulus. Within chapters: "Modern stablecoins like USDC replicate this pattern"; "When the Federal Reserve settles trillions"; "This is exactly Greece's problem"; "Today's equivalent is dollar reserves"; "Modern bond markets enforce the same discipline."

The technique is fine — Ferguson uses it; Kelton uses it. The problem is frequency and placement. The contemporary hook arrives before the historical material, robbing each chapter of the chance to surprise. By Chapter 7 the reader has been told three times that the 2020 Fed response replays 1797 and knows what the chapter will argue before it starts.

**What to do.** Pick one contemporary frame per chapter and put it at the *end*, as a short coda. Or open and close with one each, but eliminate the four-to-seven mid-chapter intrusions. The Greece parallel appears in Chapters 3, 10, 12, and 13 — choose one place. The 2008 parallel appears in 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, and the Epilogue — choose two.

### Issue 3: A stylistic tic — the "arithmetic of inevitability" voice — has metastasized.

A taxonomy of phrases recurring with damaging frequency:

- "The math was simple..." / "The arithmetic was simple..." / "The arithmetic was inexorable" / "Collapse was arithmetic, not policy error" / "Mathematically impossible"
- "The lesson was clear" / "The lesson was unmistakable" / "The pattern was clear" / "The pattern repeats"
- "This wasn't [X]. This wasn't [Y]. This was [Z]." — used at the climax of nearly every set piece (Todds, Iowa farmer, McKenzie, Cantwell)
- Bolded summary sentences as paragraph capstones — I count more than 80 instances of "**X is what made the system work**" / "**This wasn't accidental**" / "**Money is not a thing but a relationship**"

The cumulative effect: the author keeps stepping into frame to underline what the reader has just understood. Imagine if Ahamed, after describing Norman's nervous breakdown, added: "The lesson was clear: central bankers are human." He doesn't. He trusts the scene.

**What to do.** A revision pass dedicated to cutting analytical conclusions. Rule of thumb: if a paragraph ends with a bolded sentence telling the reader what they've just learned, cut it. If a section ends with "The pattern was X," cut it.

### Issue 4: The "Great Forgetting" (Chapter 8) overreaches.

The keystone of the polemic. Its claim is large — that between 1810 and 1850 a coalition of bondholders, Currency School economists, and Lord Overstone *manufactured* an orthodoxy that buried the chartalist understanding of money in service of class interest. The Overstone correspondence, Norman's fifty-one years on the Bank's Court, the Political Economy Club's gatekeeping — the case is genuinely interesting.

But the chapter overreaches in three ways a hostile reviewer will exploit. (a) It treats Attwood as a vindicated proto-Keynesian seer rather than as one voice in a complicated debate; "He was right — vindicated by the disasters of 1873–1896 and 1925–1931" conflates three different episodes and elides the distinction between "Attwood diagnosed something real" and "Attwood's program would have worked." (b) The chapter performs the same move it accuses the Currency School of performing — pathologizing opponents. Disraeli called Attwood a monomaniac; the manuscript calls Currency School orthodoxy "manufactured," "engineered," "captured," "imposed." Some of the orthodoxy's adherents — including the founders of *The Economist* — held their views on intellectual rather than venal grounds. (c) The framing "The Man They Erased" then bends to fit evidence: Attwood was an MP, knew the Prime Minister, organized the largest political assembly in British history to that date, was thanked publicly by Earl Grey. He lost a policy argument. That is a different thing from being erased.

**What to do.** Either commit to the polemic (Kelton-style) or step back to the historian's voice and let the record speak. The current middle position — "I'm just describing what happened, but here is a sentence calling it theft and institutional capture" — will get the book attacked from both flanks. I would push for the historian's voice. The evidence is strong enough that a cooler tone makes the case more devastatingly.

### Issue 5: The human-cost set pieces are the book's best material and are unevenly deployed.

The strongest pages are the embedded human-scale narratives: Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn at the temple of Gula (Ch. 1); Datini's December 12, 1399 bill (Ch. 2); Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn (Ch. 4); Senator Godeffroy's September 1872 resignation (Ch. 6); Samuel Gigney and Robert Leach in the 1820s deflation (Ch. 7); Heslop and Cantwell (Chs. 8–9); Harry Todd's seventeen months and Iowa's "Farmer A" (Ch. 10); Keynes's collapse on the hotel stairs (Ch. 11); McKenzie, Kantor, the Hagedorns, and Michinao Kono (Ch. 13); Victor and Eva Klemperer in Dresden (Ch. 13). These are why the book is a 73 rather than a 65. They will travel — reviewers will quote them, podcasts will lift them.

Their deployment is uneven. Chapter 6 has Godeffroy but drowns him in nine pages of Newton's silver-gold ratios. Chapter 4 has Backwell but disappears him for ten pages of exposition. Chapter 12 is human-scale starved (mostly Triffin and de Gaulle in conference rooms). Chapter 13 swings the other way — Kantor, the Hagedorns, Kono, and the Klemperers all in one chapter, competing for oxygen.

**What to do.** Audit and rebalance. Every chapter should have at least one human-scale figure. Chapter 6 needs a second beyond Godeffroy. Chapter 12 needs at least one (a French finance minister inside the de Gaulle gold offensive; a London goldsmith facing the March 1968 bank holiday; a Treasury official watching Britain's August 11 conversion request). Chapter 13 should redistribute one of its four set pieces to Chapter 12. The Klemperer hyperinflation passage is gorgeous and currently buried inside a comparative table — break it out.

### Issue 6: The Bretton Woods and Triffin chapters (10–12) are genuinely excellent but overweight.

Chapter 10 is 6,144 words. Chapter 11 is 6,327 words. Chapter 12 is 4,821 words. That's 17,292 words — 25% of the book — on a fifty-year span. Compare: Chapter 2 covers approximately three centuries in 4,176 words. The Bretton Woods material is good (the July 13 "gold-convertible exchange" exchange, Robertson's capitulation, Keynes's collapse, the Klemperer-quality Hamlet quotation are all strong) but it doesn't need this much room.

Specifically: Chapter 11 has a "Battle of Systems" comparison that lays out bancor vs. dollar in two parallel bullet-point structures, then narrates the same comparison in prose, then summarizes it in a "Why Keynes's System Was Superior" section, then explains "Why America Chose Dollar Standard Anyway," then summarizes again in "What America Sacrificed." That's five passes at the same argument. Cut to one prose treatment, kill the bullet structures, save 2,000 words.

Chapter 12 does something similar with the Triffin dilemma, walking through "the simple version," "the math that doesn't work," "why this was structurally unsolvable" with four labelled options, "the proof," and "why the fiat era is different." That's the structure of a textbook chapter. In a trade book, narrate the dilemma once, in prose, and let the de Gaulle gold offensive *be* the proof.

### Issue 7: The Preface promises a book the manuscript doesn't quite deliver.

The Preface is strong and makes specific promises. The first two — "money is not a thing but a relationship," and the systematic-forgetting claim — are delivered. The third — the redistributive/Polanyian thread about wealth concentration, productive assets, and rent extraction — is not. The book gestures at it (deflation-as-creditor-protection in Ch. 8; QE-as-asset-inflation in Ch. 13) but doesn't make it the spine. If the Preface invokes debt jubilees and modern housing unaffordability, the body has to do more work to connect the dots. The fourth — "without prescribing solutions" — is broken. Chapter 13 and the Epilogue prescribe specifically: build credible institutions, keep central banks independent, prefer fiscal transfers to QE, design CBDCs to be neutral, beware crypto, follow MMT principles. Either own the prescriptions and revise the Preface, or actually write a book without them.

### Issue 8: Structural redundancy is the most fixable problem.

Things the manuscript says more than three times: that credit predates commodity money (Preface plus Chs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, Epilogue); that taxation drives currency demand (Preface plus Chs. 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, Epilogue); that Paterson said the Bank creates money "out of nothing" (at least seven times); that the gold standard was Newton's accidental miscalculation (Preface plus Chs. 6, 8, 9, 12, Epilogue); that the Bank Restriction (1797–1821) proved fiat money worked (Chs. 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 13, Epilogue); that 1672 and 2008 and 2022 are the same crisis (Chs. 1, 3, 4, 11, 13); that money is not a thing but a relationship (Preface plus Chs. 1, 5, 11, Epilogue twice). Every chapter ends with a thesis-summary paragraph, then the next chapter opens with a thesis-restatement paragraph. Cut these and you reclaim at least 4,000 words.

## 3. Structure and Pacing

**The four-part architecture is broadly correct and should be kept.** Origins → Institutional Revolution → Gold Standard → Breaking and Beyond is a clean spine. The proportions, however, need work.

| Section | Words | % of book | Right? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preface | 1,128 | 1.6% | Slightly long, could trim |
| Part I (Ch 1–3) | 12,719 | 18.2% | Right |
| Part II (Ch 4–5) | 8,800 | 12.6% | Underweight; the Bank of England chapter especially |
| Part III (Ch 6–9) | 18,893 | 27.0% | Overweight, especially Ch 6 |
| Part IV (Ch 10–13) | 25,067 | 35.8% | Overweight; Ch 13 alone is 7,775 words |
| Epilogue | 3,394 | 4.8% | Right |

**Where it drags.** Chapter 6 (Newton): 5,845 words on the gold-silver ratio is too much; the "Follow the Silver: One Coin's Journey" textbook conceit doesn't earn its space; the "Three Convergent Forces" section is bullet-point exposition disguised as prose. Chapter 8 loops on Attwood — biography, "moment of realization," Birmingham rallies, Chartist petition, dismissal as monomania, institutional capture, "Modern Vindication," "How the Forgetting Happened," "Mechanics of Wealth Transfer," "Alternative They Rejected," "The Pattern." Six passes at one argument; cut to two. Chapter 11 is great but 6,327 words is a long sit; trim to 5,000. Chapter 13 at 7,775 words is the longest and tries to do the most — 2008 narrative, productive-capacity theory, state-capacity comparison table, Klemperer/Weimar, Kono/Japan, Volcker, Hagedorns, eurozone, COVID. Split it.

**Where it rushes.** Chapter 5 (Bank of England) is the book's most consequential institutional moment and gets only 4,181 words. Paterson is dispatched in a paragraph; the 1696 silver recoinage that *preceded* the Bank's founding is oddly placed in Chapter 6; the Glorious Revolution is one paragraph; the Bank's first century — including its near-collapse during the South Sea Bubble — is barely there. Add 1,500 words and a real Paterson set piece. Chapter 9 (Manufacturing Orthodoxy) is the second half of Chapter 8 in disguise; consider merging. The Epilogue's CBDC section feels grafted on; either commit to it as a final chapter with a set piece (Suzhou's digital yuan rollout; the Riksbank's e-krona pilot) or cut to a two-page coda.

**Splits and merges to consider.** Merge Chapters 8 and 9 into a single 6,000-word chapter on the manufacture of gold orthodoxy 1820–1850. Split Chapter 13 into "The Verdict" (2008, COVID, productive capacity) and "Volcker's Choice" (1970s, Volcker recession, Hagedorns). Consider whether 11 and 12 should merge — both have a Triffin section, both narrate de Gaulle's offensive, both end with Britain's August 11 conversion request.

The narrative arc (credit precedes commodity → institutions matter more than metal → gold was an accident → fiat works when institutions are credible → CBDCs are the next test) is clear, coherent, and supported. The payoff is delivered in the Epilogue, which is among the manuscript's best writing. The problem isn't the arc. It's that the reader has been told the arc seventeen times before they reach it.

## 4. Argument

**The central argument** is a synthesis of three claims:

1. Credit money is older than commodity money, and money has always been a relationship rather than a thing (the Hudson/Graeber/Wray chartalist claim).
2. Taxation creates currency demand, which is what makes fiat money work (the Knapp/Lerner/MMT claim).
3. The gold standard was a historically contingent error whose intellectual hegemony was manufactured to serve creditor interests, and its collapse is a liberation rather than a catastrophe (the Eichengreen-meets-Kelton synthesis).

The argument is clear. The evidence is mostly strong. The places where the book overreaches and the places where it underclaims are predictable.

**Where it overreaches.**

- The claim that the orthodoxy was *manufactured* by a deliberate coalition of interests reads as conspiracy when stated baldly and is strongest when softened. Lord Overstone was influential. He was not the puppet master. The book's strongest version of this argument — that monetary debates always have distributional consequences and that those who win generally hold concentrated political and economic power — is uncontroversial. The book's weakest version — that Disraeli pathologizing Attwood was "character assassination masquerading as diagnosis" — is melodrama.

- The claim that "the same pattern repeats" across 1672, 2008, and 2022 is true at a level of abstraction so high it's tautological. Of course private credit creation without a public backstop fails when confidence breaks. The interesting historical work is in the *differences* — why 1672's resolution took thirty years, why 2008's took eighteen months, why 2022's took eighteen days. The manuscript flattens these differences in service of the pattern.

- The "human cost" framing sometimes runs ahead of the evidence. The Iowa "Farmer A" passage builds a powerful narrative about gold sterilization causing one farm's foreclosure, but the evidence chain from "central bank gold sterilization" to "this specific farm in Story County" requires more counterfactual work than the manuscript performs. There were Iowa farms foreclosing in 1928 for many reasons; corn at $0.41 was overdetermined. The same is true of Maureen McKenzie — her foreclosure was caused by a payment-option ARM with a teaser rate, not directly by 2008's monetary policy. The book wants the human stories to *prove* the macroeconomic argument, when their actual function is to *illustrate* it. Clarify the rhetorical role.

**Where it underclaims.**

- The strongest argumentative thread in the book — that monetary regimes are political choices about distribution disguised as technical necessities — is hedged precisely where it should be sharpest. The Preface says "this book makes these patterns explicit without prescribing solutions"; that hedge weakens the argument. If the patterns are real, the prescriptions follow. Either own them or don't make the claim.

- The book's most original synthetic move is its argument about the *domestic-international asymmetry* — that domestic credit can be created against tax authority but international obligations require either commodity backing or institutional credibility. This is a genuinely useful framework and it explains a lot (Edward III, Greece 2010, Argentina, the Triffin dilemma, China's dollar trap). It deserves to be the book's signature contribution. Right now it's stated in Chapter 3, restated in Chapter 5, gestured at in Chapter 11, and never gets the elevated treatment it deserves. Promote this argument. Title-level treatment.

**Is the relationship between historical narrative and contemporary application earned, or imposed?**

It is more imposed than earned, and this is the most important craft criticism in the letter. The historical chapters work; the contemporary parallels frequently feel like they were written first and the history fitted to them. The Bitcoin opening to Chapter 1, the Greece opening to Chapter 3, the COVID opening to Chapter 13 — all read as if the author started with the contemporary debate they wanted to win and reverse-engineered a historical chapter to support it.

The fix is not to remove contemporary application; it's to delay it. Let the history do its work, then in a coda or a final paragraph, surface the parallel. Ahamed does this beautifully in *Lords of Finance*: 600 pages of 1920s narrative, and only at the very end does he turn to the contemporary parallels (Greenspan, the dot-com bubble). The reader earns the contemporary insight by living through the history. In the current draft, the reader is told the contemporary insight, then made to read the history as confirmation.

## 5. Voice and Prose

**Strengths.** Strong sentences when the author lets the scene do the work:
- "The smell of barley dust filled the air inside the temple granary of Uruk." (Ch. 1)
- "Backwell did the arithmetic the way any banker does." (Ch. 4)
- "The three Hamlet words at the end — *unhouselled, disappointed, unaneled* — are the words the Ghost uses for Hamlet's father, dying without confession or last rites. Keynes had spent his life choosing his quotations precisely. This one was for Britain." (Ch. 11)
- "Honour, it turned out, was an abstraction purchased with other people's ruin." (Ch. 7)

These are the manuscript at its best. The set-piece scene-setting is generally good — the dinner at 11 Downing Street; Pepys at Backwell's shop; the Datini bill on its way to Barcelona; the cold rain in London as William of Orange rode through the gates. *Lords of Finance* texture.

**Weaknesses.** The textbook voice intrudes — bracketed in-line definitions ("**bimetallic monetary system** — a monetary system using both gold and silver as legal tender..."; "**output gap** — the difference between actual and potential GDP..."). Trade readers can be trusted; the manuscript has a glossary; use it. The "this is exactly like" formulation has metastasized: in five pages of Chapter 2 alone we get USDC stablecoins, modern clearinghouses, the Federal Reserve's daily settlements, and "the merchant of 1500." Bolded summary sentences appear in nearly every section. The "imagine you're..." analogies are uneven — the designated-driver analogy for the Triffin dilemma works; the "Steve-Dollars" analogy in Chapter 11 strains. The "This wasn't fate. This wasn't market forces. This was [name]'s choice" three-beat structure repeats across Todd, Hagedorn, McKenzie, and Kantor passages. Vary the rhythm.

**Register.** Right for the educated *Atlantic*/*Foreign Affairs* reader. It slips down when the textbook voice intrudes and up when the polemic voice ramps. Pick one and hold.

**The tedious refrain.** "**Money is not a thing but a relationship**" — a fine sentence the first time, the book's thesis, repeated at least seven times in those exact words. State it once in the Preface, once in Chapter 1; trust the reader.

## 6. Character and Scene

**Memorable figures, ranked by use:** Keynes is the strongest — his collapse on the hotel stairs, the Hamlet quotation, the Savannah complaint, his death. Ahamed level. Backwell is well-handled (Sign of the Unicorn, Pepys's visit, the slow ruin in the Netherlands). Attwood is given enormous space (Ch. 8) but never quite comes alive as a person — we learn what he believed and organized; we don't learn what he was *like*. Newton's set piece in Chapter 6 is strong but he disappears too quickly. Churchill is the most underused major character — the dinner at 11 Downing Street and Keynes's pamphlet are there, but Churchill himself remains a function. Roosevelt, Volcker, and Bernanke are flat. De Gaulle exists only as a press-conference figure — the dramatic potential of his gold offensive is underexploited. Harry Todd, Datini, and Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn are among the book's best portraits.

**Underused, should be elevated:** *Paterson* — the founder of the Bank of England, arguably the most consequential individual in the book, dispatched in two paragraphs; the Darien disaster, his Caribbean years, his role as projector are absent from the narrative. *Overstone* — named repeatedly as architect of the orthodoxy but never put in a room. *Henry Thornton* — gets a competent summary in Ch. 7 but never becomes a character; he should be the proto-protagonist of the chapter, a banker-MP-evangelical who saw what no one else saw. *Triffin* — the dilemma is named for him; he never appears as a person.

**Set pieces that work:** Bruges 1399; Lombard Street 1672; the dinner at 11 Downing Street; Newhall Hill May 7, 1832; the Mount Washington Hotel July 1944; Boldon Colliery April 1926; the Klemperer café in Dresden. **Not yet working:** Newton in his laboratory (too much exposition, not enough scene); the New York Fed trading floor on March 16, 2008 (one paragraph that should be a chapter opening); the Treasury meeting Sunday January 7, 1672 (briefly excellent, then dropped).

## 7. Comparable Titles and Positioning

**Shelf:** economic history / financial history, top of trade list.

**Comps, in descending order of usefulness:** *Lords of Finance* (Ahamed, 2009) — the most important comp; the gold-standard set piece, the human-scale character work, the Pulitzer. *The Deficit Myth* (Kelton, 2020) — the MMT thesis and polemical register. *Globalizing Capital* (Eichengreen) — the serious-academic comp. *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (Graeber) — credit-precedes-commodity; close to Part I. *The Ascent of Money* (Ferguson) — broad civilizational sweep. *Crashed* (Tooze) — institutional/political crisis analysis. *The Lords of Easy Money* (Leonard, PublicAffairs, 2022). *Money: The Unauthorized Biography* (Felix Martin) — closest in argument. *Volcker* (Silber) — for the Volcker chapter.

**Target reader.** *Atlantic*/*Economist*/*NYRB* readers; non-economists who follow Fed policy; MBA students; the *Deficit Myth*/*Crashed* audience; the policy-adjacent professional class — staffers, journalists, advisers, 25–55.

**Catalog elevator pitch:**

> *Money, Trust, and Power* upends five thousand years of conventional wisdom. From clay tablets in Mesopotamia to Federal Reserve keystrokes, [Author] traces money's true history — not as a story of gold and metal, but as a story of institutions, taxation, and political power. The gold standard, we learn, was an accident of Newton's arithmetic, not a natural law; modern fiat money is a return to first principles, not a deviation from them; and every monetary regime, from the Bank of England's founding to the COVID stimulus, was a contest over who would bear the costs and who would reap the rewards. With the narrative drive of *Lords of Finance* and the analytical clarity of *The Deficit Myth*.

## 8. Title and Subtitle

**Is "Money, Trust, and Power" the right title?** Fine, not great. Strengths: it states the three thematic legs cleanly, scans well, sits comfortably next to Tooze and Silber, and looks like a serious book. Weaknesses: it's generic — at least four current books pair "Money" with "Power" — and it tells the reader the themes when it should tell them what the book is *about*. The current title would work for a Brookings policy report.

**Three alternatives:**

1. **"The Tally and the Vault: Five Thousand Years of Money, Trust, and Power"** *(preferred)* — the two physical objects do the metaphorical work (credit/commodity); the subtitle preserves branding continuity; "tally" is unfamiliar enough to intrigue.
2. **"Out of Nothing: How Money Was Made, Forgotten, and Remade"** — lifts Paterson's 1694 phrase (the manuscript's recurring motif); the three-act subtitle tracks the four-part structure; punchy, owns the polemical claim.
3. **"The Sovereign's Dilemma: Money, Power, and the Five-Thousand-Year Argument"** — promotes the book's strongest analytical contribution (the domestic-international asymmetry from Chapter 3) to the title.

If the current title is kept, the subtitle has to do real work. Try: "Money, Trust, and Power: A Five-Thousand-Year History of How Money Really Works"; or "Money, Trust, and Power: From Clay Tablets to the End of Gold." Either is better than the current naked title.

## 9. The Honest Verdict

**Would I take this to the editorial board?**

Not in its current form. I would take it to the board after one substantial revision. Specifically: a 6-month revision focused on the issues in §2 (commit to a register, kill the textbook voice, audit the contemporary parallels, rebalance the human-cost set pieces, trim Part IV by 4,000 words, expand Part II by 1,500 words). After that revision, I would take it to acquisitions with a strong recommendation.

I would *not* recommend taking it out as-is to a major trade house. It would acquire at a serious university press as-is; it would be passed at PublicAffairs, Norton, or Knopf in its present form — not because the work isn't there, but because the prose isn't yet at the level the comp titles demand and the manuscript doesn't yet know what it wants to be.

**What I would tell the author privately about the work that remains.**

Three things, in order.

**First: you have to make a decision.** You're trying to write *Lords of Finance* and *The Deficit Myth* simultaneously. They're both fine books. They're not the same book. The hybrid you've produced has the polemic's certainty and the narrative history's nuance, and they cancel each other out. Pick a lane. If you pick the narrative-history lane (which I would strongly recommend, given your archival work and your scene-writing strength), you have to soften the polemic verbs and trust the historical evidence to make the argument. If you pick the polemic lane, you have to commit harder, accept the fight, and stop hedging the prescriptions. The middle position you're in now is the worst of both.

**Second: stop telling me what you've just told me.** You're a more confident historian than your prose admits. The manuscript repeats its arguments because the author doesn't yet trust the reader to retain them. Trust the reader. They will retain them. Cut every bolded conclusion sentence, every "the lesson was clear," every chapter-opening recap of the last chapter, every chapter-closing preview of the next. That alone reclaims 5,000 words and dramatically improves pacing.

**Third: the Mesopotamian widows, Backwell, Harry Todd, Heslop, Kono — these are your book.** They are the difference between this being a serious monograph and being a strong list title. Audit every chapter and ask: where is the human face? Add one to Chapter 6. Add one to Chapter 12. Promote Paterson and Overstone to actual characters. Give Newton more time. Give Volcker his due. The macroeconomic argument is the bones; the human-scale narrative is the body. You have the bones. The body needs another revision pass.

If those three things happen, this is an 80–82, acquirable at PublicAffairs or Basic with a $75–125k advance and a serious campaign. With one further revision focused on prose-line writing and the title/subtitle, it's an 85, on the *Lords of Finance* / *The Power of Nothing to Lose* shelf, in conversation with Ahamed and Silber and Tooze.

You're closer than the rough patches in this letter might suggest. The hardest part of writing this kind of book — knowing what story to tell and why it matters now — is already done. What remains is craft.

I'd be glad to work on the revision.
