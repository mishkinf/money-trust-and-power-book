## Persona 1 — Trade nonfiction acquisitions editor (Big 5)

### Long-form review (acquisitions memo)

**Book in one paragraph.** *Money, Trust, and Power: The Five-Thousand-Year Case Against the Household Budget* is a 5,000-year economic history arguing that money is institutional credit, not a commodity; that taxation drives currency demand; and that productive capacity, not gold or accounting balances, is the binding constraint on what currency-issuing governments can spend. The book opens with a Preface that frames the thesis through the 1797 Bank Restriction and Nixon's 1971 closure of the gold window, and proceeds in four parts: Mesopotamian temple credits and merchant networks (Pt. I); the goldsmith catastrophe and the founding of the Bank of England (Pt. II); the gold standard's accidental rise from Newton's 1717 mint adjustment, the Bank Restriction, and the Currency School's institutional victory in 1844 (Pt. III); and the interwar collapse, Bretton Woods, the breaking of 1971, and the post-fiat record from Volcker through 2008, COVID, and 2021–22 inflation (Pt. IV), closing with an Epilogue that takes seven explicit policy positions. Recurring protagonists — Backwell, Attwood, Keynes, Volcker — anchor the structural argument across centuries, paired with named human stories (the Todds at Boldon, "Farmer A" in Iowa, the Hagedorns, the Preuß family, Maureen McKenzie, Renee Spero, Michinao Kono) that put distributional consequences on the page.

**Target reader.** The college-educated reader of Liaquat Ahamed, Adam Tooze, Stephanie Kelton, and David Graeber. Specifically: readers who finished *Lords of Finance* and wanted the longer view; readers who read *The Deficit Myth* and wanted the historical scaffolding; readers who read *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* and wanted a tighter argumentative spine; the *Crashed* audience who has discovered they want to understand monetary architecture rather than just crisis narrative. Secondary: policy-curious professionals (lawyers, financial-services people, central-bank watchers); economics undergraduates and MA students; the heterodox-econ-curious left; the sound-money/Austrian-curious right who want to see their tradition steelmanned and answered. This is not a beach book and not a textbook. It sits exactly where serious trade nonfiction in this category sits.

**Marketing hooks.** Five strong ones. (1) The household-budget frame: a confrontational title with a quantifiable enemy that every reader has heard their politician deploy. (2) The paired-vindication structure ("Attwood was right and Peel was right; the question is which worry to architect against"): genuinely fresh, repeated across Chs. 8, 11, 12B, and the Epilogue, and the kind of analytical move that wins the long-review treatment in the *NYRB*, *LRB*, and *The Atlantic*. (3) The named-human-cost vignettes: Harry Todd, Farmer A, the Preußes, Renee Spero. These are *Lords of Finance*-grade, and they are everywhere. (4) The current-relevance bridge: every chapter ends in 2008/2010/2015/2020/2021/2025, including the China Treasury position, the eurozone, and CBDC governance. (5) The steelman discipline: the book takes Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, and Hayek seriously, by name, on their own terms. That earns coverage from outlets that would dismiss a pure MMT-trade book on sight.

**Structural concerns.** Three real ones. First, length. At an estimated 200,000+ words across sixteen files, this is at the long edge of what the category will sustain; Ahamed is ~120k, Kelton ~80k, Tooze's *Crashed* ~250k but with the propulsion of a single decadal narrative this book cannot replicate. Second, the four-protagonist anchor system (Backwell-Attwood-Keynes-Volcker) is named explicitly in the Preface but executes unevenly — Backwell carries Chs. 3-5 strongly, Attwood Chs. 7-9, Keynes 9-10, Volcker 10-12B; what's missing is the Mesopotamian and medieval anchor, and the early chapters lean on Datini and the goldsmiths in ways that don't quite land as personal narrative the way the later figures do. Third, Ch. 12A and 12B together are doing too much — operational Treasury mechanics, a Weimar set-piece, the Japan vignette, the cryptocurrency engagement, the 1970s, Volcker's recession, the public-choice/fiscal-dominance/knowledge-problem critiques, *and* the rules-vs-stabilizer institutional debate. That sequencing is structurally explicable (validation, then tests) but cognitively dense; the Weimar Klemperer/Preuß material in 12A, magnificent on its own terms, may belong upstream as its own set-piece.

**Editorial conditions.** I would acquire with five conditions. (1) Cut 15-20%. The cumulative effect of paired-vindication invocations across Chs. 8, 11, 12B, and Epilogue is repetition; pick the strongest two placements. (2) Reposition the Klemperer/Preuß hyperinflation material as a standalone scene earlier in the manuscript or in its own short chapter — it is the strongest sustained writing in the book and is currently embedded in a comparison table. (3) Strengthen Pt. I protagonists. The Amat-Sîn/Šāt-Sîn tablet is good but underdeveloped against what Ahamed achieves with his four central bankers; Datini works hard but the chapter ends without him. (4) Add a brief reader's-orientation note at the start of Pt. IV signaling that 12A and 12B form a paired structure (validation/tests) — readers will not anticipate the split. (5) The Epilogue's seven-position close is brave and necessary, but Position 7 (trade architecture, Keen-and-Keynes-aligned divergence from canonical MMT) is doing real work that is not fully set up upstream — either expand the Bretton Woods chapter's bancor case to include the trade-asymmetry argument explicitly, or trim Position 7 to a footnote.

**Recommendation.** This is a serious, ambitious, and largely successful book. The thesis is clear, the historical material is rigorous, the steelmans are real, and the named-human-cost storytelling is the equal of the best in the category. The argumentative spine is stronger than Graeber's *Debt* and the historical depth is greater than Kelton's *Deficit Myth*; the prose does not match Ahamed's, but the ambition is comparable. With the editorial conditions above, this is a book that can be positioned alongside *Lords of Finance* and *Crashed* as a serious trade-nonfiction account that bookstore buyers will stock and reviewers will cover. Without them, it risks being read as an MMT manifesto in historian's clothing — which would underserve what the manuscript actually delivers.

**Verdict: acquire with revisions.**

## Persona 2 — Working economic historian

### Long-form review (essay-review)

**Money's Long Memory: Institutions Over Metal**

There is a particular kind of trade economic history book that the United States seems to produce every decade or so, and that the post-2008 environment has produced more frequently: the long-arc synthesis that takes a contested technical question — what is money? — and walks the lay reader through five thousand years of evidence in service of an argument the academic discipline has largely conceded but the political community has not. Graeber's *Debt* belongs to this tradition. So do Ahamed's *Lords of Finance*, Eichengreen's *Globalizing Capital*, and, in a different register, Kelton's *The Deficit Myth*. The manuscript under review — provisionally titled *Money, Trust, and Power: The Five-Thousand-Year Case Against the Household Budget* — is a serious entrant in that crowded field. It is also, on the evidence assembled here, one of the more honest of them.

The argument is the chartalist-credit-theoretic one: that money originates in obligation rather than barter, that taxation is what generates baseline currency demand, and that the binding constraint on what a currency-issuing state can spend is the productive capacity of the economy it commands rather than the metal in any vault. That much is broadly the consensus among working monetary historians; the manuscript's contribution is not primarily theoretical. What it does is two things the literature has generally not done together. First, it assembles the historical record at the length the modern MMT-aligned literature has not had occasion to make — moving methodically from Mesopotamian temple credits through Datini's bills of exchange, the Stop of the Exchequer, the Bank of England's founding, Newton's accidental gold standard, the 1797 Bank Restriction, Attwood's Birmingham, the 1844 Bank Charter Act, the interwar catastrophe, Bretton Woods, and the post-1971 fiat era. Second, it engages the strongest opposing positions — Friedman-Schwartz, the Currency School, Buchanan-Wagner public choice, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane's Fiscal Theory of the Price Level, Hayek's knowledge problem — on their own terms. The phrase the manuscript uses for the analytical structure that emerges is *paired vindication*: Attwood was right about deflation's real costs, Peel and Overstone were right about discretionary over-issuance; Volcker prevented one cruelty and imposed another; the gold standard "worked and killed." This is the analytical move that distinguishes the book from its closer competitors and that gives the steelmans real load-bearing weight rather than ornamental presence.

The historiographic faithfulness is generally strong, though with characteristic asymmetries. The Mesopotamian material follows Hudson's reading while flagging Van De Mieroop's more cautious interpretation in the body text — an unusually scrupulous handling of a contested zone. The Steinkeller figures for the Ur III *bala* system are cited as reconstructed estimates rather than census certainties. The Bardi-Peruzzi collapse acknowledges that "more recent scholarship has questioned whether Edward's default was the cause of the houses' collapse or the most visible trigger of a deterioration that was already underway across the 1340s" — the standard revisionist position the older Hunt-Sapori narrative has had to absorb. The Backwell material is genuinely impressive: the Pepys diary entries (June 17, 1662; April 12, 1669; July 23, 1666; November 15, 1667) are quoted accurately and weighted appropriately. The "good master, the King, who will not suffer him to be undone" line is the right hinge for the chapter's argument that private credit without public backstop was structurally suicidal. The Paterson "money out of nothing" attribution is flagged as historiographically contested since the nineteenth century — a level of caution most trade books would skip. The Volcker reconstruction at Camp David follows Silber and *Keeping At It* with care; the disagreement between Volcker's preferred coordinated multilateral devaluation and Connally's unilateral package is rendered correctly. The Steil-over-Helleiner choice on Bretton Woods is named explicitly in the Notes, with Helleiner's institutional-design reading flagged as a competing interpretation. The Skidelsky-Thornton-Wicksell-Keynes lineage in Chapter 7 is the kind of detail that signals the author has read the secondary literature.

Where the apparatus shows strain is in two predictable places. The first is the chapter on Bretton Woods, where the Steil-aligned reading produces an account of American power-political dominance that is documentary correct but tonally one-sided; readers who want to weigh White's institutional-design case alongside the procedural-control case will find the latter doing most of the work. The Notes flag this honestly, but the body text does not register the choice with quite the symmetry the rest of the manuscript achieves. The second is the closing Epilogue's seven-position turn, where the author moves from descriptive history to political-economy commitment. The seventh position — the Keynes-and-Keen-aligned divergence from canonical MMT on trade architecture — is genuinely original and deserves its own future treatment; the book's commitment to it is principled but the supporting argument is compressed to a degree the rest of the manuscript would not tolerate elsewhere.

The scene craft is the book's strongest popular-trade asset. Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn at the Larsa temple in 1801 BCE; Datini's December 12, 1399 bill from Bruges; Pepys at Backwell's shop counter watching three thousand crusados weighed; the Stop of the Exchequer playing out across Backwell's Lombard Street ledger; the Hamburg senator's resignation in 1872; the Todd family in Boldon Colliery in 1926, with hessian-bag shoes and snared sparrows; Eva Klemperer at the train-station coffee counter in 1923; Morvan Lebesque on a Métro bench in 1932; Maureen McKenzie and her option-ARM; Charles Kwalwasser and Renee Spero at Lehman; the Hagedorn farm filing Chapter 12 in 1986. These are not decorations. Each scene carries an analytical proposition the chapter is making, and each is sourced precisely enough that the reader can verify it. The Klemperer set-piece is the book's clearest demonstration of what it can do when the historical record gives it material to work with — the salt herring as the metaphor the family memory carried for a destroyed *Mittelstand* fortune is the kind of detail that would be at home in Ahamed.

The ambition is real. The book wants to be the historical foundation that the Kelton-Wray-Tcherneva tradition has needed and that political discourse has been arguing without. Whether it succeeds depends on what one expects from such a book. It does not break new ground in academic monetary history — most of its individual claims are visible in the secondary literature it cites. What it does do, distinctively, is take the steelmans seriously enough that the case it makes is harder to dismiss as cheerleading. The Currency School's worry is conceded as historically vindicated by Weimar through Turkey 2024. Friedman-Schwartz's claim about 1929-33 is treated as the operating premise of every modern central bank, not refuted. Buchanan-Wagner's public-choice critique is named as the institutional question the framework still owes an answer to. Hayek's knowledge problem is acknowledged as something neither institutional tradition fully escapes. This is the book's distinctive register, and it is what makes the *paired vindication* analytic structure work across the manuscript rather than feel like rhetorical insurance.

**Verdict: Original contribution.** Not because the underlying claims are new — they are not — but because the assembly of the historical evidence at this scale, with this discipline about its own steelmans and this scene craft, has not been done in this form. The book occupies a distinct position between Graeber's anthropological-political register and Kelton's policy-explanatory one, with closer kinship to Ahamed's character-driven historical narration than either. It would benefit from a more confident handling of its closing political-economy turn, but the descriptive case is the contribution, and the descriptive case stands.

## Persona 3 — Heterodox / MMT reviewer

### Long-form review (essay-review)

**A Faithful Translation, With One Architectural Concession Worth Naming**

There is a particular kind of book the chartalist tradition has been waiting for, and *Money, Trust, and Power* is closer to it than anything since Graeber's *Debt*. Where Graeber gave us the anthropology, and Kelton gave us the operational primer, Mishkin Faustini gives us the long historical argument — five thousand years of evidence, marshaled at trade-press length, that money is institutional credit, that taxation drives currency demand, and that the binding constraint on what currency-issuing governments can spend in their own currency is the productive capacity of the economy. The thesis is faithful to the source tradition. The treatment is, mostly, faithful to it as well. Where the book hedges, it hedges visibly and tells the reader it is doing so. That is rare enough in trade nonfiction to deserve naming.

Begin with the bones. Chapter 1 lands the credit-first archaeology in the form the modern Hudson-Wray-Tcherneva consensus actually defends: temple ledgers as palatial balance-sheet economies, tally as tax-credit, *jiaozi* circulating because the Song state demanded it back. The Larsa loan tablet vignette — Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn, 33⅓ percent on barley, the goddess Gula as legal creditor — is the kind of specific archaeological grounding that gives the chartalist account weight a thousand abstractions cannot. Chapter 5's reconstruction of the Bank of England's founding is the cleanest popular treatment of "loans create deposits" I have seen in a trade book; the Paterson "money out of nothing" line is properly attributed and properly hedged. Chapter 7 makes the Bank Restriction's twenty-four years do exactly the work the tradition has always wanted them to do: standing empirical proof, predating Knapp by a century, that fiat holds when the institutions behind it are credible, and Pitt's 1798 income tax is read here for what it actually was — the chartalist mechanism working in real time, not a financing operation. Chapter 12A's operational walk-through of the Treasury General Account, citing Stephanie Bell's 1998 Levy paper directly and reaching the conclusion the household-budget metaphor cannot accommodate, is the most accessible English-language statement of the modern MMT operational claim I have encountered in print. If a hostile reader wanted to know what we actually mean when we say spending creates the reserves taxation drains, this is the page to send them to.

The book's second great virtue is its insistence that distributional questions are not afterthoughts but the actual stakes. Attwood's Birmingham, Harry Todd's Boldon, the Iowa farms of 1928 and 1985, the Lehman 401(k)s, Maureen McKenzie's foreclosed ranch, Michinao Kono's twenty years in his parents' house, the Klemperer–Preuß–Berta hyperinflation arc — these are not decoration. They are the chapter-by-chapter human ledger the tradition has insisted on since Innes wrote in 1913 about debt as a social relationship. Chapter 9's Todd reconstruction is the strongest single chapter in the book; the way Keynes's 1925 arithmetic lands at a Durham doorstep in 1926 is precisely the move Lords of Finance and The Big Short use to let macroeconomic abstraction strike a reader as a story. Chapter 12B's Hagedorn farm vignette is the symmetric Volcker-era version, and to the book's credit, it neither looks away from what the disinflation cost the Hagedorns nor pretends Volcker had no case.

This is where the book's architectural concession comes in, and where a reader from this lineage has to decide what to make of it. Faustini steelmans Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, the Currency School, Hayek's knowledge problem, and the metallist tradition through Selgin and White and Ammous, on what he calls *paired vindication*: the side history vindicates as right about a real harm has typically been paired with another side right about a real worry. Attwood is right about deflation; Peel and Overstone are right about discretionary over-issuance. Volcker prevented one cruelty and imposed another. The gold standard worked and killed. The 1844 Bank Charter Act got the form wrong but the worry right. This is not Wray's posture. It is not Mosler's. It moves the book somewhat toward the institutional-design middle that Skidelsky on Keynes occupies, or that Mehrling does on dealer-of-last-resort questions. In some places this lands well — Chapter 12B's reading of Volcker as facing two patterns of cruelty, one acute and concentrated, one chronic and diffuse, with the political system structurally biased toward deferring the concentrated one, is exactly the kind of class-aware account the canonical MMT framing under-weights. In others it pulls a punch the tradition would not have pulled. The Currency School chapter (8) gives Peel and Overstone more credit than the tradition usually does, on the basis of Weimar, Argentina, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Turkey — and the chapter is honest that this is what it is doing.

Whether the concession costs the book is the real question, and my answer is: less than I expected when I started reading. Two reasons. First, the productive-capacity framework holds throughout. Faustini never slips into "we need fiscal discipline" rhetoric in the household-budget sense. The constraint is real resources, the institutional question is real, and the Buchanan tradition is engaged on its own terms but answered — Chapter 12B's empirical observation that advanced fiat regimes have run too tight more often than too loose since 1971 is exactly the move Wray and Kelton make, and it is made well here. Second, the Epilogue's seven positions are, with one important exception, faithful to the canon: the household-budget metaphor is named as the category error it is, the eurozone architecture is read for what it was (Greece 2010 as Churchill 1925), the debt-ceiling theater is named as theater, the Fed dual mandate's bias toward price stability over employment is called for what it is, and the institutional answer to public-choice concerns is explicitly stabilizer-based — the federal job guarantee, with Tcherneva, Wray, Mosler, and Kelton credited by name.

The exception, and it is worth naming: Position 7, on trade architecture, openly diverges from canonical MMT and aligns with Steve Keen's stock-flow consistent reading of the distributional cost of persistent trade deficits, plus Keynes's 1944 Bancor as the architectural model the dollar standard inherited and never solved. Mosler, Kelton, Wray, and Mitchell hold that under floating exchange rates trade deficits are real benefits to the deficit country. Faustini accepts the operational claim and rejects the political-economy conclusion, on grounds Steve Keen has been making for a decade about the offsetting private-sector leverage trade deficits induce. He flags it as a divergence rather than smuggling it in. I disagree with parts of his reading here — the canonical MMT account of currency sovereignty under floating rates is operationally tighter than he gives it credit for — but the disagreement is named, sourced, and adjudicable, which is more than most trade books achieve on a question this contested.

What the book delivers, in sum, is the historical case the modern tradition has needed and not had at this length and accessibility. It is not Kelton's *Deficit Myth* (a focused popular argument), nor Wray's primer (a technical reconstruction), nor Graeber's *Debt* (an anthropological synthesis). It is the long historical argument that the descriptive claims those three books rest on are what the historical record actually produces when read seriously against the strongest objections the orthodox tradition has put against it. The prose is workmanlike rather than literary; it is closer to Tooze's *Crashed* than to Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* in the relationship between argument and scene. The character work is real but uneven — Backwell, Attwood, Keynes, and Volcker are genuine portraits; the merchant of Chapter 2 is more functional. The scholarly apparatus, in the discursive Notes on Sources, is exemplary; Faustini tells you where every contested claim sits in the historiography and which side he picked and why.

I would publicly endorse this book. I would do so with one caveat: a reader expecting the canonical MMT line will find it on five of the seven Epilogue positions, contested moderation on one (the rate-setting question, where the book sides with Wray and Kelton against Mosler explicitly), and outright divergence on one (the trade-architecture position, which goes Keen-Keynes against Mosler-Kelton-Wray-Mitchell). The divergences are honestly flagged. The thesis is faithful. The history is sound. The distributional ledger is honored. The household-budget metaphor is properly destroyed.

**Verdict: Faithful translation, with one architectural concession (paired vindication / steelman-of-Currency-School) that the book argues for openly rather than hiding. I would publicly endorse the book.**

## Persona 4 — Target lay reader (Goodreads)

### Long-form review

**Money, Trust, and Power: The Five-Thousand-Year Case Against the Household Budget**
★★★★ (4 stars)

I picked this up because the subtitle promised the argument I've been waiting for someone to make at book length: that "we can't afford it" is a category error when applied to a sovereign currency issuer. Kelton's *Deficit Myth* made the case but I didn't love it — too argumentative, too quickly current, not enough weight underneath. This is the book I wanted *Deficit Myth* to be. It's also, in places, the book I wished it would stop being.

The strongest stretches are the ones where the author lets a person carry the argument. Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn in Lombard Street — the goldsmith who lent Charles II the money to fight a war and watched his bank die when the King simply refused to pay — is the single best character study I've read in financial-history nonfiction since Lewis's Steve Eisman. We get him weighing crusados with Pepys, building his "little town" in Cornhill, sitting at the emergency Treasury meeting on January 7, 1672 understanding that his life's work was over. He dies in exile in the Netherlands, body shipped home, eventually exhumed. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, eleven years after his death, is the institution he tried and failed to be privately. That's a thesis carried in a person, and it works.

Same with Harry Todd, the Boldon coal miner whose family ate hessian-bag shoes and snared sparrows through the seventeen-month lockout that followed Churchill's 1925 return to gold. The chapter walks you from the Downing Street dinner where McKenna tells Churchill "It will be hell" — straight to a baby born in February 1926 to a household with no wages. That's craft. The Klemperer hyperinflation chapter, the Iowa farmer's debt-deflation arithmetic, Dean and Kaye Hagedorn losing the family farm to Volcker's 20-percent prime rate, Maureen McKenzie losing her Kirkwood home to an option-ARM reset — these are placed across the manuscript so that the abstract argument keeps landing in a kitchen.

The structural move I most admired is what the author calls "paired vindication" — set up first in Chapter 8 with Attwood and the Currency School, then re-deployed in Chapter 12B with Volcker. The point is that the major monetary contests typically had one side right about a real harm and the other right about a real worry, and the institutional choice was over which worry to architect against. This is a more honest analytic frame than I expected from a book whose subtitle picks a fight. The Volcker chapter in particular — Iowa farms versus the passbook-saver retiree whose savings were halved by entrenched inflation — is the rare example of a heterodox-leaning author refusing to flatten a hard case into a clean one.

Where I leaned out: the early chapters drag in a way that almost cost me the book. Chapter 1's clay-tablet section with Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn at the temple of Gula is interesting once but goes on long enough that I started skimming, and the Mises Regression Theorem detour read like a textbook. Chapter 2 on Datini and the Peruzzi is fine but slow. The book really starts at Chapter 3 with Backwell. I'd cut twenty pages from Part I and not lose a thing.

The closing chapters are where the book gets ambitious in ways I'm still uncertain about. Chapter 12A on 2008-versus-2021 is excellent — the cleanest explanation I've read of why the same monetary tool produced opposite results. But Chapter 12B, the institutional-architecture chapter, is the one where the author's voice shows most. He clearly wants the stabilizer-based MMT answer over the rules-based public-choice answer, and he makes a real case, but I noticed myself wanting a colder hand. The Buchanan critique gets engaged seriously and then weighted toward defeat in a way I'm not certain the half-century of evidence quite supports. The Epilogue's "seven positions" section is honest about being the author talking — including a divergence from canonical MMT on trade architecture and the Bancor — and I respected the labeling, but it's the part of the book I'd revisit before recommending.

What surprised me most: how much of the Friedman, Hayek, and gold-bug case the book actually concedes. The 1923 Klemperer hyperinflation chapter, the Argentina-Brazil counterfactual to Volcker's recession, the Currency School's "directional vindication" alongside Attwood's — none of this reads as window-dressing. The book is harder on its own thesis than I expected.

Would I recommend it? Yes, with a caveat. If you read Tooze's *Crashed* and finished it, you can read this — it's at the same level of synthesis with more historical depth and fewer real-time fireworks. If you bounced off Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* (I did, twice), you'll do better here because the human stories are placed more strategically and the analytical scaffolding is clearer. If you finished and disliked Kelton, give this an honest try — the historical case is what was missing from her book.

It's not Graeber. *Debt* had a wider sweep, a more disorienting voice, more genuinely strange archival finds. It's not Lewis — nothing here has the velocity of *The Big Short*. It's tighter than Tooze and warmer than Ahamed. Four stars because the early chapters need cutting, the closing chapter advocates harder than the rest of the book has earned, and the author's voice in the Epilogue is more confident than the historical record I've just read seems to support. Three-and-a-half if the early drag had cost me the book, four-and-a-half if Part I were trimmed.

I will keep thinking about Backwell at the Unicorn. That's the test I use.

The dimension that most clearly distinguishes *well-reviewed* from *must-read* in this category — engagement curve — is the only median below 4 across the bench.

**Highest-leverage revision pass to move up one tier (well-reviewed → must-read):** a single structural-compression pass that addresses the four convergent items in the fix list as one connected piece of work, not four separate revisions. Specifically: **(i)** restructure the 12A/12B seam (compress 12A's inflation post-mortem, lift 12B's Volcker counterfactual forward, decide whether to reconsolidate as one chapter or signpost the validation/tests architecture); **(ii)** consolidate the paired-vindication framework to two strongest placements and sharpen Ch. 8's concession to distinguish the orthodox worry from the orthodox answer; **(iii)** relocate the Klemperer set-piece earlier (Preface, Ch. 9 open, or Ch. 12B open); **(iv)** improve Pt. I scene-craft and cut 15–20 pages from the early chapters. These are all craft fixes. They are addressable in a single revision pass. Together they target every reviewer's lowest-scoring dimensions (engagement curve, narrative momentum) and the dimension whose median was pulled down by P3 alone (character & scene craft, via the composite-figure replacement P3 named).

**Strategic trade-offs requiring author decision (not craft fixes):**
- **Position 7 (trade architecture) — keep, expand, or cut.** Three reviewers flag it as compressed below the manuscript's standard. Expanding requires a Bretton Woods chapter rebuild (P1's path); cutting requires accepting that the Keen-Keynes divergence becomes a future-book signal (P2 and P4's fallback). Either resolves the dimension; the author must decide which.
- **Subtitle — keep or replace.** P2 alone makes this case strongly, but it is a positioning trade-off the author owns: "The Five-Thousand-Year Case Against the Household Budget" reads as policy-position; a narrative-history subtitle (P2 suggests "From Clay to Keystroke: Five Thousand Years of Money's Real Foundation") reads as Ahamed-Tooze register. The decision is between picking a fight (current) and inviting an audience (alternative). v4 made the same observation; the manuscript has not shifted on this.
- **The heterodox/lay-reader pull-apart, persistent from earlier rounds.** P3 wants the paired-vindication framework tightened so the Currency School's *answer* is more clearly rejected even where its *worry* is conceded; P4 wants the institutional-architecture argument's preference more clearly labeled as author commitment. The trade-off is no longer the strategic either-or that v4 surfaced; it is a craft-execution question about how cleanly the framework distinguishes its two registers. That is addressable by revision.
