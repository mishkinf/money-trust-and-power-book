# Adversarial Review: *Money, Trust, and Power*

**Reviewer stance:** Sympathetic to Austrian and monetarist traditions. Reading as the WSJ editorial page, *National Review*, *Reason*, or *Cato* would.

The book is ambitious, often vivid, and at its best (Mesopotamia, the Stop of the Exchequer, the goldsmiths, the Bretton Woods drama) it earns its place near Ahamed and Graeber. But as currently written it telegraphs its conclusions so loudly, and treats so many contested matters as settled, that a hostile reviewer will have an easy time using the book against itself. What follows is intended to be useful, not friendly.

---

## SECTION 1 — The Five Strongest Attack Vectors

### 1. The book frames chartalism as proven and treats every counter-argument as already refuted, while quietly conceding it is not.

The Preface declares that money is "not a thing but a relationship" and that "Gold was never the foundation; it was merely a convenient fiction." Chapter 1 says taxation drives currency demand is a "universal principle wherever states possess fiscal authority" and that the metallist position cannot survive scrutiny. Then in the very same chapter (the section "Once established, institutions ruled") the author concedes: "The Regression Theorem identifies a genuine puzzle… The chartalist answer… is theoretically coherent but historically hard to prove for the *earliest* systems, where the documentary record runs out before the question can be answered cleanly."

A *Reason* or Cato reviewer pounces here. The author has just admitted that the central claim of the book — that credit preceded commodity, that taxation creates currency value at the origin — is unproven for the earliest systems, and then continues to assert it as established for the rest of the book. The Epilogue's "Lesson 1: Credit preceded commodity money" is presented without qualification, even though Chapter 1 already said the matter was unresolvable.

**Vulnerability:** The book wants to have it both ways — chartalism as historical fact when convenient, chartalism as the better-of-two-defensible-readings when pressed. Pick one.

**Fix:** Rewrite the Preface, Chapter 1's opening, and the Epilogue's "lessons" to make the claim what it actually is: *once monetary systems are established*, taxation sustains demand for state-issued money. That is defensible and very nearly uncontested. The stronger origin claim — that money *began* as state credit — is contested and the book should say so up front, not in a buried passage.

### 2. The "manufactured orthodoxy" thesis collapses chartalism's loss into a conspiracy of bondholders.

Chapter 8 ("The Great Forgetting") is the most rhetorically aggressive chapter, and a hostile reviewer will read it as the book's tell. Sample passages:

- "This wasn't the discovery of truth. It was the consolidation of economic theories that served specific interests."
- "Orthodox economics called it sound money. Attwood called it theft through monetary policy. The arithmetic favored him."
- "This wasn't intellectual persuasion. This was institutional capture."
- "The orthodoxy persisted because it served concentrated interests."

This is functionally identical in structure to the conspiracy narratives the author criticizes elsewhere. A *National Review* reviewer will note: Ricardo's quantity theory wasn't adopted because bondholders bribed Parliament; it was adopted because (a) it was simpler, (b) it appeared to explain the inflation of the Restriction era, (c) Britain *won* on resumption, and (d) Henry Thornton — whom the book lionizes — also broadly supported resumption, just more gradually. The book treats Currency School victory as captured-institutions; the same evidence is consistent with Currency School winning a fair intellectual fight on the question their contemporaries thought mattered (price stability and convertibility) while losing the question we now think matters (employment and credit elasticity). That is a much more interesting story than "bondholders bought Parliament."

**Vulnerability:** Sophisticated readers can tell when an author is loading the dice. The "manufactured orthodoxy" frame is currently strong enough that even sympathetic readers will discount the chapter's empirical points (Overstone's correspondence, Norman's tenure, the Political Economy Club gatekeeping — these are interesting!) because they're embedded in advocacy.

**Fix:** Steelman Ricardo and the Currency School. Say what they were trying to solve (the genuinely awful Restriction-era inflation in 1800-1815, the lessons of the assignat collapse, the costs of arbitrary debasement). Then explain why their model failed on its own terms (deposit money, real-bills procyclicality). The reader will trust the book more, and the conclusion will be *more* damning, not less.

### 3. MMT is endorsed without the usual caveats, in language that goes well beyond what serious MMT economists claim.

Chapter 13 says: "This vindicated Modern Monetary Theory's core insight: governments issuing their own currency face real resource constraints, not financial ones." And: "We can mobilize unused resources without inflation. We cannot print past resource scarcity." The Preface says politicians who claim "we're out of money" are repeating a forgotten lie.

Two problems. First, MMT is not vindicated by 2008-2020; that is a vastly contested claim. Olivier Blanchard, Larry Summers, Ken Rogoff, Greg Mankiw, John Cochrane — none of them are gold bugs, and none would accept that 2020 vindicated MMT. The 2021-2022 inflation, which the book attributes almost entirely to supply shocks ("supply chain disruptions accounted for approximately 60% of above-trend inflation"), is in active dispute; Federal Reserve research (Bernanke-Blanchard 2023, Gagnon, Shapiro) gives much more weight to demand-side stimulus, and the IMF's 2022 *World Economic Outlook* attributed a substantial share to fiscal policy. The book picks one side of an ongoing empirical debate and presents it as settled.

Second, the book skips the political-economy critique that even sympathetic readers find devastating. Stephanie Kelton herself acknowledges that the "real constraint is inflation," but the practical question is *who decides when capacity is reached*, and *what stops Congress from spending past that point in election years*. The book waves at this in a single sentence near the end of Chapter 13 ("The answers are institutional, not metallic") but never engages it seriously. Buchanan's public-choice critique of fiscal politics — the *reason* Friedman wanted rules over discretion — is nowhere addressed.

**Vulnerability:** A WSJ reviewer says: "The author has discovered that governments can print money. He has not discovered any reason they will stop."

**Fix:** Add a substantive engagement with the fiscal-political-economy critique. Acknowledge that Kelton's *Deficit Myth* makes a partial argument that even she would not extend to "we cannot print past resource scarcity" without much more institutional architecture. The Japan example the book leans on (260% debt/GDP, no inflation) is genuinely strong; use it more carefully and don't generalize from it to claims Japan-specific institutions cannot bear.

### 4. The 1797-1821 Bank Restriction is presented as proof of fiat success when it is, on the book's own numbers, an ambiguous case.

Chapter 7's claim: "twenty-four years of empirical proof that money worked without metal." The chapter cites cumulative inflation across the period of 22.3% (~1.2% annually) and presents this as moderate.

A monetarist reviewer will note several things:

1. Twenty-two percent cumulative inflation over a generation is *not* trivial. It was a major political controversy at the time precisely because it was substantial. The book frames it as "moderate inflation, not hyperinflation" — true, but the relevant comparison is not Weimar; it is the price stability the gold standard delivered after 1821.

2. The pound's exchange rate fell during the Restriction. The book mentions this once and moves on. A defender of metallism would say: this is exactly what the Bullionists predicted, and exactly what gold prevented.

3. The book attributes Bank Restriction success to "institutional credibility" and "taxation authority." But Britain in 1797 had a credible institution *because* it had honored gold convertibility for 103 years before that. Suspending convertibility burned credibility that had been earned under gold. You cannot run that experiment a second time without restoring the anchor.

4. The Restriction ended in deliberate restoration *because the people who lived through it* judged it inadequate. The book treats this as elite capture; a fair reader notes that Parliament, the Bank, and most commercial opinion wanted gold back. That is evidence about the system's perceived performance.

**Vulnerability:** The book's central historical case for fiat — Britain 1797-1821 — does not bear the weight placed on it. Hostile reviewers will quote the 22.3% inflation figure back at the book.

**Fix:** Present the Restriction more carefully. It proved paper *could* function during emergency; it did not prove paper was *equivalent* to gold; the elites who restored gold were not stupid or captured, they were responding to real pathologies (exchange rate collapse, unstable prices, accumulated political demands for debasement).

### 5. The book systematically conflates correlation with causation on the gold standard's distributional effects.

Recurring claim throughout Part III: "The gold standard's deflation in 1821 destroyed farmers who borrowed during inflation, enriching creditors whose bonds appreciated in value." The author calls this not ideology but "empirical observation."

But the book's own narrative shows that the deflation followed the wartime *inflation* of 1797-1815. Farmers borrowed at high nominal prices that the war's monetary expansion had created. When prices reverted, the real burden rose. A monetarist reviewer says: this is not an indictment of gold; it is an indictment of the inflation that preceded it. The same mechanism would have hurt farmers under any monetary regime that returned to its pre-war level — and would have spared them under any regime that did not let inflation rise during the war in the first place.

The same conflation appears in Chapter 10 (Churchill 1925). The book says Churchill "valued the pound's exchange rate at $4.86 instead of the market rate of $4.77" and that this caused Harry Todd's lockout. But the *reason* the market rate was $4.77 is that Britain had inflated relative to the U.S. during and after the war. Churchill's mistake (and Keynes was right about this) was the *level*, not the choice of gold. Returning to gold at the *market* rate, which is what Keynes recommended, would have produced a successful resumption with a credible anchor. The book never states this clearly. A reader who knows Keynes's *Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* will notice that Keynes's actual argument has been collapsed into a generic anti-gold polemic.

**Vulnerability:** A Cato or *Reason* reviewer says the book mistakes the costs of *inflating during wartime* for the costs of gold, then attributes the costs to the wrong system.

**Fix:** Distinguish more carefully between (a) gold as a regime, (b) the choice of parity at which to enter or re-enter that regime, and (c) the prior monetary policies that determined whether re-entry would require deflation. These are three separate questions and the book runs them together.

## SECTION 2 — Where the Book Treats Contested Claims as Settled

**The Mesopotamian credit-first thesis (Chapter 1).** The book leans heavily on Hudson and Graeber. It cites Schmandt-Besserat once but only to dismiss her token thesis. The genuinely live academic debate — between credit-theorists (Hudson, Wray, Graeber) and metallist/Mengerian readings (Selgin, White, parts of Mises) — is presented as already won. A fair-minded skeptic notes that the cuneiform record shows debts denominated in *silver by weight*, that Ur III used silver standards, and that the relationship between accounting units and settlement media is genuinely contested in the assyriological literature. The book's own concession in Chapter 1's metallist-challenge section already says this; the Preface and Epilogue should match.

**That Ricardo's quantity theory was wrong (Chapters 7-9).** The book writes as if the quantity theory has been refuted. It has not. Friedman won the Nobel in part for vindicating a sophisticated quantity theory. Bernanke's 2002 apology to Friedman, which the book quotes approvingly, is *literally* a vindication of monetarism — the Fed caused the Depression by letting M2 collapse. The book treats this as evidence for chartalism. It is evidence for monetarism. A *National Review* reviewer will note the book uses Friedman's framework when convenient (Bernanke's apology, the 2008 prevention narrative) and rejects it when not (the 1970s, where Friedman's predictions held remarkably well).

**That the Real Bills Doctrine "caused the Great Depression" (Chapter 7).** The book's claim: "Friedman and Schwartz later demonstrated that this monetary contraction *caused* the Great Depression." Friedman and Schwartz did demonstrate the monetary contraction; the *causes* of that contraction are still debated. Eichengreen attributes much to gold-standard mentality, others to specific Fed personalities, others to the structure of the 1913 Fed. The book picks the Real Bills explanation and assigns it sole agency. This is presented with no qualification.

**That the gold standard had a "deflationary bias" that hurt workers (Chapters 6-8).** The empirical record is more mixed than presented. The classical gold standard era (1880-1914) saw real wage growth for British workers of roughly 1-1.5% per year — the strongest sustained period of real-wage growth before WWII. Total factor productivity, real income per capita, and the size of the British middle class all grew under the standard. The book mentions none of this. It cites the deflation of 1873-1896 (the "Long Depression") as proof of harm, but the actual macroeconomic record of that period is contested — Friedman and Schwartz, and more recently David Hackett Fischer, argue real growth was strong. A fair-minded skeptic would say: the gold standard had real costs, but it also had real benefits the book never names.

**That monetary regimes are "redistributive choices."** This phrase, which appears in the Preface, Chapter 6, and the Epilogue, is doing a lot of work. It is true that any monetary regime has distributional consequences. It is *not* obvious that any monetary regime is therefore equivalent to a redistributive policy choice. A neutral monetary regime that produces predictable outcomes is different in kind from a discretionary regime whose outcomes depend on which faction controls the central bank in any given decade. The book treats these as the same. They are not.

**That the eurozone's design flaw is comparable to the gold standard (Chapters 3, 10, 12).** The Greek crisis as gold-standard-replay is rhetorically powerful but historically slippery. Greece chose to join the euro. The eurozone is a political union with explicit fiscal rules. The gold standard was a global regime with no central authority. Treating these as functionally equivalent papers over real institutional differences. A reader from *Cato* will note that the book wants to use the euro to attack gold *and* attack fiat austerity *and* attack monetary unions — three different arguments smuggled together.

## SECTION 3 — Where Opposing Views Are Misrepresented or Strawmanned

**Mises and the Regression Theorem.** Chapter 1 presents the Regression Theorem fairly and even concedes "it has never been fully refuted on its own terms." Then the book proceeds as if it had been refuted. Mises's actual position is more sophisticated than the book lets on: he did not deny that state institutions could *sustain* fiat money, only that they could not *originate* monetary value without prior commodity use-value. The book's Mesopotamian evidence does not actually disprove this — silver-by-weight accounting is consistent with the regression — but the book presents it as if it does.

**Hayek.** Mentioned twice in passing. Hayek's *Denationalization of Money* and his entire monetary-competition research program are not engaged at all. A reader from the Hayekian tradition will find this conspicuous. Hayek would not have endorsed the gold standard; he favored competitive private currencies. The book's framing of "gold vs. state fiat" as exhaustive misses an entire family of positions on the right.

**Friedman.** Mentioned mostly as cover for the book's own claims (the 2002 Bernanke apology). Friedman's actual monetary framework — k-percent rules, the long and variable lags, the 1968 presidential address on the natural rate of unemployment — is absent. Friedman's prediction in the early 1970s that abandoning Bretton Woods would not cause hyperinflation but would require institutional discipline is exactly vindicated by the post-1971 record, but the book presents the post-1971 record as if it vindicated chartalism instead. Friedman would have read Chapter 13's account of 2008-2020 as describing a successful application of *monetarist* lessons (Bernanke as scholar of the Depression), not chartalist ones.

**Selgin and free banking.** Chapter 9 dismisses Scottish free banking in two paragraphs, claiming it "dissolves upon examination" because Scotland operated within British state frameworks. This is not the steelman. Selgin's actual claim is that competitive note issue produced fewer panics, more elastic money supply, and lower transaction costs than the contemporaneous English system — and that this happened *despite* the lack of a lender of last resort, not because of state backstops. Even if you reject Selgin's policy conclusions, his empirical record is one the book should engage. Currently it is dismissed in a way that will read to anyone familiar with the literature as evasive.

**Rothbard.** Not engaged. The book attacks gold-standard advocates generically without naming the strongest version of their argument: that fiat money's flexibility is precisely the feature that allows fiscal capture. A serious engagement with Rothbard's *Mystery of Banking* or *What Has Government Done to Our Money?* would strengthen the book by giving it a real opponent.

**The Bullionists.** Chapter 7 paints them almost entirely as creditor stooges. But the Bullionist Report of 1810 is actually one of the great documents of empirical economic reasoning — Henry Thornton's contributions to it included the proto-Wicksellian insight that the book *praises* later in the same chapter. The book uses Thornton against the Bullionists, even though Thornton was one of them. This is not a small error.

**Modern fiscal hawks.** The "household budget metaphor" is treated as economic illiteracy ("the metaphor is false"). Fair-minded economists who use household analogies (Mankiw, Ferguson, Cochrane) are not denying that governments issue currency; they are making claims about *political* sustainability. The book treats their position as a misunderstanding of accounting when it is actually a claim about fiscal incentives. This is the central mistake the book makes about the strongest version of its opposition.

## SECTION 4 — Where the Polemical Tone Undercuts the Argument

The substantive case for the book's main claims is much stronger than the rhetoric. The rhetoric is hurting the argument.

Examples that will read badly to undecided readers:

- **Preface:** "These aren't ideological claims but empirical observations documented across millennia." When an author has to *insist* a claim is not ideological, the reader becomes more suspicious, not less.

- **Preface:** "When central banks claim to be 'out of ammunition' or politicians insist 'we can't afford' social programs while creating trillions for bank bailouts, they are making political choices about distribution and calling them technical constraints." The 2008 bank rescues had a real economic rationale (preventing the payment system from freezing) that the book elsewhere accepts. Asserting bad faith here undercuts the book's own analysis later.

- **Chapter 6:** "Honour, it turned out, was an abstraction purchased with other people's ruin." This is a literary flourish that will read to skeptical reviewers as an unearned moral judgment.

- **Chapter 8:** "Orthodox economics called it sound money. Attwood called it theft through monetary policy. The arithmetic favored him." Calling 19th-century resumption "theft" is exactly the move that makes a book look like advocacy. Even readers sympathetic to the distributional point will discount it.

- **Chapter 10:** "But the rentier class, the Bank of France, and conservative politicians feared inflation more than unemployment. They prioritized creditors' gold-denominated claims over workers' livelihoods." The phrase "rentier class" is doing political work the surrounding economic argument doesn't need.

- **Chapter 11:** Harry Dexter White is described as having been "explicit" about wanting "American power." The historical record is consistent with this, but the book's tone treats it as if White had no economic argument at all. He did — Bretton Woods *was* superior to Keynes's bancor on certain dimensions (transparency, enforceability, settlement finality). The book's contempt for White's position weakens the book's own analysis of bancor's merits.

- **Chapter 13:** "Volcker chose to break inflation not by addressing supply constraints but by deliberately engineering the worst recession since the Great Depression." This frames a textbook macroeconomic decision as moral failing. There is a real distributional critique to be made — the costs of the Volcker disinflation fell heavily on workers — but the framing here will lose every reader who thinks the Volcker disinflation was, on net, the right call. Many such readers exist, including most of the modern Fed.

The cumulative effect: a reader starts the book thinking the author has a strong case. By Chapter 8 the reader is wondering if every interpretation has been tilted. By Chapter 13 the reader is checking footnotes. The author should *want* the reader to do that, but only after the case has been made — not before.

## SECTION 5 — Where the Book Is Genuinely Strong

A hostile critic must concede the following:

**The Stop of the Exchequer chapter (Chapter 3 / Chapter 4).** Edward Backwell, Robert Vyner, the Sunday Treasury meeting, the legal aftermath — this is first-class financial history. The 0.01% effective annual return calculation is the kind of detail that earns trust. The parallel to 2008 shadow banking is drawn carefully and is well-supported. Lean into this.

**The Datini bill of exchange in Bruges/Barcelona (Chapter 2).** The reconstruction of December 12, 1399 is excellent. The mechanism — *cambium non est mutuum*, the disguised loan structure, the 12.5% effective rate — is exactly what Ahamed-style narrative nonfiction does well. This will read to skeptical economists as the book at its best, because the historical evidence is doing the argumentative work, not the rhetoric.

**The Bretton Woods chapters (11 and 12).** The Keynes-White dynamic is well-handled. The Robbins quote ("We know we will be beaten and we hope to avoid being humiliated") and the Hamlet allusion at the end of Chapter 11 are genuinely affecting. The Triffin dilemma is well-explained for a general reader. The "kid who owns the ball is captain" framing works.

**The narrative structure of contemporary parallels.** Beginning each chapter with a modern hook (2008, 2010 Greece, 2020 COVID, 2025 China-dollar) is a smart trade-nonfiction device that will play well with the *Lords of Finance* audience. It works most cleanly when the parallel is structural rather than rhetorical — Chapter 3's medieval-vs-Greek-debt comparison is the strongest use, Chapter 13's COVID-as-MMT-vindication is the weakest.

**The Restriction-era detail.** The James Woodforde diary, the wheat-price collapse, the named insolvents (Gigney, Leach, Cantwell) — this is the book successfully doing what trade nonfiction is supposed to do: making the abstract concrete. Even where the analytical framing overreaches, the human cost is real and the documentation is solid.

**The 2008-2020 contrast.** The argument that 2008 QE was non-inflationary because the output gap was wide, and that 2021 inflation reflected supply constraints colliding with stimulus into a near-full-employment economy, is mostly right and well-presented. It would be even stronger if the book conceded that the demand-side contribution to 2021-22 inflation is also significant — this would actually *strengthen* the book's productive-capacity framework, because both elements are doing work.

**The institutional thread.** The book's deepest insight — that monetary regimes work when the institutions behind them are credible, that gold's apparent automaticity always concealed active management, that fiat's flexibility creates new institutional demands rather than removing them — is genuinely important and largely correct. This is the argument the book should foreground. When the author trusts this argument and lets the historical evidence carry it, the book is excellent. When the author reaches for "manufactured orthodoxy" and "bondholder capture," the book becomes a polemic that distracts from its own best material.

## Closing note

The book's central thesis — money is a social institution, gold was never the foundation, fiat works when institutions work — is defensible and important. The book will be a better book, and harder to dismiss, if it makes that case with the steel of opposing views intact rather than with the manufactured-orthodoxy frame that currently animates Part III. The author has the historical evidence to make a strong argument. The argument does not need the rhetoric.

A few specific revisions would do most of the work:

1. Make the Mesopotamia chapter's metallist concession the actual position of the book, not a buried footnote.
2. Replace "manufactured orthodoxy" framing in Chapter 8 with a more honest account of why the Currency School won the argument that contemporaries thought they were having (price stability, convertibility) and lost the argument we now think they were having (employment, credit elasticity).
3. Engage Friedman, Hayek, Selgin, and the public-choice critique of fiscal politics seriously in Chapter 13. Concede that fiat's flexibility is a constraint problem, not a solved problem.
4. Distinguish carefully between gold-as-regime and gold-at-wrong-parity in Chapters 6, 7, and 10. Keynes himself made this distinction; the book conflates them.
5. Drop or reframe the worst rhetorical tics ("manufactured orthodoxy," "rentier class," "theft through monetary policy," "honour purchased with other people's ruin"). The history is more powerful when allowed to speak.

The book has the bones of something that could sit on the shelf with Ahamed and Graeber for a long time. Right now it reads like a draft that hasn't yet decided whether it is history or argument. Choose history, and the argument will come through stronger.
