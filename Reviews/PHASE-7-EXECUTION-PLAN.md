## Upfront design decisions (require user input)

### Decision 1 — MMT appendix: cut or revise?

The v2 critique flagged the MMT appendix as the manuscript's now-weakest piece. Three of five personas flagged it independently. The economist called it "the part of the book where the steelmanning discipline visibly slips." The editor called it "a liability... cut the appendix and let Chapter 12 stand."

**Option A — cut entirely.** Bolder, simpler. Removes the descriptive-not-prescriptive framing the economist named as a "rhetorical dodge" and the flat 2021-supply-chain claim that contradicts Chapter 12's negotiated position. Costs ~3,000–4,000 words of the book's word count, all of which the body chapters now cover or qualify.

**Option B — substantively revise.** Keep the appendix as the explicit chartalist-tradition reading list and policy summary; revise to (a) drop the descriptive-not-prescriptive framing, (b) engage Sargent-Wallace inside the appendix instead of asserting its irrelevance, (c) name where 2021 hurt the framework rather than asserting supply chains explain everything, (d) remove the Kelton/Wray/Mosler reading list or move it into Notes on Sources where it sits alongside the other intellectual-tradition citations.

**Option C — keep as-is.** Defensible only on the argument that the appendix serves a specific reader (the academic-adjacent reader who wants the lineage made explicit) and that no body-chapter work fully replaces that function. The v2 evidence suggests this option costs verdicts.

**Recommendation: Option A (cut).** The appendix is doing work the body now does better, and its contradiction with Ch.12's negotiated position is the kind of inconsistency an alert reviewer (Krugman, Wolf, FT Alphaville) will catch and use to discredit the body chapters. The conservative critic's grudging respect for the appendix's honesty about MMT lineage will not be lost; the Notes on Sources can preserve the explicit acknowledgment of the chartalist tradition.

### Decision 2 — Part I compression?

The editor's v2 memo: "Chapters 1–3 feel obligatory; the book genuinely begins at Chapter 4 (Backwell)." The proposal would compress Chs. 1–3 into a single 8,000-word prologue and let the institutional revolution start earlier.

**Option A — full compression.** Aggressive structural change. Risks losing the Mesopotamian temple credit and Datini bills-of-exchange material that opens the book and that two of the v2 personas (NYT, historian) named as effective scene-setting.

**Option B — light tightening.** Cut the thesis-restating tic at the end of Chs. 1 and 2; trim the modern-parallel coda paragraphs in Ch.1 (Greece-as-Edward-III is a Ch.3 closer, not a Ch.1 one); leave the structure intact.

**Option C — no change.** Hold structure; rely on threading from Ch.3 onward to give Part II the spine.

**Recommendation: Option B.** Full compression is a big move late in revision; light tightening costs little and addresses the editor's actual complaint (the chapters feel obligatory because of the thesis-restating, not because the material is inert). The Larsa tablet and Datini scenes are doing useful opening work; do not throw them out.

### Decision 3 — Length target?

Current: ~72,000 words. Editor's call: 90k character-led. NYT's call: cut 20%. Historian's call: 72k tight against Lewis. Threading adds an estimated 7,800–11,700 words; cuts (MMT appendix if cut, modern-parallel trim, thesis-restating cuts) recover 4,500–7,500 words.

**Net target: 75,000–82,000 words.** This is the natural landing of the planned edits and matches the comp neighborhood (Tooze ~150k is the ceiling, but Graeber ~100k and Mehrling's The New Lombard Street ~80k are realistic peers). No additional padding for length; no additional cuts to defend a 72k target.

## Thread bibles

Each thread is one figure carried across multiple chapters as a recurring rather than one-chapter presence. The threading is the load-bearing craft work; the chapter-by-chapter pass implements these bibles plus the smaller fixes.

### Thread 1 — Edward Backwell (1618–1683)

Currently anchors Ch.4 in full; appears nowhere else. Existing material is excellent (the Sunday Treasury meeting, the post-1672 begging letters, the Netherlands exile, the 1698 private Act).

**Ch.3 entry (~800–1,200 words).** Open or section-break with Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn in the late 1660s — the goldsmith-banker who has built the only extant private credit system in England, lending to the Stuart Crown at a scale no predecessor matched. Ground in Pepys diary entries (Pepys was a Backwell client and mentions him repeatedly in 1666–68). The chapter is currently about the sovereign's dilemma in the abstract; Backwell is the human face of the counterparty whose ruin the dilemma will produce. Sets up Ch.4 as the catastrophe Backwell did not see coming.

**Ch.4 (existing).** Already deepened in Phase 3. No major changes; possibly thin one or two paragraphs of analytical exposition that compete with the existing Backwell scenes for breathing room. Add the Notes on Sources entry the historian flagged as missing.

**Ch.5 payoff (~400–600 words).** Position the Bank of England's 1694 charter as the institutional answer to what destroyed Backwell. Parliamentary backing instead of personal Crown credit. Lender-of-last-resort design instead of solo counterparty risk. Backwell died in 1683 in Netherlands exile; he never saw the Bank that resolved what destroyed him. Use the eleven years between his death and the Bank's founding as the chapter's framing arc. Brief — integrated into existing chapter as a closing or section-break passage, not a new section.

**Source base for new material:** Pepys diary (Latham/Matthews edition, multiple references); Roseveare on the Restoration financial system; Carruthers City of Capital on Backwell's pre-1672 lending; existing research on Backwell already cited in Ch.4.

### Thread 2 — Thomas Attwood (1783–1856)

Currently anchors Ch.8. Available threading carries him backward into Ch.7 (where he is currently mentioned only briefly via the "Attila the Hun" quote) and forward into Ch.9 (where the Todd family material can be framed as Attwood-vindicated-too-late). The Attwood thread is doubly useful: it delivers the bestseller-tier protagonist work AND it solves the v2 critique's structural complaint about Ch.8 being a closed prosecution.

**Ch.7 entry (~800–1,000 words).** Open a section with Attwood at twenty-eight in his Birmingham manufactory during the 1821 deflation, watching workers laid off as Peel's resumption bites. This is where his lifelong conviction forms. Frame the chapter's analytical material on the Bullionist controversy as the intellectual context Attwood was reading and reacting to in the 1820s. He does not anchor the chapter; he opens a thematic line that pays off in Ch.8.

**Ch.8 narrative-architecture rebalance.** Maintain the explicit steelman currently at line 91 (Peel and Overstone "had identified a real worry... an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will, eventually, over-issue"). Restructure the chapter so the analytical engagement on the Currency School's strongest claim comes BEFORE the institutional-capture material on Overstone's £2 million, the Norman family's continuity, and the Political Economy Club. The institutional-capture material stays — the v2 economist did not ask for it to be cut, only to stop pre-empting the analytical argument. Net change to chapter word count: ~0; structural rearrangement only.

**Ch.9 payoff (~400–600 words integrated, mostly recasting transitions).** Frame the Todd family at Boldon Colliery via the Attwood prediction. The 1926 General Strike is the disaster Attwood predicted in 1832 arriving on schedule. This is light add — most of the material exists; what changes is the connective tissue tying Todd's miner's family to a forecast made ninety-four years earlier by a Birmingham manufacturer the reader has now seen across two chapters. The vindication-too-late frame turns the chapter ending from a discrete tragedy into a thread closing.

**Source base for new material:** Briggs *The Age of Improvement* on Attwood's Birmingham years; Moss on the Birmingham Political Union; Miller's *Political Unionism*; Hilton on the 1820s political economy. Most of these are already in the Notes on Sources for Ch.8; the Ch.7 and Ch.9 threading does not require new research, only redeployment.

### Thread 3 — Paul Volcker (1927–2019)

Currently anchors a section of Ch.12. Available threading carries him backward into Ch.10 (graduate student / early Treasury career during Bretton Woods) and Ch.11 (Under Secretary of the Treasury at Camp David, August 1971, witnessing the system break). The Volcker thread is the spine the post-WWII chapters currently lack.

**Ch.10 entry (~600–900 words).** Open a section with young Volcker at Princeton (BA 1949), Harvard MA 1951, the New York Fed in the 1950s and Treasury in the early 1960s. He spent his life inside the system Ch.10 describes the founding of. Frame his early career not as biographical color but as the inside view of how the Bretton Woods institutions actually operated in the decade before they began to break. The chapter is currently about the system's design; Volcker is the man who watched it run.

**Ch.11 entry (~700–1,000 words).** Volcker as Under Secretary of the Treasury during the August 1971 Camp David weekend. Pull from his memoir *Keeping At It* (2018) directly: the line "If the British were going to take gold for their dollars, it was clear the game was indeed over" is the chapter's central document. He was in the room where the gold window closed. This is the chapter's strongest available scene; the existing Ch.11 material can frame the weekend through Volcker's eyes rather than through the more familiar Connally/Nixon viewpoint. Likely integrates as a section opener or a sustained scene mid-chapter.

**Ch.12 (existing).** The 1979–82 disinflation material stays. Frame in opening: the man who saw the system break in 1971 was the one who imposed the discipline that remade it. This is one or two sentences of recasting at the chapter's Volcker section — the rest of the material is unchanged.

**Source base for new material:** Volcker, *Keeping At It* (2018) for the Camp David weekend and the early career; Silber, *Volcker: The Triumph of Persistence* (2012) for the Princeton/Harvard/Treasury years; Garten *Three Days at Camp David* (2021) for the August 1971 weekend; existing Volcker material in Ch.12 already grounds the 1979–82 disinflation.

### Thread 4 — John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946)

Currently anchors Ch.10 in full (Bretton Woods, the bancor, the heart attack on the hotel stairs). Light add only — Keynes is already the heaviest-voiced figure in the book; what's missing is recurrence in the chapters that frame his Ch.10 arrival.

**Ch.7 entry (~300–500 words).** Keynes's intellectual descent from Henry Thornton — the 1802 *Paper Credit* tradition, the Real Bills doctrine Keynes would later overturn. Brief; placed where the chapter currently treats Thornton, with one or two sentences forward-pointing to the *General Theory* that this lineage produced.

**Ch.9 entry (~300–500 words integrated).** Pull more of Keynes's *Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* (1925) directly into the chapter's narrative. The 1925 polemic should be the chapter's central textual document — currently cited but not voiced. Integrate as quoted passages in the existing Churchill-1925 material, not as a new section.

**Ch.10 (existing).** No major changes.

**Source base:** Skidelsky's three-volume *Keynes* biography for the Thornton lineage; Keynes's *Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* (1925) directly for the Ch.9 material.

## After the chapter pass — global moves

1. **Notes on Sources coherence pass.** Read the back-matter front-to-back. Verify all 12 chapters are represented. Verify that the new entries match the discursive register of the existing entries (Ch.10's Steil-vs-Helleiner choice, Ch.8's Briggs/Miller/Moss historiography). Any cross-references the new entries need to make to existing entries.
2. **Crypto section rebalance (~300–500 words within Ch.12).** The conservative critic's complaint: fiat is judged on its institutional successes AND its Ch.12 failures, crypto on the failures of centralized intermediaries. Rebalance: judge crypto on its protocol record (Bitcoin's monetary-base discipline across the same window the Fed's $9T balance sheet expanded) AND its intermediary failures. Outcome of the rebalance need not change; the framing must be parallel.
3. **Front-to-back read for register and momentum.** Single read across the whole manuscript at one sitting. Catch (a) thesis-restating tic recurrences, (b) modern-parallel mannerisms that survived chapter-level trim, (c) register breaks between humanist scene and graduate-seminar exposition, (d) threaded figures appearing without sufficient setup or paying off without sufficient earlier material. Final pencil edits.
