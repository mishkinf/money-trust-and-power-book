# 1. NYT Book Review staff reviewer

**Money Without Metal: A Five-Thousand-Year Argument**
*A review of "Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work"*

The clay tablet that opens this book is dated to roughly 1801 BCE. A woman named Amat-Sîn and her daughter Šāt-Sîn appear before a temple scribe at Larsa to borrow 622 liters of barley from the goddess Gula. Three witnesses press their seals into the wet clay. The standard interest rate of thirty-three and a third percent is so taken for granted that the tablet does not bother to record it. Nearly four thousand years later, the tablet survives, catalogued as Kress 83, in a private collection in Bochum.

It is a wonderful opening — specific, dignified, weighted with what the author wants the rest of the book to argue: that money has always been a relationship, recorded by an institution powerful enough to enforce it, and that we have known this since before we had alphabets. The author then promises to march that thesis through Mesopotamian temples, medieval Florentine bills of exchange, the Stop of the Exchequer, the founding of the Bank of England, Newton's bimetallic miscalculation, the Bank Restriction, the Currency School's victory in 1844, the interwar gold standard's catastrophic failure, Bretton Woods, Nixon's gold window, the 2008 rescue, and the 2021 inflation. Twelve chapters. Seventy-two thousand words. The scope is, by any standard, immense.

The book mostly earns it. Where it strains, it strains in ways the author appears to know about and tries to honor.

The narrative chapters are the engine. Chapter 4's reconstruction of Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn — the goldsmith-banker ruined by Charles II's 1672 Stop of the Exchequer, who kept the doors open and begged, took ambassadorial work for the same Crown that had broken him, was declared bankrupt in 1682, died in exile in the Netherlands the following year, and required a private Act of Parliament fifteen years after his death to wind up his affairs — is one of the finest pieces of monetary-historical narrative I have read in a decade of reviewing in this category. Liaquat Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* found the same register for Norman, Strong, Schacht, and Moreau. This author finds it for a seventeenth-century banker most readers will not have heard of and makes him the human face of why William Paterson's 1694 proposal had to be different in kind from anything that had come before.

The Harry Todd chapter (Chapter 9) is comparable. Churchill's 1925 return to gold at the pre-war parity is treated by economic historians as a famous error; here it becomes Boldon Colliery, Durham, and a coal miner's family making shoes from hessian coal sacks while baby Henry is born in February 1926 into the middle of a seventeen-month lockout. The Iowa farmer of 1920 who borrows $90,000 conservatively and watches corn fall from $2.00 to $0.41 a bushel because of monetary decisions in Washington and Paris; Maureen McKenzie's 2005 payment-option ARM that cap-balances out from under her in 2008; the Klemperers in Dresden in 1923, watching coffee reprice between two sips — these are the chapters' anchor moments, and the author has a real gift for finding them. They are not decorative. They do the analytical work: they name who, exactly, paid for each policy choice, and they refuse to let the reader treat the costs as line items in a price index.

The author also takes the metallist and conservative critique seriously in a way most books in this genre do not. The preface concedes openly that Friedman-Schwartz on 1929–33 is the operating premise of every modern central bank. Chapter 8 spends a long, unhurried passage explaining that Peel and Overstone were not merely running interference for bondholders — that the worry an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will eventually over-issue is real, recurring, and unanswered by simply pointing at the Bank Restriction's success. Chapter 12 names Hayek's knowledge problem, Sargent-Wallace's "unpleasant arithmetic," Cochrane's fiscal theory of the price level, and Buchanan-Wagner's *Democracy in Deficit*, and does not pretend to have refuted them. It says, with admirable clarity, that the productive-capacity framework "does not, by itself, address" the constraint Sargent-Wallace identifies, and proposes an institutional architecture (symmetric fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils, pre-legislated inflation triggers) that it then admits is "a thinner answer than the public-choice critique probably wanted." This is intellectual honesty of the kind Tooze brings to *Crashed*. It earns trust.

The 2021 inflation chapter is the manuscript's most analytically demanding section, and where it has clearly been most rewritten. The author resists the partisan temptation in both directions. He gives Blanchard, Summers, and the Chicago Fed staff explicit credit for the directional call that the American Rescue Plan would generate inflation. He notes that Krugman, Kelton, Wray, and Yellen all got the magnitude wrong, and quotes their later retractions in their own words. He synthesizes the post-hoc decompositions — Shapiro at the San Francisco Fed, the New York Fed's Amiti-Heise-Karahan-Şahin work, the IMF cross-country evidence — and concludes that 2021–22 was a layered episode "no framework called cleanly." This is not the take a partisan of the productive-capacity framework would write if he were grinding axes. It is the take of a writer trying to stay honest in front of a record that did not vindicate any single theory.

So why isn't this an unqualified rave?

Three reservations, of varying weight.

First, the prose. It is competent throughout, lucid in stretches, but it does not have the line-level music of Lewis or Graeber or, at his best, Ahamed. The book frequently lapses into the historian's tic of restating the chapter premise in an abstract paragraph after a successful narrative scene, as if the author does not entirely trust the scene to have done its work. Chapter 1 ends with an admonitory paragraph that begins "Money, then, is not a thing but a relationship." The reader has been told this, in some form, fourteen times by that point. The book would gain twenty percent of its punch if a hard editor cut every sentence that begins with "What this reveals is" or "The lesson was clear."

Second, the modern-parallel paragraphs. Most chapters end with a paragraph or two pulling the historical episode into 2008 or 2010 or 2020 or 2025. Some of these are excellent — the Greece-as-Edward-III closer to Chapter 3 is genuinely sharp. Others are mechanical, almost reflexive: every chapter must have its modern hook. By Chapter 11 the device has lost its surprise. The book wants to be a five-thousand-year argument and a contemporary intervention, and the seams between those two ambitions sometimes show.

Third — and this is the structural one — the book is at its weakest where its thesis is at its weakest, which is precisely the post-1971 chapter. The author knows this, which is why Chapter 12 has been so heavily revised. But the more honest the chapter becomes about what the productive-capacity framework cannot answer, the more it raises the question of whether the framework can carry the rhetorical weight the earlier chapters place on it. The book sells a thesis in Chapter 1. It half-unsells the same thesis in Chapter 12. A more confident book would either sell harder or pivot the argument; this one threads a needle that some readers will find scrupulous and others will find evasive.

But these are reservations within a recommendation. *Money, Trust, and Power* is a serious, ambitious, and largely successful work of trade nonfiction that does what its category requires: it makes a contested argument with documentary specificity and human consequence, it engages its critics in good faith, and it leaves the reader knowing more about how money has actually worked than ninety-five percent of the published commentary on the subject manages.

**Verdict: QUALIFIED RECOMMEND.**

**Pull quote:** "The clay tablets of Uruk had already answered. Money never was, and never needed to be, a scarce commodity — it was a relationship, recorded by institutions powerful enough to enforce it, and worth precisely what those institutions could compel."

**On the bestseller question.** At the craft level, this manuscript sits in the upper second tier rather than the top. The historical narrative chapters (Backwell, Todd, Iowa farmers, the Klemperers, the Hagedorns, Lebesque's winter on the Métro benches) are at Ahamed's level — concrete, dignified, doing analytical work through scene. The conceptual passages are not at Graeber's or Lewis's level: the prose lacks the controlling voice that makes *Debt* feel like an extended seminar with a brilliant friend or *The Big Short* feel like a thriller. Chapter 12's analytical honesty is admirable but reads more like Tooze's footnotes than Tooze's body text — careful where the bestseller version would be propulsive. The book is also forty thousand words shorter than its competitors, which paradoxically makes it feel less authoritative rather than more disciplined; *Lords of Finance* and *Crashed* earn their length through accumulated narrative weight this manuscript does not quite reach. To bestseller-tier from here would require either a more distinctive prose voice (the Lewis solution) or a single galvanizing argument the author refuses to qualify (the Kelton solution, which this author's intellectual honesty makes structurally unavailable). With aggressive platform support — major podcast circuit, ideally a Tooze or Krugman blurb, and a foreign-rights push into the European policy-debate market where productive-capacity framing has the most political traction — it could reach extended-list bestseller status. Crossing into top-ten would require craft work the manuscript has not yet done.

# 2. Skeptical mainstream economist

Let me say at the outset what this manuscript is not. It is not a polemic in the Kelton or Mosler vein. The author has plainly read the literature he disagrees with, and large stretches of the prose — particularly in the back half — show a writer working hard to render the orthodox case in language its proponents would recognize. That is rarer than it should be in trade books on money, and it deserves credit before I turn the knife. The question for a reviewer in my chair is not whether the author *gestures* toward the other side. It is whether the steelmen survive contact with the punchline.

On the orthodox monetarist case, the answer is qualified yes. Chapter 7's treatment of Friedman is more honest than I expected. The passage at the end of the Real Bills section — "Money does matter for nominal variables; sustained inflation is, in the long run, a monetary phenomenon; and a central bank that lets its banking system collapse is committing in any era the same error it committed in 1931" — concedes the institutional minimum I would insist on, and concedes it without weasel words. The Bernanke 2002 quotation is given its actual force. The retreat the author then makes — that post-1980 velocity instability and QE-era multiplier collapse undermine *operational* monetarism — is, I grudgingly admit, the position much of my own profession has migrated to. So far, this is not Kelton.

But there is a sleight in Chapter 12 I cannot let pass. The line "Two predictions of the inflation deserve credit at the outset" introduces Blanchard and Summers in February 2021, and the chapter does name them, name Bianchi-Fisher-Melosi, and concede that "their directional call was right." Good. Then the next paragraph reframes their argument as "turned on aggregate demand exceeding a fixed productive capacity — what the standard output-gap arithmetic measures" and pivots to the claim that "productive capacity in 2021 was not fixed. It was actively being destroyed and reorganized by COVID." This is the rhetorical move I have been waiting for: concede the prediction, then attribute it to a stylized model nobody actually held. Blanchard's February 2021 piece did not assume capacity was fixed in some textbook sense. It estimated an output gap of $900 billion against $2.8 trillion of stimulus and called the ratio dangerous. That call survived the supply-side reframing the chapter applies to it. The chapter's eventual landing — "the U.S. fiscal package mattered while supply factors did the larger share of the work" with fiscal contributing "on the order of a third" of overshoot — is closer to the post-hoc literature than the framing implies, but the architecture of the section is still designed to leave the reader with the impression that orthodox demand-overshoot was *partially* right while the productive-capacity framework was *more* right. The cited New York Fed staff report (Cox-Del Negro-Giannoni) attributes "roughly two-thirds of total model-based inflation to aggregate demand shocks." That number, taken on its own, is not a productive-capacity-framework win. It is a demand-side win the chapter has folded into a capacity narrative.

On Volcker (Chapter 12, sections "The counterfactual: Argentina" and following), I owe the author an unqualified compliment. The Argentina-Brazil counterfactual is the strongest section of the book. The passage acknowledging that the cost of *not* breaking inflation in 1979 would have fallen on "the passbook saver, the fixed-pension retiree, the wage-earner without indexation in her contract" — and the explicit naming of Bernanke's 2005 essay on Latin American inflation as the analytic framework — is exactly the engagement Volcker's defenders ask for and rarely receive. The closing sentence, "Volcker's contribution was to refuse that calculation," is a generous reading. Even Friedman would have signed it. The Hagedorn farm vignette is held in the same paragraph as the retired wage-earner whose savings the alternative would have eaten. That is the trade Volcker faced, and the chapter names it cleanly. Whether the author *also* concedes the Great Moderation that followed was a real institutional achievement — yes, in line 285: "headline inflation falling from 13.5 percent to 3.2 percent in three years, and the institutional foundation for what later became known as the Great Moderation… they were real." That sentence buys the chapter a great deal of credit with me.

Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane: this is where the manuscript performs above its trade-press category. Lines 327-333 of Chapter 12 do not name-check fiscal dominance and move on. They restate the unpleasant arithmetic correctly ("tighter money now can mean higher inflation eventually"), restate FTPL correctly ("the price level adjusts so that the real value of nominal debt is equal to the present value of primary surpluses"), and then — and this is the part I had not expected — *concede the category error*: "They are not the same claim, and it is a category error to treat them as one. They answer different questions." The book then names UK 1945-51 financial repression, Brazilian indexation, and Turkey 2021-24 as live cases consistent with Sargent-Wallace rather than productive-capacity. The reply — that productive-capacity binds *conditional* on credible fiscal-monetary coordination — is intellectually honest. Cochrane himself would, I think, recognize this as a fair statement of what his theory and the author's framework respectively claim. This is genuine engagement.

On Buchanan-Wagner, the chapter-end architecture (lines 323, 341) is real but thin. Independent fiscal councils, symmetric rules, pre-legislated automatic tightening — these are not new proposals, and naming them is not the same as defending them against the public-choice rejoinder that *the same political incentives that produce chronic deficits will erode the institutions that constrain them*. Buchanan's deepest point is that constitutional constraints get rewritten under fiscal duress. The chapter does not answer this. It gestures at it. Partial credit only.

On the knowledge problem: line 339's admission — "The chapter does not have a clean answer to this objection" — is the most honest sentence in the book. Hayek would not be satisfied, but he would acknowledge engagement. This is rare.

The Currency School treatment in Chapter 8 is where I become a harder grader. Line 91 — the explicit steelman — is competent: Peel and Overstone "had identified a real worry... an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will, eventually, over-issue." Good. But the chapter's narrative architecture — the framing of Attwood as the suppressed truth-teller, the institutional-capture story (Overstone's £2 million, the Norman family's 123-year continuity, control of the Political Economy Club) — overdetermines the verdict before the steelman has a chance to land. A reader emerging from this chapter believes the Currency School won by fortune and gatekeeping rather than by being substantially right about what the assignat collapse and the post-Restriction inflation portended. The 1844 Act *did* fail to anticipate deposit money. That is fair. But the deeper Currency School worry — that fiscal-monetary discretion under political pressure debases — has been validated by enough subsequent episodes (including ones the book itself names: Turkey, Argentina, Weimar) that treating Overstone primarily as a creditor-class operative is unbalanced. The chapter knows this; line 122 explicitly leaves the question of "whether it was the necessary cost of monetary discipline or a distributional choice dressed as natural law" as contested. But the surrounding rhetoric closes what the explicit concession claims to leave open.

The MMT appendix is the manuscript's weakest piece on intellectual fairness grounds. The framing that MMT is "descriptive" rather than prescriptive — "Like Newton describing gravity or Darwin explaining natural selection" — is exactly the framing Krugman and Summers (correctly, in my view) called a rhetorical dodge. The Sargent-Wallace constraint Chapter 12 took seriously does not appear here; the appendix asserts that "Japan avoids crisis despite 260% debt-to-GDP because the debt is yen-denominated" without engaging the Japanese demographic argument or the FTPL point that yen-denomination is necessary but not sufficient. The appendix is the part of the book where the steelmanning discipline visibly slips.

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** Volcker, Friedman-Schwartz, and Sargent-Wallace-Cochrane get serious treatment well above trade-press standards. The 2021 inflation is engaged honestly enough to admit the orthodox call was directionally right and a third of overshoot was fiscal, even while the chapter's framing tries to absorb the win. Buchanan-Wagner and the Currency School get real steelmen that are then rhetorically outflanked. The MMT appendix lapses into the genre conventions the rest of the book had largely transcended. This is not a strawmanning book. It is a book that has done two-thirds of the work of fairness, and where the remaining third holds it back from the first rank.

**On bestseller-tier craft.** No, not yet, and the gap is specifiable. Ahamed and Tooze sustain narrative across a single decade with five or six characters readers come to know; Kelton wins on argumentative clarity at sentence level; Lewis on character voice; Graeber on conceptual provocation per page. This manuscript has Ahamed-quality vignettes (Eva Klemperer's coffee, the Hagedorn diary, Kono's ramen-restaurant collapse, Aunt Berta's salt herring) but they are stranded inside chapters that toggle between humanist set-piece and graduate-seminar exposition without the connective narrative spine Ahamed maintained. The Currency School institutional-capture material reads as research output rather than story. The 2021 inflation section is analytically excellent and structurally inert — it is an essay in a book that needs to be a narrative. Fix: pick three or four through-lines (a family across generations, an institution across centuries, one or two recurring scholarly antagonists) and braid the analytical material into them. Cut perhaps 12,000 words of mid-chapter exposition. The vignettes are already there. The book needs the courage to let them carry more weight.

# 3. Trade nonfiction acquisitions editor (Big 5)

**TO:** Editorial Board
**FROM:** [Senior Editor], Trade Nonfiction
**RE:** *Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work* — ~72,000 words, 12 chapters
**RECOMMENDATION:** ACQUIRE WITH REVISIONS (with a real possibility of PASS — see structural concerns)

## Book in one paragraph

A 72,000-word sweep history arguing that money is not a commodity but a relationship — credit recorded by institutions powerful enough to enforce it. The author marches from a 1801 BCE barley loan in Larsa through tally sticks, Datini's bills, the Stop of the Exchequer, the founding of the Bank of England, Newton's 1717 mint adjustment, the Bank Restriction, the Bank Charter Act of 1844, Churchill's 1925 return to gold, Bretton Woods, the Nixon Shock, 2008, COVID, and the 2021 inflation, ending with cryptocurrency, CBDCs, and a serious institutional engagement with the public-choice and fiscal-dominance critiques. The thesis is essentially the credit/chartalist tradition of Innes-Knapp-Goodhart-Wray-Graeber, translated for a general reader, but with unusual intellectual honesty about Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, and Hayek-Selgin-Ammous, all of whom get steelmanned at chapter length rather than swatted away.

## Comps

- **Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*** (Melville House, 2011). The closest thematic comp and the obvious shadow over this manuscript. Graeber sold ~250k+ in trade and became an academic-trade crossover hit. Ceiling: this book is more disciplined, less polemical, and more institutional than Graeber, which means it loses Graeber's anarchist edge and viral readership. Comp is honest but unflattering on platform.
- **Ahamed, *Lords of Finance*** (Penguin Press, 2009). Pulitzer winner, ~300k+. The structural model the author is *trying* to write to — Notes on Sources says so explicitly. Ceiling: Ahamed had four protagonists across one decade. This book has no sustained protagonists and tries to cover 5,000 years. Not a true comp on craft.
- **Lewis, *The Big Short*** (Norton, 2010). Wrong genre comp. Listed only because the brief asks. Lewis is character-driven reportage on one event; this is an institutional history. The author has no Steve Eisman.
- **Tooze, *Crashed*** (Viking, 2018). Closer in ambition and tone — institutional, transatlantic, willing to engage technicalities. Tooze did ~150k hardcover. Ceiling: Tooze had 2008 as a single dramatic spine and a built academic platform from *Wages of Destruction*. This book has neither.
- **Kelton, *The Deficit Myth*** (PublicAffairs, 2020). NYT bestseller, ~150k+. Carries the same MMT-adjacent ideas this book defends. Ceiling: Kelton had a campaign-cycle hook (Sanders advisor), a single accessible argument, and a 280-page format. This book is richer but four times as ambitious and harder to position as a partisan flag.
- **Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters***. Academic comp, not commercial — useful for proving the author has read the literature.

Honest read: the realistic comp neighborhood is **Graeber + Tooze**, with a Kelton-shaped political-discourse possibility if MMT becomes news again. That's a 50–150k commercial range with the right push, not a *Lords of Finance* ceiling.

## Target reader

The Tooze/Eichengreen reader who buys *Foreign Affairs*, listens to *Odd Lots*, and read Kelton without being convinced. Educated professionals 35–65, finance-curious but not finance-employed. Strong overlap with the *Debt* readership that has aged out of Graeber's politics. Secondary: undergraduate course adoption in econ history and political economy — this would teach *very* well, which matters for the long tail.

## Marketing hooks

- "Money is not a thing but a relationship." A genuinely good single-sentence thesis that survives an elevator pitch.
- The 1797–1821 Bank Restriction as a 24-year fiat-money natural experiment most readers have never heard of. Strong podcast hook.
- The Backwell/Stop-of-the-Exchequer chapter is genuinely the best piece of writing in the manuscript and reads like a Lewis vignette. Pull-quote bait.
- The 2021 inflation chapter is willing to grade MMT proponents as having missed the call. That's *news*. It positions the author as a credible non-partisan voice in a tribal debate. Op-ed, *FT* interview, *Ezra Klein Show*.
- CBDC framing in the Epilogue is timely without being faddish.

## Structural concerns

This is where the recommendation gets serious.

**1. No sustained protagonists.** This is the single biggest commercial risk and the brief flagged it correctly. Ahamed had Norman, Strong, Schacht, Moreau across 300 pages. Tooze had Bernanke and Geithner threaded through a decade. Lewis had four traders. *This book has roughly fifteen one-chapter cameos*: Amat-Sîn, Datini, Backwell, Paterson, Newton, Attwood, Heslop, Todd, Lebesque, Farmer A, the Hagedorns, the Klemperers, Kono, McKenzie, Kantor. Each is well-rendered. None recur. The reader never gets the *Lords of Finance* pleasure of watching a character age across chapters and pay for earlier choices. The vignette method is *competent* — better than most sweep-history attempts — but it caps the book's narrative ceiling. The author appears to know this; the planning docs in the recent commit history flag "structural threading." Whatever was attempted, it has not landed in the manuscript I read. **This is the revision.**

**2. The Mesopotamia-to-modern arc is the genre's structural curse.** Twelve chapters covering 5,000 years means ~6,000 words per chapter for events that deserve a book each. Chapters 1–3 feel obligatory; the book genuinely begins at Chapter 4 (Backwell) and runs strong through Chapter 12. A bolder edit would compress Part I into a single 8,000-word prologue and let the institutional revolution start earlier.

**3. Steelmanning has become a tic.** The intellectual honesty toward Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, and Hayek is admirable and is part of what could make this a *durable* book rather than a partisan one. But by Chapter 12 every section ends with a "the conservative tradition is partly right about something the framework cannot resolve" coda. That register — appropriate once or twice — becomes a verbal mannerism that drains forward momentum. Tighten by half.

**4. The Notes on Sources is excellent and signals an author who has done the work.** The recent commit history shows phase-4/phase-5 critique-and-revision passes. The manuscript reads like it. This is actually a positive on the acquisition side — the author is a reviser, not defensive.

**5. The MMT Appendix is a liability.** Putting it in the back doesn't insulate the book from the MMT framing the way the author hopes. Either own it in the body or cut the appendix and let Chapter 12 stand.

## Verdict: ACQUIRE WITH REVISIONS

I'd offer at the **mid-five-figures advance** range, contingent on a substantive revision pass that (a) threads two or three protagonists across chapter lines — Backwell echoed in 2008, Attwood echoed in 1925, Keynes from Versailles through Bretton Woods — (b) compresses Part I, (c) cuts the MMT appendix, and (d) tightens the steelmanning. With those revisions, this is a credible *Crashed*-tier book with course-adoption tail. Without them, it's a 30k-copy book that gets compared unfavorably to Graeber. The author's recent revision history makes me think the revisions are achievable; the structural protagonist problem is the real risk.

If editorial bandwidth is tight or the author resists the structural revision, **PASS** is defensible. The book on the page right now is a B+ that wants to be an A− and is one revision pass away from getting there.

**On bestseller-tier craft.** Setting aside platform: no, not yet — but closer than 90 percent of manuscripts in this category. The prose is consistently strong (the Backwell-at-the-Unicorn passages, the Klemperer hyperinflation set piece, and the Hagedorn farm bankruptcy genuinely match Ahamed sentence-for-sentence), the research is real, and the intellectual seriousness is bestseller-grade. What holds it back at the craft level is the same thing that holds back almost every sweep-of-history money book: the absence of recurring protagonists whose decisions in Chapter 4 determine their fate in Chapter 9. Ahamed, Lewis, and Tooze all wrote books in which the *reader's investment in specific humans* carries the analytic argument; Kelton wrote a single-thesis polemic short enough to bypass the problem; Graeber substituted an authorial persona for protagonists. This manuscript does none of these. Fix that — even partially, by threading three lives across the institutional revolution — and the platform/marketing question becomes: a serious *FT*/Ezra Klein/Odd Lots tour, a strong long-form excerpt placement (the Stop of the Exchequer chapter), and a contrarian-but-credible 2021-inflation positioning ahead of the next Fed cycle. That combination, on this prose, gets to bestseller territory. As written, it gets to respected-but-niche.

# 4. Conservative / libertarian / sound-money critic

**Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work**
A skeptical reading from the sound-money tradition.

There is a moment, roughly 60,000 words into this manuscript, when the author finally puts the book's thesis into a sentence one can argue with: "The binding constraint on what governments can spend in their own currency is not gold reserves or accumulated revenues; it is the economy's ability to produce goods and services." Strip the candy wrapper off the historical pageant — the Mesopotamian grain tablets, the Newton vignettes, the Lehman pension stories — and that is the argument. Productive capacity is the cap; institutions are the steering wheel; gold was never the foundation, it was a security blanket the British accidentally got attached to in 1717 because Isaac Newton miscalculated by 2.33 percent.

The reader from *Reason* or *Cato Journal* will recognize this immediately. It is Modern Monetary Theory dressed for a Knopf catalog, with an appendix politely confirming the family resemblance. To the manuscript's credit, that appendix is more candid than most MMT primers: it lists Kelton, Wray, Mitchell-Wray-Watts, and Mosler as further reading and explicitly defines productive capacity as the "more stringent" constraint replacing the "fake" one of budget balance. Whatever else this book is, it is not hiding the ball.

Where it goes wrong is in the framing it offers itself credit for. The author repeatedly claims to "steelman" the sound-money tradition. Hayek, Selgin, White, Ammous, Friedman, Buchanan, Sargent, Wallace, and Cochrane all appear by name. The Chapter 12 cryptocurrency section — which I read carefully because the project is mine to take seriously — opens by quoting *Denationalisation of Money*, gives Selgin's productivity norm a respectful paragraph, and even concedes that "the cryptocurrency tradition is partly correct to demand" the institutional answer the book proposes. So far, so generous.

Then comes the empirical adjudication, and the steelman becomes a strawman in slow motion. Bitcoin is dismissed on volatility grounds (true), stablecoins on the perfectly reasonable observation that they reintroduce dollar dependence (also true), El Salvador on adoption data (fair). But the comparison is rigged: fiat money is judged on its 2008 rescue and its 2020 mobilization, while cryptocurrency is judged on Mt. Gox, Terra/Luna, FTX, and Celsius — i.e., the failures of *centralized intermediaries* that the cypherpunk tradition explicitly warned against. The author concedes "the protocol works" and then proceeds as if that concession costs the argument nothing. It costs the argument a great deal. A fairer comparison would set Bitcoin's monetary base discipline against the Fed's $9 trillion balance sheet and ask which institution is more likely to be running the same playbook in 2050. The book never asks.

The Hayekian knowledge problem fares better. The chapter contains a genuinely honest passage acknowledging that the productive-capacity framework requires real-time sectoral judgment that Hayek would say no central planner can make. "The chapter does not have a clean answer to this objection," the author writes — a sentence that, in the trade-nonfiction context, is unusually adult. Credit where it's due. But the candor only sharpens the question: if you cannot read the constraint in real time, on what authority do you tell us the constraint *is* productive capacity? The fiscal council and inflation-trigger architecture proposed at the end of Chapter 12 is the same technocratic faith Hayek spent forty years dismantling, with new acronyms.

The gold-standard chapters (6 through 9) are where the book is at its most rhetorically aggressive and historiographically tendentious. The framing is consistent: every gold-standard episode is read through the deflation it produced and the workers it ground up. Newton's 1717 ratio is "an accident." Britain's 1821 resumption ruined Farmer Gigney. The 1925 return ruined Harry Todd. The 1928–32 sterilization ruined Farmer A in Iowa. These vignettes are well-written and, individually, defensible. Cumulatively they constitute a 250-page indictment with no opening statement for the defense. The classical gold standard's 1880–1914 record — perhaps the most stable monetary regime in modern history, the one Saifedean Ammous and Selgin actually defend — is dispatched in Chapter 6 as Britain extracting "from its colonies what its trade balances could not provide." That is a serviceable left-wing reading of imperial finance. It is not a refutation of the gold standard's price-level performance, capital-mobility record, or trade-financing function, none of which the chapter engages.

The Volcker chapter (11, with continuation in 12) deserves special mention because it is genuinely stronger than I expected. The author lets Volcker speak in his own institutional voice, names the South American counterfactual Bernanke wrote about in 2005, and acknowledges that the diffuse cost of accommodating inflation — passbook savers, fixed-pension retirees, wage-earners without indexation — was real and would have been borne by people the Hagedorn-farm vignette obscures. The line "Volcker's choice was not between cruelty and gentleness. It was between two patterns of cruelty" is the kind of sentence I expected this book to refuse to write. It wrote it anyway. That is the moment I stopped wanting to throw the manuscript across the room.

It does not, however, undo the book's structural problem: it relentlessly humanizes the cost of monetary discipline (Todd, Hagedorn, Gigney, the Iowa farmer, the Durham miner) while the cost of monetary indiscipline appears almost exclusively as anonymous statistics or distant foreign cases (Weimar's Klemperer is the exception, and his vignette is excellent — the Preuß family is good too). The American retiree in 1979 watching her Treasury bonds halve in real terms gets a paragraph; the Hagedorns get a chapter.

A final word on the productive-capacity framing. The author insists this is not MMT, but the appendix concedes the lineage and the policy implications are identical: the deficit isn't the problem, inflation is, and inflation is mostly a supply-side phenomenon until proven otherwise. In 2021 that frame was wrong by a third to a half, and the book — to its credit — admits this in Chapter 12, conceding that the early-2021 sanguine view was "a broad consensus across institutional and heterodox voices" and that the framework's heterodox proponents got the magnitude wrong. The concession is real. It is also the moment at which the framework's predictive content becomes hard to specify. If 2008 confirmed it and 2021 only half-confirmed it, what would refute it?

**Verdict: ★★★ (three stars).** A well-researched, well-written, intermittently honest brief for the institutionalist side of a debate it pretends has been settled. The Volcker chapter and the Currency School's distributional account belong on serious shelves; the cryptocurrency dismissal and the gold-standard caricature do not. Read it next to *The Bitcoin Standard* and *A Monetary History*, not instead of them.

**On bestseller-tier craft.** No, not quite. The prose is clean and the vignettes (Klemperer's coffee, the Preußes' salt herring, the Hagedorns' Iowa diary, Lehman's Renee Spero ten weeks pregnant) are genuinely competitive with Ahamed and Tooze at their best — better than Kelton, who can't write a vignette to save her life. What holds it back at the craft level is structural: the book argues a thesis where Ahamed *narrates a moment* and Lewis *follows characters*, and thesis-driven trade nonfiction at this length needs either a tighter through-line (Graeber's anthropological provocation) or a single defining set piece (Tooze's 2008) to anchor 72,000 words. *Money, Trust, and Power* has neither — it is a 12-chapter march through five millennia, each chapter making a variant of the same point, which produces the sensation of being repeatedly persuaded of something one already conceded by Chapter 4. To unlock NYT-list trade-nonfiction status it would need either a tighter human spine (pick three families across three regimes and braid them) or a more provocative framing (a single counterintuitive claim about the present) — and probably platform via a *Foreign Affairs* essay, a Tooze blurb, and a Kelton-vs-Cochrane debate tour. The craft is *almost* there. It isn't quite there yet.

# 5. Working economic historian

**An Ahamed-shaped Synthesis Built on a Heterodox Spine**

For a book that announces in its preface that it is not making a new argument — "It is not a recovery of suppressed knowledge; it is a translation of a settled scholarly tradition for the readers who deserve to have it" — *Money, Trust, and Power* arrives more candidly than most trade synthesis. The author takes the credit-theory/chartalist tradition that runs from Mitchell Innes and Knapp through Goodhart, Wray, Hudson, Ingham, and Graeber, and threads it across five thousand years and twelve chapters. The thesis: money is institutional credit, taxation is what gives a currency baseline demand, and productive capacity — not metal, not bond markets — is the binding constraint on what a sovereign issuer can spend in its own unit.

Within the genre this is competently executed. The prose is closer to Ahamed than to Tooze — narrative-led, character-driven, with set-piece scenes (Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn in January 1672, Keynes collapsing on the Mount Washington Hotel stairs, a Durham miner's family snaring sparrows during the 1926 lockout) doing the affective work that lets a reader carry an institutional argument across centuries. The decision to anchor each thesis in a named historical individual — Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn at Larsa, Datini in Bruges, Backwell, Paterson, Attwood, Harry Todd, Farmer Hagedorn, Klemperer, Michinao Kono — is the manuscript's signature craft move, and on the whole it works. These vignettes are sourced (the Larsa loan tablet to its CDLI catalogue number; the Klemperer diary entries to the published volumes; the Hagedorn diary to family records) and the manuscript flags the evidentiary status of each.

The factual baseline is mostly sound on the bits one expects a reviewer in this orbit to spot-check. The Newton 1717 mint adjustment narrative tracks the modern consensus (Feavearyear, Craig, Kleer); the chapter is appropriately careful about distinguishing Newton's intent from the de facto gold standard that emerged. The Bardi/Peruzzi treatment now flags the revisionist literature questioning whether Edward III's default was cause or trigger — a real improvement over the older trade-book reflex of treating the 1345 default as a single decisive event. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 is correctly read as a Currency School victory that misidentified the form money would take (the deposit explosion of the next century), and Goodhart's "fare-thee-well" line is in its proper place. The treatment of Henry Thornton — with proper credit for his anticipations of Wicksell, Bagehot, and modern lender-of-last-resort doctrine — is welcome; this is the kind of move that distinguishes the manuscript from its competitors. The 1672 Stop of the Exchequer arithmetic (£1,328,526; Backwell's £295,994 exposure; the 0.01% effective return calculation; the 1698 private Act winding up his estate) tracks the specialist literature.

A few specific complaints. The Wisselbank narrative still slightly overstates the bank's "decline from internal corruption" line — the late-eighteenth-century crisis is more entangled with VOC failure than the chapter's framing implies, though this is now a matter of degree. The Hamburg Godeffroy resignation in Chapter 6 is cleanly hedged ("the link cannot be made on the public record"); the manuscript shows discipline here that earlier drafts presumably lacked. The 1023 *jiaozi* nationalization figure and the 1204 reserve ratio are the standard von Glahn/Peng numbers — uncontroversial. The 1690 Massachusetts bills-of-credit episode is correctly identified as the first colonial fiat issuance (Goldberg's revisionist work would push back on some details, but the chapter's framing is defensible). The 2021 inflation chapter is the strongest in the manuscript on contested contemporary material: the author concedes that the early-2021 sanguine framing was a broad consensus that included MMT proponents alongside Krugman and Yellen, names the Blanchard/Summers warnings, cites Shapiro, Amiti et al., Cox/Del Negro/Giannoni, and Bems et al. for the post-hoc decompositions, and explicitly notes that "no framework called the episode cleanly." This is intellectually honest in a way most popular books on either side of the inflation debate were not.

Where the manuscript sits versus the comparators: it is more synthetic than original. Graeber's *Debt* did the deeper anthropological labor on the Mesopotamian credit tradition and the *amargi* historiography; this book leans on Hudson and flags the Van De Mieroop counter-reading without resolving it. Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* did the gold-standard interwar analysis with quantitative depth this manuscript does not attempt. Mehrling's *New Lombard Street* did the institutional analysis of post-2008 central banking that Chapter 12 only gestures at. Ahamed's *Lords of Finance* did the central-banker-as-character work this manuscript imitates. Tooze's *Crashed* did the post-2008 global political economy with archival depth this manuscript does not have. Goetzmann's *Money Changes Everything* did the comparative civilizational sweep more rigorously. Ferguson's *Ascent of Money*, the closest direct comparator, was glossier and worse on the chartalist material; this manuscript is better-argued than Ferguson on the Bank of England, on 1925, and on the post-1971 era.

The Notes on Sources back-matter, treated as scholarly apparatus, is serviceable rather than serious. The Ahamed model Notes on Sources runs to sixty or seventy pages of dense, discursive engagement; this manuscript's runs perhaps fifteen to twenty pages and covers seven chapters out of twelve. Chapters 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 11 are unrepresented — striking omissions given that Chapter 4 (Backwell, Stop of the Exchequer) and Chapter 9 (Churchill, gold sinks, Roosevelt) carry some of the manuscript's most specific factual claims. The notes that exist are competent: the Steil-vs-Helleiner choice in Chapter 10 is named honestly, the Hudson-vs-Van De Mieroop tension is acknowledged, the Briggs/Miller/Moss historiography on Attwood is properly cited. But this is closer to a "Notes on Selected Sources" than to genuine apparatus. A working historian would want footnoted page references; a serious general reader would still want the Backwell, Bretton Woods, and 1925 material sourced. As scholarly apparatus it does not function. As Ahamed-style courtesy to the reader it works.

The novelty question. The manuscript's positive contribution is not in the chartalist argument itself — Wray, Kelton, Tcherneva, and Tymoigne have made this case at greater technical depth — but in three integrative moves. First, the deliberate engagement with the strongest opposing positions (Mises's regression theorem, Palley's metallist critique, Buchanan-Wagner public choice, Sargent-Wallace fiscal dominance, Cochrane's FTPL, Hayek's knowledge problem, the Hayek-Selgin-White cryptocurrency case via Ammous). The Sargent-Wallace section in Chapter 12 is unusually honest for a heterodox-friendly trade book: it concedes that the productive-capacity framework does not by itself answer the nominal-anchor question. Second, the integration of distributional analysis (Hamburg 1873, the 1821 deflation, the 1925 return to gold, Volcker's Hagedorns, post-2008 QE) into a single thread. Third, the post-2021 honest accounting of where MMT-adjacent voices got the inflation call wrong. None of these is original scholarship; together they constitute a more intellectually serious synthesis than the field's previous trade entries.

**Verdict: COMPETENT SYNTHESIS.**

The book translates a settled scholarly tradition with discipline, fair engagement of opposing positions, and craft that occasionally rises to *Lords of Finance* level. It does not advance the historiography. It is the trade-nonfiction book Wray and Kelton should have wanted written about the chartalist tradition.

**On bestseller-tier craft.** Yes, the manuscript is at the craft level of a bestseller in this category, though closer to Ahamed and Kelton than to Lewis or Tooze. The set-piece scenes — Backwell, the Mount Washington Hotel, Klemperer's mark, Harry Todd's seventeen months, the Hagedorn farm — land at *Lords of Finance* register; the Datini bill of exchange and the Larsa tablet vignettes are the kind of opening hooks that move books in airport bookstores. What holds it back is structural: twelve chapters and 72,000 words is a hard sell against Lewis's narrative tightness, and the manuscript occasionally lectures (the appendix on MMT principles reads as authorial reassurance rather than argumentation). The Buchanan-Wagner and Sargent-Wallace concessions, though intellectually honorable, blunt the polemical edge that drove *Deficit Myth* up the lists. Platform-wise this needs Tooze-level public-intellectual amplification, a *New Yorker* or *NYRB* essay-extract on the 1925/2015 Greece parallel, and a Ezra Klein/Tyler Cowen podcast circuit; with that, it can hit the lists. Without it, it lands as a well-reviewed mid-list. The ceiling is real; the launch architecture would have to do the rest.

# Synthesis

## Part A — Cross-persona synthesis

### Where the personas converge

**1. Chapter 12's analytical revisions landed.** The single clearest signal across this round is that the chapter the author worked hardest on is the chapter the hostile readers most respected. The skeptical economist calls the Sargent-Wallace/FTPL section "above its trade-press category" and singles out the explicit admission that "they are not the same claim, and it is a category error to treat them as one." The conservative critic calls the Volcker treatment — *the* place a sound-money reader expects to be insulted — "the moment I stopped wanting to throw the manuscript across the room." The NYT reviewer calls Chapter 12 "intellectual honesty of the kind Tooze brings to *Crashed*." The historian calls the 2021 inflation handling "intellectually honest in a way most popular books on either side of the inflation debate were not." Four of five personas — including the two most hostile — independently flagged Chapter 12 as the manuscript's most honest section. That is movement.

**2. The set-piece scenes remain the manuscript's craft achievement.** Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn is named as a peak by all five personas — the NYT reviewer calls it "one of the finest pieces of monetary-historical narrative I have read in a decade," the historian places its arithmetic against the specialist literature, the editor calls it "the best piece of writing in the manuscript and reads like a Lewis vignette." The Klemperer hyperinflation passage, the Hagedorn Iowa farm bankruptcy, Harry Todd at Boldon Colliery, Eva Klemperer's coffee, Aunt Berta's salt herring, Kono's ramen restaurant — these scenes are doing the affective work the rest of the book leans on. The phase-3 deepening of Backwell post-1672 visibly registered. None of the five personas faulted a specific set piece on craft grounds.

**3. The protagonist/threading problem is unchanged.** The editor states it most directly — "this book has roughly fifteen one-chapter cameos. Each is well-rendered. None recur" — but the NYT reviewer, the economist, and the conservative critic all reach the same diagnosis from different angles. The economist: "stranded inside chapters that toggle between humanist set-piece and graduate-seminar exposition." The conservative: "a 12-chapter march through five millennia, each chapter making a variant of the same point." The NYT: "the book sells a thesis in Chapter 1. It half-unsells the same thesis in Chapter 12." All four diagnose the same underlying issue: a thesis-illustrated-by-material structure, where the comps run on character-investment-generates-thesis. The phase-6 structural threading work flagged in the recent commit history has not yet landed in a way these readers can detect.

**4. The MMT appendix is now the manuscript's weakest piece.** Three personas (economist, conservative, editor) flag it independently. The economist: "the part of the book where the steelmanning discipline visibly slips." The editor: "a liability... cut the appendix and let Chapter 12 stand." The conservative: it "concedes the lineage and the policy implications are identical [to MMT]." This is the cleanest fix on the table — every reviewer who reads it asks for the same change. Whatever revisions Chapter 12 received did not propagate to the appendix.

**5. Chapter 8 (Currency School) is partially improved but still rhetorically tilted.** The economist reads the explicit steelman at line 91 as "competent" but flags that "the surrounding rhetoric closes what the explicit concession claims to leave open" — Attwood-as-suppressed-truth-teller plus the institutional-capture material (Overstone's £2 million, Norman family continuity, Political Economy Club gatekeeping) overdetermines the verdict. The conservative reaches the same conclusion via the gold-standard chapters more broadly: "a 250-page indictment with no opening statement for the defense." This is unchanged from v1. The steelman language is in the chapter; the chapter's narrative architecture still reads as a prosecution.

### Where the personas diverge

**On novelty.** The historian rates the manuscript "more synthetic than original" with three integrative moves of real merit. The editor treats it as a translation of a settled tradition for general readers — useful, durable, course-adoptable. The conservative reads it as MMT in nicer clothes. The NYT reviewer reads it as a serious contested argument with documentary specificity. The economist reads it as a partly-fair partly-tilted brief that reaches the front rank in three sections and falls short in three others. These are different readings of the same book, and the divergence is itself a finding: the author has produced a book the chartalist tradition can claim, the orthodox tradition can grudgingly engage, and the sound-money tradition can argue with — without any single tradition being able to dismiss it. That is itself the achievement of the steelman strategy, even where the steelmen are imperfect.

**On the historian's apparatus.** Only the historian reviews the Notes on Sources at length, and the rating — "serviceable rather than serious" — is the most specific actionable critique in this round. Chapters 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 11 are unrepresented. Chapter 4 carries the Backwell material, Chapter 9 carries the Churchill/1925 material, Chapter 11 carries the Volcker arithmetic. These are exactly the chapters where the most specific factual claims live, and the most specific factual claims are where a specialist endorser will press. The v1 critique flagged superscripts-without-footnotes as a non-starter; that has been resolved (phase-5 superscript stripping). What replaced it is partial coverage. The fix is mechanical: extend the Notes on Sources to cover the missing chapters.

**On length and Chapter 12 overload.** The editor still wants Part I compressed and the manuscript expanded into deeper character portraits at 90k. The NYT reviewer wants 20% cut. The historian implicitly wants the Notes on Sources doubled. This divergence is a structural decision the author has to make, and v1 flagged the same trilemma.

**On the Volcker chapter.** Universally upgraded. The economist owes "an unqualified compliment." The conservative names it as the breakthrough. The NYT reads it as part of Chapter 12's general intellectual honesty. The phase-4 register-consistency pass and the post-1672 Backwell deepening (commits 60, 62) appear to have done their work; if there is a parallel "phase-N Volcker register pass" in the commit history, it is paying off.

### Highest-priority remaining issues

In order of severity:

1. **MMT appendix.** Either substantively revise to match Chapter 12's negotiated position (drop the descriptive-not-prescriptive framing, engage Sargent-Wallace inside the appendix, name where 2021 hurt the framework) or cut. Three personas flag this. (Economist, conservative, editor.)
2. **Notes on Sources gaps.** Extend coverage to Chapters 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11 — particularly 4, 9, and 11 where the most specific factual claims live. (Historian.)
3. **Chapter 8 narrative architecture.** The explicit steelman at line 91 is fine; the surrounding rhetoric closes the question the steelman leaves open. Either restructure the chapter so the institutional-capture material follows rather than precedes the analytical engagement, or cut the institutional-capture material and let Overstone-Peel-Goodhart's-Law be argued on the merits. (Economist, conservative.)
4. **Structural threading.** The single biggest commercial risk. Pick two or three figures (Keynes from Versailles through Bretton Woods is named twice; Backwell-echoed-in-2008; Attwood-echoed-in-1925) and braid them across chapters as recurring rather than one-chapter presences. The phase-6 plan exists in the repo; it has not yet landed in the manuscript. (Editor, NYT, economist.)
5. **Cryptocurrency-section asymmetry.** The conservative's complaint is specific and fair: fiat is judged on its institutional successes, crypto on the failures of centralized intermediaries. A fairer comparison would set Bitcoin's monetary-base discipline against the Fed's $9T balance sheet across the same window. (Conservative.)
6. **Modern-parallel tic.** Several chapter-end "this is exactly how X works today" passages still pull the prose into op-ed register. (NYT, editor.)
7. **Chapter 12 framing on 2021.** The economist's specific point: the chapter concedes the orthodox directional call, then absorbs the win into a productive-capacity narrative via a "fixed capacity" reframing Blanchard-Summers did not actually rely on. The cited NY Fed report attributes two-thirds of inflation to demand shocks; the chapter's closing register obscures that number. Single-paragraph fix. (Economist.)

## Did the revisions move the manuscript meaningfully?

Yes, but not across verdict boundaries. The clearest movement is in tone: the two most hostile readers (skeptical economist, conservative critic) both wrote v2 reviews that contain genuine compliments where v1 contained none, and the specific compliments cluster on the work that was specifically revised — Sargent-Wallace/FTPL, the Volcker counterfactual, the 2021 inflation honest accounting, the Hayek knowledge-problem concession. The Backwell-post-1672 deepening (phase 3) and the register-consistency pass (phase 4) and the superscript stripping (phase 5) all visibly registered with the personas equipped to notice them. None of these moves was wasted. But none of them was sufficient to move a verdict, because the items that did not move are also the items that gate the verdicts: the structural protagonist problem (gates the editor's ACQUIRE), the MMT appendix (gates the economist's PARTIAL becoming SERIOUS), the Chapter 8 narrative architecture (also gates the economist), the Notes on Sources gaps (gates the historian's COMPETENT SYNTHESIS becoming ORIGINAL CONTRIBUTION — which it would not reach in any case, since it is by design synthesis), and the gold-standard chapters' rhetorical balance (gates the conservative's three stars becoming four).

The highest-leverage remaining moves to reach must-read tier are clear from the convergence: (1) substantive revision or removal of the MMT appendix, (2) extension of the Notes on Sources to cover the six missing chapters, (3) restructuring of Chapter 8 so the institutional-capture material does not pre-empt the analytical engagement, and (4) execution of the phase-6 structural threading plan to give the manuscript at least two or three sustained protagonists across chapters. Items 1 and 2 are mechanical and high-leverage; item 3 is a chapter-level revision; item 4 is the largest piece of work and the one with the highest commercial payoff. Three out of five reviewers explicitly note that the author has demonstrated, in the Volcker section especially, the capacity to do this kind of work when he chooses to. The revisions of the first cycle moved tone significantly and verdicts not at all. A second cycle that addresses items 1, 2, and 3 would likely move the economist to SERIOUS and the historian's specific factual reservations off the table; the editor's verdict will not move without item 4.
