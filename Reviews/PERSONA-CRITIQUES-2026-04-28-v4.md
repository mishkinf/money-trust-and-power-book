## Persona 1 — Trade Nonfiction Acquisitions Editor

### Long-form review (Acquisitions Memo)

**1. Book in one paragraph.** *Money, Trust, and Power* traces five thousand years of monetary history to argue a single thesis: money is not a commodity but a recorded relationship of obligation, sustained by institutions powerful enough to enforce it; taxation creates currency demand; and productive capacity, not gold or accounting balances, is the binding constraint on what sovereign issuers can spend. The book moves from a Larsa barley loan in 1801 BCE through Datini's Bruges-Barcelona bills, the Stop of the Exchequer, the founding of the Bank of England, Newton's miscalculation, the Bank Restriction, the Currency School's 1844 victory, Churchill's 1925 return to gold, Bretton Woods, Camp David, Volcker's recession, and the 2008/2020/2021 fiat-era tests. It steelmans Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, and Hayek-Selgin-Ammous on their own terms, then offers an institutionalist rebuttal grounded in the historical record.

**2. Target reader.** Educated trade-nonfiction readers who buy Ahamed, Tooze, Lewis, Kelton, and Graeber. Specifically: the *Lords of Finance / Crashed* reader who wants the long arc rather than a single crisis; the *Deficit Myth* reader who wants the historical scaffolding MMT typically asserts rather than argues; the policy-curious professional who has absorbed the 2008–2021 monetary debates and wants a synthesis. Secondary: graduate seminars in political economy and monetary history, where its steelman discipline makes it usable as a teaching text.

**3. Marketing hooks.** (a) "Five thousand years on one thesis": the Uruk-to-keystroke arc is durable cocktail-conversation material and reviewable as ambition. (b) The 2021 inflation chapter, which honestly concedes that no framework — including the author's — called the episode cleanly, lands as intellectually credible in a market saturated with triumphalism. (c) The human-scale set pieces (Backwell at the Unicorn, Harry Todd's seventeen months, the Hagedorn farm, Berta's salt herring, Michinao Kono's 605:1 job application) supply the *Lords of Finance* texture buyers expect. (d) The Hayek-Selgin-Ammous engagement gives the book a cryptocurrency angle that is neither dismissive nor credulous — rare and salable. (e) Direct collision with the household-budget metaphor that runs national fiscal discourse.

**4. Structural concerns.** First, *the steelman pattern becomes a tic*: every chapter pauses for a respectful concession to the opposing tradition, and by Chapter 12 the rhythm — claim, steelman, qualified return — risks reading as throat-clearing rather than argument. Second, *Chapter 8 carries too much architecture*: the Currency School institutional-capture material (Overstone's £2 million, Norman's 51 years, the Political Economy Club gatekeeping) is essential to the argument but presented as a catalogue rather than as scenes. The Heslop section and the Cantwell vignette feel parachuted in. Third, *the contemporary parallels at chapter ends are a known-good device used too systematically* — Greece-2010, 2020 Fed, China-2025, eurozone — and several are doing the same rhetorical work twice.

**5. Editorial conditions of acquisition.** (i) *Chapter 8 reorganization*: cut roughly 15–20 percent of the Currency School institutional-capture catalogue and route the surviving material through Attwood's perspective and the parliamentary debate. (ii) *Steelman discipline*: identify the three or four steelmans the argument genuinely requires (Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Hayek's knowledge problem, the Currency School's vindication-on-its-own-terms section), and let the others go. (iii) *Chapter 12 tightening*: split the chapter — one chapter on 2008 validation + 2021 reckoning + Hayek-Selgin-Ammous; one on the Volcker counterfactual + public-choice / fiscal-dominance trilogy. The current chapter is the longest in the book and the seam shows.

**6. Recommendation. Acquire with revisions.** The thesis is genuine, the historical sweep is real and sourced, the human-scale writing in the strongest set pieces is at the level the comp titles demand, and the book takes its opponents seriously enough that it can be reviewed by them without a fight. The three conditions above are real but addressable in one revision pass.

## Persona 2 — Working Economic Historian

### Long-form review

**A Cathedral of Trust, Honestly Built**

It is a fashionable move in trade nonfiction about money to declare that everything you think you know is wrong. Mishkin Faustini's *Money, Trust, and Power* resists that posture even as it stakes out a thesis — money is institutional credit, taxation drives currency demand, productive capacity is the binding constraint — that places the book firmly in the credit-theory tradition running from Mitchell Innes through Knapp, Hudson, Goodhart, Wray, Ingham, and Graeber. What distinguishes the book from its more polemical cousins is its refusal to dunk. The Currency School worry, the Friedman-Schwartz lesson, the Buchanan-Wagner public-choice critique, Sargent-Wallace's fiscal-dominance arithmetic, Cochrane's fiscal theory of the price level, and Hayek's knowledge problem are all named, steelmanned, and engaged on their own terms. By the time the book closes, in an Epilogue that explicitly itemizes the open questions the framework has not closed, the reader has been treated as an adult.

The structural spine is conventional and effective. Part I traces credit money from a 1801 BCE Larsa barley loan through Datini's 1399 Bruges-to-Barcelona bill of exchange to Charles II's 1672 Stop of the Exchequer — where Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn becomes the human face of unbacked private credit's failure mode. Part II builds the Bank of England as the institutional solution that goldsmith banking required and could not provide; Paterson's "money out of nothing" is set, with appropriate historiographic caution about the attribution, against his own ruin in the Darien venture, where the institutions did not run on his audacity. Part III takes the gold standard seriously: Newton's 1717 ratio adjustment is correctly framed as under-correction rather than founding act, the classical era's price stability and capital-mobility achievements are credited rather than dismissed, and the post-1815 deflation is documented through specific Devon and Essex farmers in the *London Gazette* insolvency lists. The Bank Restriction (1797–1821) is the book's central empirical claim: twenty-four years of fiat sterling, financing the Napoleonic Wars, working in a Norfolk rector's daily accounts. The chapter that follows — "The Great Forgetting" — is the book's strongest piece of historiographic argument, tracing how Lord Overstone's network and three generations of Norman directors at the Bank of England embedded a doctrinal architecture that outlived the metal it was built around.

Part IV is where the trade-nonfiction craft has to carry the weight, and largely does. The Todd family's seventeen-month lockout in Boldon Colliery, with hessian-bag shoes and snared birds, is the human cost of Churchill's 1925 parity decision; "Farmer A" of Story County, Iowa, taken from William Murray's 1933 study, gives the gold-standard deflation an Iowa courthouse record. The Klemperer and Preuß families' Weimar inflation, including Aunt Berta's salt herring, demonstrate the symmetric truth that fiat money fails when the state behind it fails. The Hagedorn farm in 1980s Iowa documents what Volcker's disinflation cost. The book's most morally serious passage may be the section in Chapter 12 that constructs the Argentina counterfactual: Volcker chose between two patterns of cruelty, one acute and concentrated, one chronic and diffuse, and the latter would have fallen on retirees and wage-earners without indexed pensions whose suffering would never appear on the same chart as the Hagedorn farm's foreclosure.

The book's spot-checkable claims hold up against the standard literature. The Pepys-Backwell pre-1672 material runs through D.M. Mitchell's *Silver Society Journal* article and Quinn's dissertation, both correctly cited. The Briggs-Miller-Moss historiography on Attwood is handled with rare honesty: the chapter neither inflates Attwood into a proto-Keynesian nor dismisses him, instead landing on Briggs's actual position — directionally vindicated, analytically pre-Keynes. Volcker's *Keeping At It* and Silber's *Volcker* are the load-bearing sources for Camp David and the 1979–82 disinflation, and the chapter's reading — that Volcker preferred the coordinated multilateral devaluation Connally overrode — matches both. The Bretton Woods narrative follows Steil over Helleiner; the Mesopotamian credit-theory framing follows Hudson over Van De Mieroop. Both choices are flagged in the Notes on Sources.

The prose carries what trade nonfiction in this category requires. The opening of Chapter 4 ("A cold rain fell on London…") and the Camp David weekend in Chapter 11 read with the cinematic competence of Ahamed; the historiographic wrap-up at the end of Chapter 8 reads with the analytic compression of Tooze. There are sentences that would not survive a copyedit — the metaphor of "welding shut one valve while leaving five other valves wide open" in Chapter 8 does too much work — but the voice is consistent across chapters and the structural transitions are earned. The 2008 financial crisis and its 2021 successor are treated with the empirical precision the moment demands: the New York Fed's Amiti-Heise-Karahan-Şahin model, Shapiro's sectoral PCE decomposition, and Cox-Del Negro-Giannoni are all named. The MMT-adjacent sanguine consensus of early 2021 is conceded to have been wrong, and Krugman's and Yellen's retractions are quoted. This is not a partisan book pretending to be balanced. It is a serious book.

The Notes on Sources, written in the discursive Ahamed-Tooze style rather than as numbered footnotes, tells the reader where the author has chosen one defensible interpretation over another. That is the right move for trade nonfiction at this register; it is also a quiet act of scholarly courage.

Where the book is open to challenge: the productive-capacity framework, defended throughout, is honest about not yet having a fully worked-out institutional architecture sufficient to answer the public-choice critique. The Epilogue says this. The chapter on the age of pure fiat says this. A reader sympathetic to Buchanan-Wagner will leave the book wishing the institutional architecture in Chapter 12 — symmetric fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils, pre-legislated tax adjustments — were sketched in more concrete detail. A reader sympathetic to Hayek's knowledge problem will note that the chapter concedes the framework cannot fully resolve the objection. These are not failures of the book; they are the book's own catalogued open questions, and the author's willingness to mark them is part of what makes the work trustworthy.

**Native verdict: original contribution.** The synthesis is competent, but the institutional-political reading of the 1820–1850 Currency School triumph, the sustained engagement with Buchanan-Wagner and Sargent-Wallace inside a credit-theory framework, and the calibrated Volcker counterfactual go beyond what *Debt*, *Crashed*, *Lords of Finance*, or *The Deficit Myth* attempted. It belongs on the shelf next to them.

The set-piece scene work (Backwell at the Unicorn, the 11 Downing Street dinner, Camp David, Aunt Berta's salt herring, the Hagedorn farm) is at Ahamed's level. The analytic synthesis is at Tooze's level on Bretton Woods and the post-2008 response. What holds the manuscript back from bestseller-tier craft is a register problem in Chapter 12: the analytic blocks (Sargent-Wallace, the fiscal theory, Hayek's knowledge problem, the cryptocurrency engagement) crowd the human-cost vignettes that carried the earlier chapters. Lewis would have moved the cryptocurrency material into a dedicated short chapter; Ahamed would have given each public-choice critique a single human stake; Tooze would have leaned harder on the 2021–22 inflation as a single multi-character scene. Then platform and marketing — comp-title placement against *Lords of Finance* and *Crashed*, an op-ed lane in the *Atlantic* or *FT*, a finance-podcast circuit — would be the unlock.

## Persona 3 — Sympathetic Heterodox / Chartalist Reviewer

### Long-form review

**Money's Long Memory: Notes on a Translation**

Every generation of credit-money writers stands in a peculiar relation to the public conversation we keep failing to enter. We know — Innes settled it in 1913, Knapp had settled it in 1905, the Mesopotamia people settled it in the 1990s — that money is a relationship recorded by institutions, that taxation drives currency demand, that the binding constraint on a sovereign issuer's spending is real productive capacity rather than the metal in any vault. We know, too, that when an inflation debate or a deficit panic erupts in the *Wall Street Journal*, none of this knowledge will be in the room. The household-budget metaphor will run unchallenged. Someone will claim that Greece could just print money. Someone else will earnestly explain why hyperinflation is around the corner. The discipline assembled by Hudson, Wray, Tcherneva, Mehrling, Goodhart, Ingham, and Graeber sits a foot away from a discourse it cannot reach.

Trade nonfiction is the bridge that has occasionally been built across that gap. Graeber built one. Kelton built one. Ahamed built one in a different idiom. The book under review attempts another, and it does so with discipline and, more strikingly, with intellectual honesty toward the traditions it has to argue against. *Money, Trust, and Power* opens in a Mesopotamian temple, runs through Bruges and Lombard Street and the founding of the Bank of England, traverses Newton's accidental gold standard and Britain's twenty-four-year experiment in fiat money during the Napoleonic Wars, follows the Currency School to its 1844 victory and the human cost of that victory through Birmingham, Boldon Colliery, Story County Iowa, and a métro entrance in Paris in the winter of 1932–33, and lands in a sober, well-instrumented account of the 2008 and 2020 monetary mobilizations and the 2021 inflation. The argumentative spine is faithful to the chartalist lineage. The faithfulness is real, and the book deserves to be read on that basis first.

What is more interesting, and what makes the book distinct from the existing trade-credit-money library, is the architecture of concession. The Preface promises, and the chapters deliver, serious engagement with the steelmen — Currency School, Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, Hayek-Selgin-Ammous. Chapter 8 stops the Birmingham narrative dead halfway through to ask, in Peel's and Overstone's voices, "Was the worry justified?" — and answers honestly that the long historical record vindicates the central Currency School concern that an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master will, eventually, over-issue. Weimar, Hungary 1945–46, Argentina, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Turkey: the book names them. Chapter 12 takes Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane on directly, concedes that the productive-capacity framework alone does not specify the fiscal-monetary credibility conditions under which capacity becomes the binding constraint, and admits that Hayek's knowledge-problem critique is "partly right about something the framework cannot resolve by design." Chapter 12's section on Volcker walks through both halves of the disinflation ledger — the Hagedorn family's farm and the diffuse cost the alternative would have imposed on passbook savers and fixed-pension retirees — without absolving either pattern of cruelty. This is rare in the popular credit-money literature. It is also, I should say, a more truthful representation of the chartalist position than the cheerleading versions that have circulated since 2020. Steelmanning the opposition is what credit-money writing should have been doing for thirty years and largely has not.

The narrative craft is uneven but at its best very strong. The Datini scene that opens Chapter 2, the dinner at 11 Downing Street and Harry Todd's seventeen months at Boldon Colliery in Chapter 9, the Camp David weekend in Chapter 11, and the Klemperer / Preuß Mittelstand vignettes in Chapter 12's Weimar section achieve the kind of human-scale anchoring that *Lords of Finance* gets credit for. Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn carries the 1672 Stop with real weight, and his unanswered question — what an institution capable of carrying a sovereign's bad year would look like — is paid off properly when Paterson and Godfrey deliver the Bank's charter in Chapter 5. Volcker's career arc, threaded across Chapters 10 and 11 from his 1949 Princeton thesis through his Treasury years to Camp David and finally to the 1979 chair, is a structural achievement.

On the home dimensions — faithfulness to the chartalist source tradition and policy clarity — the book is strong but not maximal. The taxation-drives-demand mechanism is laid out cleanly in Chapter 1 and properly recapitulated through Chapter 5, Chapter 7 (the Pitt income tax as the closed loop sustaining inconvertible Bank notes), and Chapter 8 (the colonial hut taxes as the empirical case the metallists denied). The productive-capacity framing is consistent throughout. The book's clearest policy statement — that a credible fiscal-flexibility regime needs symmetric fiscal rules, an independent fiscal council, and pre-legislated automatic stabilizers — appears in the right place in Chapter 12, but it is a paragraph rather than a chapter, and a reader looking for the operational architecture the framework demands will have to assemble it from scattered references. The book has chosen — and I think this is a deliberate authorial decision rather than an oversight — to treat the chartalism as the older Innes-Knapp-Hudson-Goodhart line, not the post-2010 American MMT brand, and the choice is defensible — even bracing — but it means the reader who comes to the book expecting *The Deficit Myth* in long form will find something more catholic and somewhat more cautious.

The book's largest unresolved tension is between the heterodox argumentative spine and the genuinely sympathetic concessionary architecture. Chapter 8's "Was the worry justified?" section concedes — correctly, in my view — that the Currency School's central concern about discretionary over-issuance has been vindicated by the post-1971 record of South American and other inflations. Chapter 12 concedes that the 2021 inflation was a layered episode no framework, including the productive-capacity framework, called cleanly. The book's institutional-credibility answer to those concessions is that fiscal rules, fiscal councils, and credible independent central banking can substitute for the metal-anchor discipline gold provided. That answer is correct as far as it goes. It is also thinner than the concessions deserve. A reader who takes the steelmen seriously — and the book has worked hard to make them serious — may finish Chapter 12 thinking the framework's institutional architecture is underspecified relative to the strength of the worries the framework has acknowledged.

This is a real critique, and I make it as someone whose home tradition the book represents. It is not a fatal critique. The book that fully specifies the post-fiat institutional architecture credible enough to satisfy Buchanan-Wagner and Sargent-Wallace simultaneously has not been written by anyone, in any tradition. *Money, Trust, and Power* is honest about that. It also gets more right, and represents the chartalist credit-money tradition more faithfully and more generously, than any trade nonfiction title since *Debt*.

**Native verdict: faithful translation.** The book is a faithful translation of the chartalist credit-money tradition for trade readers, with concessionary architecture more honest than the existing popular literature has offered and historical narrative craft strong enough to carry it.

**Endorsement:** Yes — I would publicly endorse this book; I would do so with the note that it represents the tradition more honestly than the cheerleading texts and is the book I would now recommend first to a non-specialist reader who wants to understand why the household-budget framing is wrong.

## Persona 4 — Target Lay Reader (Goodreads)

### Long-form review

**★★★★ (4 stars)**

I picked this up because Odd Lots had me in a monetary-history mood and because *Debt* is one of the few "big idea" books I've read twice. I finished it. That's not nothing — I bounced off *Lords of Finance* twice in the German chapters, and this book asks you to sit through Mesopotamian temple accounting, a Newton mint report, and "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic" before you reach anything resembling our own century. It earned the read. I have real reservations, but it earned it.

The thesis is simple enough to fit on the back cover: money is a relationship enforced by institutions, not a thing made of metal. Five thousand years of evidence say so. The book sets that up in the Preface honestly — including, refreshingly, a list of the people who'd disagree (Friedman, Buchanan-Wagner, the Currency School, Cochrane) and a promise to take them seriously. That promise is mostly kept. There's a version of this book that's a triumphalist MMT pamphlet, and this isn't it. The chapters that engage Friedman, Selgin, Ammous, and Sargent-Wallace are some of the strongest in the manuscript, and the willingness to write the sentence "the worry was real then. It is real now" about the Currency School's case is the single thing that made me trust the writer.

Where the book sings: **set pieces**. Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn, ruined by Charles II's 1672 Stop of the Exchequer, is genuinely Lewis-grade. I knew nothing about this man or this episode and now I'll remember both. Harry Todd's seventeen-month lockout in Boldon, Durham, after Churchill's 1925 return to gold — the family making shoes from coal sacks, the baby born in the middle of the strike, the Keynes pamphlet predicting all of it from Cambridge — is the chapter I'd give to a friend to convince them this isn't dry. The Klemperer hyperinflation diary, Aunt Berta's salt herring, Morvan Lebesque on the Métro benches in '32-'33, the Hagedorn farm in Iowa during Volcker's recession: these scenes carry the argument better than the argument carries itself.

Where it leaned out: **chapter 1**. The Mesopotamian opening is the part I respected more than enjoyed. The Larsa loan tablet is fine; the digression into Mises's Regression Theorem and Menger and "the metallist challenge" feels like the writer warding off graduate-school critique I didn't know I was supposed to be having. *Debt* covered the same ground in 50 fewer pages and made me feel like I'd been let in on a secret rather than caught up on a literature. Chapter 8 ("The Great Forgetting") does brilliant work on Attwood and Birmingham but spends too long re-litigating whether he was a "proto-Keynesian" — historiographic care I appreciate but don't need three passes through. Chapter 12 is overstuffed: 2008, 2021, Japan, Weimar, Volcker, Hayek, Bitcoin, Sargent-Wallace, fiscal theory of the price level, Argentina-as-counterfactual. I get why each is there; together they break the chapter's spine. By the time Buchanan-Wagner shows up, I was checking how many pages were left.

Compared to **Tooze's *Crashed***, this book is doing something braver and harder: stitching Sumer to Volcker to Bitcoin into one argument. Tooze had the advantage of writing about events most readers half-remembered. The price the writer pays for the bigger ambit is that the connective tissue — "the same mechanism appears in..." paragraphs between centuries — sometimes feels like a claim being asserted rather than earned. Tooze never has to assert that the 2008 crisis matters; this book has to keep telling me that medieval tally sticks anticipate modern central banking, and the eighth time I'm told it I start to resist.

The Notes on Sources are genuinely good. Discursive, honest about contested interpretations, willing to say "the chapter follows Steil over Helleiner because..." That's serious work. I wish more pop-history did this.

Would I recommend it? **Yes, conditionally.** If you finished *Debt* and *Crashed* and wished someone had explained where the institutions actually came from — yes, this is your book. If you've never thought about money beyond "the Fed prints it," I'd start you with *Debt* and let you graduate to this. The book asks for stamina the writing mostly earns. If it cut a hundred pages — most of them in chapters 1, 8, and 12 — it would be a five-star book. As is, it's a strong four. The set pieces are why.
