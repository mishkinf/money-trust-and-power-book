# 1. Acquisitions Editor (Big 5 Trade Nonfiction)

## Internal Acquisitions Memo

**Title:** *Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work*
**Editor:** [Trade Nonfiction, Big 5]
**Status:** First read of full manuscript

**Book in one paragraph.** A 5,000-year synoptic history arguing that money is not a commodity but institutional credit — a record of obligation enforced by powers strong enough to make the record stick — with taxation as the foundational mechanism that gives currencies their value and productive capacity (not gold, not accounting balances) as the binding real constraint on what governments can spend. The book runs from Mesopotamian temple tablets and the Datini archive through the Bank of England's 1694 founding, Newton's accidental gold standard, the Bank Restriction's 24-year fiat experiment, the Currency School's Parliamentary triumph in 1844, the interwar gold catastrophe, Bretton Woods, Nixon's gold-window closure, and ends with a dual reckoning of the 2008 and 2021 episodes against three steelmanned critiques (Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace/Cochrane, Hayek's knowledge problem). It positions itself explicitly as the historical foundation under MMT's descriptive claims while declining to advocate the policy program; it concedes ground to Friedman, Currency School, public-choice, and Hayek-Selgin-Ammous on their own strongest terms.

**Target reader.** The Tooze/Ahamed/Lewis cohort — financially literate but non-specialist trade nonfiction readers who buy *Crashed*, *Lords of Finance*, *The Deficit Myth*, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, and *The Big Short*. Readers radicalized or confused by 2008, COVID stimulus, the 2021 inflation, and the household-budget framing of fiscal politics. Estimated length sits in the 130,000–160,000-word zone — squarely within category norm.

**Marketing hooks.** The architecture is strong. (1) Single-sentence thesis — "money is institutional credit, given value by the power to tax and bounded only by productive capacity" — that returns as a refrain at each Part close. (2) Sustained character work: Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn (Ch. 3–5), Attwood at Newhall Hill (Ch. 7–9), Keynes at Bretton Woods (Ch. 10), Volcker as the through-line from Princeton 1949 to Camp David 1971 to the 1979 disinflation (Chs. 10–12B). (3) Visceral human cost vignettes — the Todd family making shoes from coal sacks during the 1926 lockout, Aunt Berta's salt herring in Weimar, the Hagedorns' Iowa diary entry during Volcker's recession, Maureen McKenzie's 2008 foreclosure. (4) The Currency School institutional-capture chapter (Ch. 8) lands as standalone material — Overstone, Norman, the Political Economy Club — that could excerpt in *The Atlantic* or *The Economist*. (5) Topical relevance: 2021 inflation post-mortem, CBDC governance, China's dollar trap, eurozone-as-Edward-III all give the book newsworthy hooks beyond the historical.

**Structural concerns.** Three real ones. First, length and density: the manuscript is *long*, and Chapters 6–8 in particular pack five distinct arguments (Newton's accident, Hamburg's currency conversion, the Bank Restriction, Attwood's biography, Currency School institutional capture) into Part III. Some readers will fall off. Second, the 12A/12B split: the author has clearly grappled with the analytical structure here, and the resulting two-chapter shape is more honest than a single triumphalist Chapter 12 would be — but the reader has just spent 100,000 words building toward "validation" and now gets a chapter explaining what the validation does *not* settle. The intellectual courage is admirable; the narrative payoff suffers. Third, the four-Part structure under-signposts: the reader needs a clearer architectural map in the Preface and Part dividers to know where she is.

**Editorial conditions.** I would acquire conditional on three revisions. (1) Tighten Part III by ~20%, primarily by consolidating the Hamburg material into the Germany 1871 narrative and trimming the Real Bills exposition. (2) Restructure the Ch. 12A/12B sequence so the reader feels the validation land *before* the qualifications arrive — possibly by reorganizing 12B's three critiques as discrete sections within a single, sharper concluding chapter. (3) A serious read on the "Currency School institutional capture" framing in Ch. 8 — the argument is correct but the rhetorical register tilts polemical in places that risk legal review and that an unsympathetic reviewer at the *FT* would weaponize.

**Recommendation: Acquire with revisions.** This is a serious book. The thesis is load-bearing, the historical research is real, the prose mostly earns its register, and the character work in the Backwell, Keynes, Volcker, and Attwood threads is the kind that sells trade nonfiction. The author has done the hardest editorial work — steelmanning the opposition — that most books in this lineage skip. The 12A/12B restructuring suggests an author already revising honestly. With a strong line edit and a sharpened final chapter, this sits at the upper end of the comp range. **Native verdict: acquire with revisions.**

# 2. Working Economic Historian (Tooze/Mehrling/Eichengreen orbit)

## Essay-Review

In *Money, Trust, and Power*, the author attempts what is by now a familiar synthesis — the credit-theory-of-money tradition, running from Mitchell Innes through Knapp, Goodhart, Wray, Hudson, Ingham, and Graeber — and tries to do something the popular literature in this lineage has not quite done: take the opposing positions seriously enough that a reader who finishes the book has been instructed in both halves of the argument. The Preface is explicit about this. Friedman's monetarism, the Currency School, Buchanan-Wagner public choice, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane's FTPL, Hayek's knowledge problem — each is named as something the manuscript will steelman before it answers. Most books in this tradition do not bother. Kelton's *Deficit Myth* certainly does not; Graeber's *Debt* gestures at opposition but rarely engages it. The author's stated ambition is therefore unusual: a credit-theory book that treats its critics' best version as load-bearing rather than as a foil.

How well does the book deliver on that ambition? The answer is uneven, but on the whole more honest than I expected when I began. The new Chapter 12B — separating the validation case from the critical tests — is the structural decision that lets the book do what the Preface promises. It allows Chapter 12A to make the productive-capacity case from 2008, 2020, and the Bank of Japan's record, and then permits 12B to engage Buchanan-Wagner, Sargent-Wallace, Cochrane, and Hayek as standing critiques the framework owes answers to. The treatment of Volcker's 1979 disinflation in 12B is the section I most respect: it neither rolls Volcker into a hero narrative (the Friedman lineage's preferred reading) nor reduces him to a bondholder enforcer (the cruder MMT framing); it presents the South American counterfactual Volcker thought he was preventing, names the diffuse class of households entrenched inflation would have ruined, and concedes that "Volcker's choice was therefore not between cruelty and gentleness. It was between two patterns of cruelty." That is the right register for a serious treatment.

The book's strongest historiographic chapters are the Bank of England material (Chapter 5) and the Bullionist–Currency School–Banking School contest in Chapters 7 and 8. The reconstruction of the 1844 Bank Charter Act as the consolidation of a long political-and-intellectual contest, decided through institutional capture as much as through argument — Overstone's £2 million fortune, the 51-year Norman directorship, the Norman family's 123-year continuity at the Bank, the Political Economy Club elections — is well-evidenced and tracks the modern revisionist literature on the institutional contest of 1820–1850. The "paired vindication" framing in Chapter 8 — that Attwood was directionally right about money's non-neutrality *and* that the Currency School's worry about an issuer with discretionary power and a fiscal master was vindicated repeatedly by Weimar, Argentina, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Turkey — is the kind of two-handed argument the credit-theory literature rarely makes. It is the chapter I would recommend to a graduate student who wants to understand what is at stake in the 1844 Act without being indoctrinated.

The book's signature device is the human-scale vignette. Amat-Sîn and Šāt-Sîn at the temple of Gula in 1801 BCE; Datini's December 1399 bill of exchange; Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn; Harry Todd at Boldon Colliery; the Klemperer and Preuß families in Weimar; Michinao Kono in Takarazuka; the Hagedorn farm in Iowa; Maureen McKenzie in Kirkwood, Missouri. The technique is borrowed from Ahamed and Tooze, and at its best it carries real argumentative weight — the Todd hessian-bag shoes are the cost of Churchill's $4.86, made arithmetic. But the device is uneven across the manuscript. The Backwell reconstruction is exemplary; the chapter has clearly been worked carefully against Pepys, Quinn's dissertation, the NatWest archives, and the *Calendar of Treasury Books*. The Lebesque vignette is sourced to his memoir. The Klemperer material is anchored to the famous diaries. But other vignettes — particularly the more anonymized characters like "Farmer A" and Maureen McKenzie — function more as illustrative archetypes than as primary historical evidence, and a careful reader will notice that the prose around them sometimes reaches for a novelistic specificity ("the priest scratched: 'Farmer Enlil: -3 barley'") that the cuneiform record does not actually deliver.

Spot-checking the historical claims: I checked Pepys on Backwell pre-1672, and the manuscript's reconstruction (the June 1662 crusados scene with Mrs. Backwell and Don Duarte de Silva, the 1666 Fire material, the November 1667 "Sheerness vain glory" remark, the July 1666 "good master, the King" line) tracks the published Latham-Matthews diary edition correctly. The Briggs/Miller/Moss historiographic caution on Attwood's Birmingham years is faithfully registered — the chapter explicitly cites Briggs's "It would be unhistorical to call Attwood's analysis 'quasi-Keynesian'" and walks back the proto-Keynesian laurel rather than claiming it. The Volcker material on the August 1971 Camp David weekend matches *Keeping At It* and Silber: Volcker did draft a contingency plan, did prefer a coordinated multilateral approach, was overruled by Connally, and did record the British £3 billion request as the moment "the game was indeed over." Skidelsky on Keynes's Thornton lineage — that Wicksell's cumulative process descends from Thornton, that Keynes's *Treatise* inherits a Thornton-derived monetary architecture, that Hayek edited the 1939 reprint — is correctly rendered. The Hudson vs. Van De Mieroop disagreement on Mesopotamian credit is named and the chapter chooses Hudson's framing while flagging the choice in the text. On Steil vs. Helleiner on Bretton Woods, the Notes on Sources are unusually candid: the chapter follows Steil's power-political reading and explicitly tells the reader that competing accounts (Helleiner, the middle-ground Skidelsky) give the institutional-design dimension of the dollar standard more independent weight. That kind of declared interpretive choice is exactly what scholarly trade nonfiction should do. The Notes on Sources are, as a whole, the best thing in the manuscript — discursive, honest about contested questions, and unusually willing to name where the chapter has chosen one defensible reading over another.

What the book does not deliver is original archival contribution. This is competent synthesis rather than discovery. The Backwell ledger figures are Quinn's; the Story County farm material is Murray's 1933 study; the Hamburg currency-conversion arithmetic is well-documented in the German monetary-history literature. The contribution lies in the assembly — in putting Mesopotamia, the Datini archive, Backwell, Paterson, Newton, Attwood, Churchill, Bretton Woods, Volcker, 2008, and 2021 inside a single argumentative arc — not in primary research. The author is honest about this; the Preface explicitly disavows "recovery of suppressed knowledge" and frames the book as "a translation of a settled scholarly tradition for the readers who deserve to have it." That framing is right. This is a Mehrling-Eichengreen-O'Sullivan-aware popularization, not a contribution to the scholarship.

The manuscript's weaknesses are largely the costs of its ambition. The book runs long. The Mesopotamia-to-Datini-to-Backwell arc occupies four chapters when three would do. The 12A/12B split is structurally correct but pulls the book away from narrative momentum at exactly the moment a trade reader needs it. The prose is competent throughout but rarely musical; it does not have Ahamed's gift for portraiture, Lewis's pacing, or Tooze's controlled aerial-view-then-ground-zoom rhythm. The "Money is institutional credit, given value by the power to tax and bounded only by the productive capacity of the economies that bear it" refrain at the end of each Part is the kind of pedagogical scaffolding that helps the argument and slightly cheapens the prose.

**Verdict: competent synthesis.** This is competent synthesis at the high end of the category — closer to Ahamed-and-Tooze ambition than to the typical popularization, anchored to the standard literature, honest about contested questions, and unusual within its tradition for engaging its critics' best versions. It is not original contribution: there is no archival discovery, no new analytical apparatus, no interpretation that meaningfully revises the modern monetary-history consensus. But it is not "not for specialists" either; the historiographic apparatus and the steelmanned critical engagement put it within range of recommended reading for a graduate seminar on monetary history that wants to give students the credit-theory case in its strongest form alongside its most serious critics.

### (b) Bestseller-tier craft question

The book's actual peer is something like Mehrling's *New Lombard Street* extended to general audiences, which is a real and respectable category but not a bestseller category. The fix is craft, not concept: tighter prose, sharper scene-economy in Part I, fewer refrains. With those revisions and Tooze-tier blurb support and the right institutional adoption (NYT Books, FT, Foreign Affairs), bestseller-tier sales become plausible.

# 3. Heterodox / MMT Reviewer (Chartalist / Post-Keynesian Tradition)

## Essay-Review

Among the books that have tried to popularize the chartalist-credit lineage for a general audience — Graeber's *Debt*, Kelton's *Deficit Myth*, Wray's various reconstructions — *Money, Trust, and Power* is the first that feels like it was written for the reader who would be uncomfortable in any of those rooms. The book opens at the dispatch box in 1797 and closes on a Camp David weekend in 1971, and across the five thousand years between those scenes it executes a project that is rarer and harder than most credit-money trade books attempt: it tries to assemble the historical case for the descriptive claims of the chartalist tradition without the polemical posture that has, fairly or not, kept the tradition off the New York Times bestseller list for fifty years.

The descriptive bones are solid. Taxation drives currency demand: the Mesopotamian temple credits of Chapter 1, the medieval Exchequer tallies of Chapter 3, the Massachusetts bills of credit, the colonial hut taxes of Chapter 8, the Bank Restriction's empirical proof in Chapter 7 — each one delivered with documentary specificity. Money is institutional credit: Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn appears across three chapters with the kind of biographical depth Ahamed gave the central bankers of the 1920s. The book understands, and demonstrates with appropriate scale, that productive capacity is the binding constraint — the World War II inflation-versus-mobilization arithmetic, the 2008 QE versus 2021 ARP comparison in Chapter 12A, the Japan three-decade case. The Innes-Knapp-Wray-Tcherneva-Mehrling lineage is faithfully extended. Paterson's "out of nothing" is in the Bank of England chapter; Hudson's *amargi* framing is in the first chapter; Mehrling's hierarchical-money intuition runs through the two-tier pyramid argument in Chapter 5. A reader familiar with the modern MMT canon will find their framework treated with respect and the historical foundation it has long lacked.

The harder question is whether the steelmanning has hedged the argument into mush. Mostly, it has not. The Currency School chapters are the test, and Chapter 8 holds. The "Vindication, Both Sides" section names directly what most chartalist popularizers will not name: that the Currency School was right about something. Discretionary issuance under fiscal duress has, on the historical record from Weimar through Argentina to Turkey, repeatedly produced the over-issuance Peel and Overstone described. The book treats this as a worry the framework owes an institutional answer to, rather than as embarrassing evidence to be waved away. Chapter 12B carries the argument into the contemporary case, where Sargent-Wallace and Cochrane's fiscal-dominance critique gets a fairer hearing than I have seen in any other trade-nonfiction credit-money book — and where the productive-capacity framework is held responsible for what it cannot, on its own, settle. Hayek's knowledge problem is named directly. The chapter does not pretend to resolve it. That epistemic humility is rare in this lineage and earns the book a credibility it could not have purchased through assertion.

What worries me is what the steelmanning costs at the level of force. The framework's home claims — that productive capacity is the real constraint, that the household-budget metaphor is incoherent, that the post-1971 record vindicates fiat — are stated, but they are stated softly. The Preface explicitly disavows advocacy of "the federal job guarantee, the consolidated government balance sheet" as topics for "their own arguments and their own books." Chapter 12B describes the rules-based and stabilizer-based traditions as if the choice between them were a tea-room debate; the author's "view" is offered in a single sentence that arrives almost apologetically near the end. Kelton would not have written it this way. Wray would not. The historical case is built well enough that the political implications should detonate. They do not detonate. They sit politely on the shelf where the next book is supposed to go.

This restraint has costs that fall along recognizable political lines. The distributional honesty of Chapters 8 and 9 is real — the 1821 deflation as wealth transfer, the 1925 return to gold as a transfer from miners to bondholders, Volcker's recession as a distributional choice between two classes of cruelty — but the framework rarely names *who benefits* from the orthodoxy in the present tense. The household-budget metaphor's persistence is described as an institutional inertia question (Chapter 8's coda); it is not described, as it ought to be in a book at this lineage, as a class commitment in technical clothing. The MMT critique that Buchanan-style fiscal rules entrench an asymmetry — austerity treated as the absence of a problem rather than as its own problem — is allowed into Chapter 12B, but it is allowed in once, briefly, and balanced immediately by the procedural disclaimer that "this is not an advocacy book." The rentier class that has actually run rich-country fiscal politics for forty years gets named, but never indicted.

The 2021 inflation reading is the place where the apologetic posture most clearly weakens the argument. The chapter concedes — too much, in my reading — that "the framework's heterodox proponents" got the call wrong, and treats Blanchard and Summers's directional victory as a partial vindication of the orthodox demand-overshoot story. The post-hoc decompositions the chapter cites do attribute a substantial share of the inflation to demand pressure, but a careful productive-capacity reading should have stressed harder that the fiscal package's contribution was small relative to the supply destruction COVID imposed on capacity, that the eurozone hit a comparable peak with a smaller fiscal trajectory, and that a framework that says "capacity was binding" is exactly the framework that *predicts* a binding-capacity inflation under the conditions of 2021. The chapter has the elements but does not assemble them into the punch the framework had earned.

The craft is high. The Datini bill of exchange in Chapter 2, the Backwell scenes in Chapters 3 and 4, the Todd family in Chapter 9, the Hagedorn farm in Chapter 12B, the Klemperer hyperinflation diary, the Lebesque Métro winter, the Iowa "Farmer A" — these are the human anchors a trade book in this category needs, and they are reported with the documentary specificity of *Lords of Finance* rather than the rhetorical compression of *Debt*. The "we follow one coin" set-piece in Chapter 6, the Ur III barley loan with its tablet catalogue numbers in Chapter 1, the Camp David weekend in Chapter 11 — these are the moments where the book becomes physically readable in the way trade nonfiction has to be readable. The thread sentence — *Money is institutional credit, given value by the power to tax and bounded only by the productive capacity of the economies that bear it* — returns at the close of each Part, and the device works. It is the kind of structural commitment a Tooze book makes.

**Verdict: faithful translation, partial.** The historical case for the chartalist tradition's descriptive claims is delivered with documentary care, the steelmans are taken seriously, and the framework's home claims are intact. What is partial is the political-economy candor about whose interests the orthodox framing has served, and the willingness to let the descriptive case land with the force the source tradition has spent a century trying to deliver.

I would publicly endorse it, with a sentence of caveat — that it is the best historical foundation for the credit-money tradition currently available in trade form, and that the next book this author writes needs to stop apologizing for the implications.

# 4. Target Lay Reader (Goodreads, 4 stars)

## Goodreads Review

**Money, Trust, and Power: Five Thousand Years of Credit, Crisis, and the Institutions That Make Money Work**
★★★★ (4 stars)

Okay, this is the book I wish I'd had three years ago when I was trying to figure out, on Odd Lots and via Tooze tweets, why nobody seemed to know what money actually *is*. I don't have an econ background. I read Graeber's *Debt* twice and loved it but couldn't quite explain to my partner what its argument was. I read Kelton's *Deficit Myth* and finished it without ever feeling like the foundations had been laid — she's making the argument, and I sort of bought it, but I didn't know the history well enough to know whether to trust her. This book is the foundation Kelton's book doesn't bother to build. It's also way better-written than I'd feared from the title.

The core argument is one sentence, and it's repeated at the end of every Part: *Money is institutional credit, given value by the power to tax and bounded only by the productive capacity of the economies that bear it.* The first half of the book makes that argument historically — Mesopotamian temple ledgers, medieval tally sticks, the Bank of England's founding, the Bank Restriction during Napoleon — and the second half makes it analytically through the gold standard's failures and what happened after 1971. I followed all of it. Even the parts I didn't fully follow (some of the medieval banking mechanics in Chapter 2, the Sargent-Wallace stuff in 12B) I followed enough of to know what was being argued.

Where I leaned in hardest: the Backwell chapter (Chapter 4) is fantastic. Edward Backwell at the Sign of the Unicorn, ruined by Charles II's 1672 Stop of the Exchequer, dying in the Netherlands in 1683 — the author makes you feel what it is to have built a 17th-century banking empire and to watch a king's signature destroy it on a Friday afternoon. The Harry Todd chapter (Chapter 9) is even better, and I'd argue it's the emotional center of the book. A Durham coal miner whose family eats sparrows and makes shoes from coal sacks because Winston Churchill wanted the pound at $4.86 instead of $4.77. The Hagedorn farm in 1985, lost to Volcker's interest rates ("I've never been so terrified in my life"), is up there with anything in *Lords of Finance*. The Klemperer salt-herring scene in the Weimar section made me put the book down for a minute.

Where I leaned out: Chapter 8 (the Currency School / Attwood / Bank Charter Act of 1844) is the longest and most demanding chapter, and I'll be honest, around the Lord Overstone Political Economy Club material I started skimming. It's important to the argument — the author wants you to see how an orthodoxy got institutionally captured — but it's the kind of "follow the patient committee work" history that I have less patience for than I should. The same is true of stretches of Chapter 12B, where I got the gist of Buchanan-Wagner versus the MMT job guarantee but felt like the writing got more academic.

The Bretton Woods chapter (Chapter 10) is genuinely thrilling. White's procedural manipulation, Keynes literally collapsing on the hotel stairs, Morgenthau threatening to put troops in the hotel to force the British to sign — this is *Lords of Finance*-quality narrative, and the Volcker thread that runs through Chapters 10, 11, and 12B is the kind of structural craft I always notice and admire. Volcker as a Princeton senior in 1949 reading the documents he'd later have to live with at Camp David is the kind of move a really good editor would have pushed for, and someone clearly did.

What surprised me: how fair the book is to the other side. The preface explicitly says it's going to take Friedman, Buchanan, Hayek, Cochrane, and the Currency School "on their own strongest terms," and it actually does. The Currency School "vindication" passage in Chapter 8, where the author concedes that Weimar/Argentina/Zimbabwe/Venezuela/Turkey are real failures of fiat regimes that the Bullionists were directionally right to worry about, is the kind of argumentative honesty I almost never see in this category. Same with the Bitcoin section in 12A, which gives the Hayek-Selgin-White tradition a serious hearing before saying why it disagrees. The book takes a position but never feels like a polemic.

What's missing for me: I wanted more on China, more on the dollar's geopolitical fragility, more on what happens next. The CBDC discussion in the Epilogue is good but brief. I wanted the author to take a real position on what the next decade looks like, and instead got a "the next chapter has not yet been written" line that felt like a hedge.

Recommend to: anyone who finished *Crashed* and wanted the longer historical setup; anyone who started *Deficit Myth* skeptical and wanted the historical evidence; anyone who's tried to argue with a Bitcoin friend at a dinner party and felt under-equipped. Don't recommend to: someone looking for *The Big Short*-pace narrative (this is more patient than that), or someone who needs a clean political prescription (this book is descriptive on purpose).

Solid four stars. Would be five with tighter editing in Chapters 8 and 12B and a bolder swing at the open questions.
