## Section A — Narrative passage (approx. 1,700–1,900 words)

On paper, the Preuß family belonged to the solid *Mittelstand* that had done everything right before the war. Paul Preuß held a secure civil-service post as a *Gewerbeoberlehrer* at a Berlin vocational school, a rank that placed him in the middle tiers of the Prussian teaching service with a pension entitlement and the social standing of an educated professional. His wife kept house; their three children included Dorothea, born in June 1914, and her younger sister Hilde; together they made up the “five‑headed family” Dorothea later described. No one in 1913 would have imagined that within a decade their salaries, pensions, and savings would be reduced to the value of a single salt herring.[^3][^2][^4][^1]

The family’s network extended beyond Berlin to the small industrial town of Guben on the Neisse, where relatives owned a combined drugstore and seed shop. Aunt Röschen and Uncle Wilhelm ran the business; another of the mother’s siblings, Berta, lived alone and relied on income from investments. Following the familiar advice to middle-class Germans, Berta had put her inheritance into “safe” assets: mortgages on family property, war bonds, and shares—paper claims ultimately guaranteed by the imperial and then republican German state. Before 1914 such holdings were precisely what teachers, retired officials, and small shopkeepers were told to buy if they wanted security in old age.[^5][^1]

The war quietly undermined that security long before anyone in the family saw a French soldier or a mark note with twelve zeros. To finance the conflict, Berlin suspended gold convertibility in 1914 and issued war loans rather than imposing heavy taxes, allowing the Reichsbank to expand the note issue with only paper claims on the Treasury as backing. Prices more than doubled between 1914 and 1919, and although official salaries were raised, they did not keep pace with the cost of living. Like millions of others in the *Mittelstand*, the Preuß family entered the Weimar years already living off a shrinking real income and paper claims eroded by wartime inflation.[^6][^7][^8][^3]

By New Year’s Day 1923, the family’s position looked, in nominal terms, respectable. A mid‑ranking civil servant in Berlin earned tens of thousands of marks a month on the paper salary scales introduced after the war, and mid‑1922 figures show a “mittlerer Beamter” receiving around 26,000 marks per month. But the mark had fallen so far that this income bought only a fraction of what it would have purchased before 1914, and by January 1923 it took roughly 49,000 paper marks to obtain one U.S. dollar, compared with 4.20 marks in 1913. Berta’s mortgages, war bonds, and shares still existed as legal claims, yet their purchasing power had already been “lost shortly after the war,” as Dorothea later wrote.[^9][^8][^10][^1]

The decisive shock came not from a sudden change in prices, but from the breakdown of the state’s ability to enforce its own financial obligations under the pressure of war debts, reparations, and occupation. On 11 January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr to secure reparations in kind after Berlin fell into arrears, and the German government responded by proclaiming “passive resistance” and paying Ruhr workers to strike rather than cooperate with the occupiers. This meant that, on top of existing deficits, the state now took on the wage bill of an entire industrial region without corresponding tax revenues, financing it by further note issues through the Reichsbank. The institutions that had promised to honour bonds, pensions, and civil-service pay thus chose—under acute political pressure—to protect national pride and employment first, and to let the currency absorb the strain.[^11][^12][^13]

In Berlin, the Preuß family experienced this institutional crisis as a progressive shortening of the time between pay and purchase. Dorothea recalled that at the height of the inflation her father no longer received his salary once a month, or even once a week, but every day. “My father, a *Gewerbeoberlehrer* by profession, received his salary payment daily at that time,” she wrote; “when he came home at midday with a briefcase full of banknotes, Mother ran straight to the grocer.” Often she returned empty‑handed, because the shopkeeper had just pulled down the shutters for the midday break—time he used to change the price tags before reopening. The state could still print and distribute money, but could no longer guarantee its value from one mealtime to the next.[^14][^1]

Price series for Berlin put hard numbers behind this recollection. A kilo of bread that cost 0.66 mark in 1913 and 1.09 marks at the end of the war had risen to 474 marks in May 1923, then 2,200 marks by July; by early October, the same loaf cost 14 million marks, and four weeks later 5.6 billion. Tram fares that had been 10 Pfennig before the war climbed to 4 marks by mid‑1921 and reached 150 billion marks at the peak of the crisis. Wages were adjusted frequently, but not enough: in November 1923, a mid‑level civil servant’s monthly pay averaged 73 trillion marks and still bought only about 41 percent of what his pre‑war salary had purchased. For a family like the Preußes, whose breadwinner was stuck on a fixed salary scale and whose savings were in paper assets, the erosion of purchasing power felt like watching the floorboards burn under their feet as they tried to feed five mouths.[^15][^16][^8]

The family’s lifeline ran through the Guben relatives. In her 2010 text, Dorothea evokes the autumn school holidays she and Hilde spent there, travelling alone “in the fourth class in the compartment for travellers with hand luggage,” to be collected at the station by cousins with a handcart. Their uncle and aunt let the girls roam the house and storerooms, eating sweets from the stock—“bonbons, chocolate, peppermint, peanuts”—and, most importantly, help behind the counter in the shop. Each evening ended with the ritual of counting the day’s takings. “In the autumn of 1923,” she wrote, “we took in billions each day, then even trillions. We children could handle these then-unimaginable sums without difficulty.” As the denominations climbed, old 100‑ and 1,000‑mark notes were overprinted in red with new values in millions and billions, bundled, and carried in large bags to the bank.[^1]

In real terms, these fantastic sums often amounted to little more than a pre‑war day’s turnover, but the imagery captures the inversion of all previous monetary habits. While the Guben shop could pass on rising prices to customers and receive new notes each evening, Paul Preuß’s salary and Aunt Berta’s investments lagged behind. At home in Berlin, Dorothea notes, “the hyperinflation made life difficult for us,” and without occasional food parcels from Guben “things would have looked bad for the provisioning of the five‑person family.” The distinction between those with goods to sell—shopkeepers, farmers, exporters—and those dependent on fixed nominal incomes had become stark.[^1]

The most brutal demonstration of this difference in the family came when Berta’s remaining mortgages were repaid. Berta, described by Dorothea as “kind‑hearted and helpful” but lacking “mental gifts or practical abilities,” had never married and so lacked the security of a husband’s salary or pension; her livelihood depended on the interest from mortgages and the coupons on war loans and shares that had once been classed as *mündelsicher*, suitable even for guardians of orphans. These investments had been “lost already shortly after the war,” as the state paid interest in ever‑depreciating paper, but the “high point of the blows to the neck” came when one of her sisters, Emma, repaid Berta the principal on the family mortgages “without prior notice” in 1923.[^5][^1]

Berta ran downstairs, weeping, to brother Wilhelm’s shop, clutching the bundles of notes. Wilhelm did what any retailer facing tomorrow’s price rises would do: he sold her “a salt herring” for the entire sum. The herring became “a legend in the family history,” a synecdoche for the destruction of an entire stratum’s carefully accumulated savings. It was not that the herring was unusually expensive; it was that by the time the state nominally honoured its promise to repay Berta’s capital, the mark it paid with no longer had any power. The institutional failure—war loans backed by a defeated state, mortgages repaid in a collapsing currency—preceded and produced the spectacle of trillions changing hands for a piece of fish.[^1]

By October 1923, the monetary system had effectively ceased to function as a store of value or even a medium of exchange in many parts of Germany. The dollar–mark exchange rate, which had stood at 49,000 marks per dollar at the end of January, passed 4.86 million in early August, 53 million in early September, 440 million by 3 October, and more than 32 billion by 22 October. In mid‑November it peaked at around 4.2 trillion, a million‑million times the pre‑war parity. Farmers and traders increasingly withheld goods, and the Bundestag’s own retrospective account notes that economic exchange “stalled” in the autumn as money lost its function as both means of payment and store of value. For civil servants like Preuß, paid in paper marks, this meant that by the time a salary was brought home and turned into food, it no longer bore any stable relationship to prices in shops.[^17][^18][^11][^9]

The stabilization in November 1923 was as abrupt as the collapse had been drawn‑out. The Rentenmark was introduced on 15 November at a rate of one Rentenmark to one trillion (*10^12*) paper marks, with an official gold parity identical to the pre‑war mark, and a promise of limited issuance backed not by gold but by mortgages on German land and industry. As part of the same stabilization package, the Reich government enacted the Personalabbauverordnung of 27 October 1923, which aimed to cut public‑sector payrolls by around 25 percent and authorized mass dismissals of “dispensable” employees. At the same time, a new *Besoldungsgesetz* converted civil‑service salaries into stable *Goldmark* equivalents; contemporary calculations show that lower‑grade officials regained roughly 70–75 percent of their 1913 basic pay in real terms, while middle ranks, like many secondary‑school teachers, received about 47 percent and higher ranks only 39–43 percent.[^19][^20][^21][^18][^11]

For the Preuß family, the practical effect was that Paul’s pay packets stopped swelling in nominal terms and became small again in Rentenmark, but they no longer had to be delivered daily and spent at once. Statistical reconstructions suggest that by December 1923 the real value of civil‑service salaries had fallen to around 60 percent of their pre‑war level, compared to 70 percent for industrial wages. In other words, stabilization preserved their status as salaried *Mittelstand* but locked in a permanent loss of wealth and purchasing power; there was no mechanism to restore Aunt Berta’s war bonds or mortgages to anything like their previous value. “All who were not clever enough to hoard forbidden stable currencies or gold,” as an Austrian contemporary put it of a similar experience, “have, without exception, suffered losses … we belong to the new poor.” Berta’s salt herring was the family’s lived version of that arithmetic.[^8][^22]

The later 1920s brought a measure of social recovery, but not restitution. National income and industrial output rose in the so‑called “golden years” of Dawes‑Plan Germany, and the hyperinflation receded into memory even as it left what one recent study calls a “trauma” in the collective imagination. Dorothea’s own biography indicates that the family managed to remain in the educated middle class: after completing intermediate school, she attended the Lette‑Haus, a higher commercial school for girls in Berlin, where she studied foreign languages, shorthand, and typing. By 1929, aged fifteen, she could take part in the first Franco‑German youth exchange organized by the Deutsche Liga für Menschenrechte, spending six weeks with a French family in Paris—a sign that, while the family had lost capital, they retained enough income and social capital to support educational mobility in the new republic.[^23][^12][^2]

Yet the hyperinflation had permanently altered how families like the Preußes related to the state and its money. Scholarly reconstructions underline that middle‑class savers, pensioners, and civil servants bore disproportionate real losses, while debtors and industrialists with real assets often emerged strengthened. Dorothea’s later career embodies one common response: seeking security not in private savings but in state employment. After working as a clerk and foreign‑language correspondent, she joined the Foreign Office’s translation service in 1941, formally entering the NSDAP in order to secure the post—an act she later presented matter‑of‑factly as a career requirement rather than an ideological calling.[^12][^24][^2]

By the time the Depression hit in 1929, the cohort that had watched its “safe” investments turn into a salt herring in 1923 now faced mass unemployment, business failures, and renewed political extremism. For the Preuß family, the record is silent on how they voted or what they thought of the Nazis’ promises to restore order and punish those blamed for earlier catastrophes; Dorothea’s recollections of the inflation years, written at the end of her life, contain no retrospective moralizing. They do, however, fix in family memory the moment when the German state proved unable to honour its own money and debt, and a teacher’s lifetime of thrift and a spinster aunt’s careful investments were commuted, at the cashier’s counter, into a single preserved fish.[^2][^23][^12][^1]

## Section B — Sources and methods (approx. 600–700 words)

This reconstruction rests primarily on one named ego-document, supplemented by institutional biographical material and quantitative series on wages, prices, and exchange rates for 1913–1923.

1. **Core ego-document and biographical note**

The central primary source is Dorothea Günther (née Preuß), “Die Inflation 1923,” a witness text written in June 2010 for the Stiftung Haus der Geschichte’s “Lebendiges Museum Online” (LeMO) project. In this short memoir, Günther recalls childhood visits in autumn 1923 to relatives who owned a drugstore and seed shop in Guben, the experience of counting daily takings in “billions” and later “trillions,” the overprinting of old 100‑ and 1,000‑mark notes in higher denominations, and the routine of bundling and carrying the money to the bank. She then describes the impact of hyperinflation on her own household in Berlin, noting explicitly that her father was a *Gewerbeoberlehrer*, that he received his salary daily during the crisis, that he came home at midday with a briefcase full of notes, and that her mother immediately ran to the grocer only to find the shutters drawn while the shopkeeper changed price tags.[^1]

The same text gives the key anecdote about her Aunt Berta: an unmarried, “good‑hearted” but impractical woman who had invested her inheritance in mortgages, war bonds, and shares; that these investments were already lost “shortly after the war”; and that in 1923 her sister Emma repaid the mortgage principal, whereupon Berta ran downstairs in tears to brother Wilhelm’s shop and received a single salt herring in exchange for the bundle of notes. This anecdote is the basis of the “salt herring” episode in Section A.[^1]

A second LeMO entry, “Die Flucht vor dem Geheimdienst,” provides a biographical “Zur Person” note on Dorothea Günther. It confirms her full name, birth and death dates (1914–2010), place of birth (Berlin), and parentage (she was “born on 1 June 1914 as the daughter of the *Gewerbeoberlehrer* Paul Preuß”). The same note outlines her later education at the Lette‑Haus commercial school, her work as a clerk and foreign‑language correspondent, her employment in the Foreign Office’s translation service from 1941, and her NSDAP membership to secure that post, as well as her marriage and post‑war flight from the Soviet zone. These biographical details underpin the statements about the family’s social location and Dorothea’s later career.[^2]

Because both texts are retrospective (written in 2010 about events nearly ninety years in the past), they are treated as carefully as one would treat a memoir rather than a contemporaneous diary. Specific quotations in Section A reproduce Günther’s German wording (e.g. “Mein Vater, Gewerbeoberlehrer von Beruf, erhielt in dieser Zeit seine Gehaltszahlung täglich”) in translation; in a Chicago footnote apparatus these should be cited to the LeMO web page with date and URL, with the translation explicitly credited to the book’s author.[^1]

2. **Quantitative context: wages, salaries, prices, exchange rates**

To situate the Preuß family’s experience in the broader dynamics of hyperinflation, Section A draws on a set of quantitative reconstructions:

- The website inflation1923.de, curated by financial journalist Frank Stocker, provides time series for wages and salaries and for key consumer prices. Its page on “Löhne und Gehälter” states that in November 1923 the average monthly income of a “mittlerer Beamter” was 73 trillion marks and that this corresponded to about 41 percent of his pre‑war real income; it also notes that by December 1923 the real value of civil‑service salaries had fallen to roughly 60 percent of 1913 levels, compared to 70 percent for workers’ weekly wages. The “Preise” page gives pre‑war, wartime, and hyperinflation peak prices for bread, butter, eggs, and tram fares in Berlin (for example, a rye bread rising from 0.66 mark pre‑war to 840 billion marks at the peak, and a tram fare from 0.10 mark to 150 billion marks).[^16][^8]
- A *WirtschaftsWoche* feature on the 1923 hyperinflation supplies intermediate bread prices in Berlin (474 marks in May 1923, 2,200 marks in July, 14 million in early October, and 5.6 billion in early November), which are used in Section A to give the month‑by‑month feel of escalating costs.[^15]
- The German Wikipedia article “Deutsche Inflation 1914 bis 1923” contains a table of exchange rates and postage charges that is based on standard secondary literature; the key dollar–mark rates for January, June, August, September, and October 1923, as cited in Section A, are taken directly from this table. The same article confirms the conversion rate of one Rentenmark for one trillion paper marks in the November 1923 stabilization.[^18][^9]
- A Bundestag historical note on the 1923 hyperinflation summarizes the social effects of the crisis and reports that economic exchange “stalled” as farmers and traders withheld goods, that wages and salaries lost purchasing power, and that around a quarter of public‑sector employees were dismissed following the stabilization measures; these contextual statements are referenced where Section A speaks of exchange collapsing and the severity of civil‑service cuts.[^11]
- A Reichskanzlei document on the twelfth supplement to the *Besoldungsgesetz* (salary law) is used to support the claim that, after stabilization, new gold‑based salaries restored roughly 70–75 percent of pre‑war real pay for lower civil servants but only around 47 percent for middle ranks and 39–43 percent for higher officials.[^20]

3. **Macro‑political context**

The linkage between war finance, reparations, the Ruhr occupation, passive resistance, and the explosion of inflation is drawn from several recent syntheses:

- A 2023 DIW (German Institute for Economic Research) article on the German hyperinflation emphasizes the role of monetized budget deficits, war debts, and reparations, and notes the cumulative “depreciation–wage–price–depreciation spiral” that culminated in the 1923 collapse of the monetary system.[^12]
- An ECB blog post and a Deutsche Bundesbank publication on the 1923 inflation provide the framing that hyperinflation obliterated savings and financial assets, hit pensioners and fixed‑income groups especially hard, and has been remembered as a national trauma that shaped later German attitudes toward fiscal and monetary policy.[^25][^23]
- Deutsche Welle’s centennial article “1923: How Weimar combatted hyperinflation” is cited for the narrative that the Ruhr occupation in January 1923, the government’s call for passive resistance, and the printing of money to pay striking workers were decisive in “fueling the depreciation of its own currency.”[^13]

Although Volker Ullrich’s *Deutschland 1923: Das Jahr am Abgrund* (C.H. Beck, 2022) and Gerald D. Feldman’s *The Great Disorder: Politics, Economics, and Society in the German Inflation, 1914–1924* (Oxford, 1993) are important secondary works on this period, the online snippets consulted do not provide new named Mittelstand case studies beyond what the LeMO material offers. They are therefore cited, if at all, only for general statements already corroborated by the sources above.[^26][^27]

4. **Limits and uncertainties**

There are no surviving salary slips or bank ledgers for Paul Preuß in the accessible material, so statements about his nominal income rely on average figures for “mittlerer Beamter” and are explicitly presented as typical rather than precise. The household composition (five‑person family) and investment choices (Berta’s mortgages, war loans, shares) come directly from Dorothea’s recollection, but the exact amounts of those assets and their conversion into Rentenmark cannot be reconstructed from available sources. Similarly, the family’s political attitudes in 1929–33 are unknown; the narrative therefore stops short of attributing specific responses to the Depression or to the Nazi rise to power, beyond noting Dorothea’s later NSDAP membership as documented in the biographical note.[^10][^8][^2][^1]

Within these constraints, the passage uses only details that can be anchored either in Dorothea Günther’s own text or in quantitative and institutional sources, and refrains from inventing dialogue, internal thoughts, or undocumented episodes.

## Section C — Runner‑up candidates (approx. 250–300 words)

Several other potential case studies were considered; two in particular offer rich material but were ultimately set aside in favour of the Preuß family.

1. **Erna von Pustau and her Hamburg family**

Erna von Pustau, the daughter of a Hamburg fish merchant, recalled her experience of hyperinflation in an extended interview with Pearl S. Buck, published as *How It Happens: Talk About the German People, 1914–1933* (New York: John Day, 1947). Excerpts widely used in teaching and in Adam Fergusson’s *When Money Dies* show her reflecting on the uneven impact of inflation—“the winners in our inflation were big businessmen… the great losers were the working class and above all the middle class, who had most to lose”—and on popular confusions about the mark’s fall (“we used to say: ‘The dollar is going up again,’ while in reality the dollar remained stable and our mark was falling”). Her account offers vivid detail on urban life, food queues, and resentments, and the family’s small business positions them on the boundary between working and lower middle class. However, because the father is a small trader rather than a salaried civil servant or pensioner, and because the family’s pre‑war situation and post‑1923 trajectory are less clearly documented, the case rhymes less closely with the Todd and Hagedorn material than the Preuß–Berta constellation does.[^7][^28][^29][^30][^31]

2. **Anna Eisenmenger in Vienna**

Anna Eisenmenger, a Viennese middle‑class widow, kept a diary later published as *Blockade*, which Fergusson uses extensively to illustrate how Austrian hyperinflation destroyed the savings and securities of a family living on interest and pensions. Her notes about watching “gilt‑edged” securities evaporate and about ending as part of “the new poor” closely parallel Berta’s experience, and the diary’s contemporaneity makes it methodologically attractive. The main drawback is geographic: Eisenmenger’s story unfolds in Austria, not Germany, and the legal and institutional context of the Austrian krone differs from that of the German mark.[^22][^7]

3. **Anonymous and aggregated Mittelstand cases**

Both Feldman and Ullrich make extensive use of letters and diaries from German civil servants, teachers, and pensioners, but in the accessible digital samples these are either anonymized or cited only briefly, without enough continuous material to sustain a narrative chapter focused on a named family. Given the manuscript’s requirement for a fully named, structurally rhyming counterpart to Harry Todd and the Hagedorns, these otherwise valuable sources could not supply a single anchor figure.[^27][^32][^26]

Against this field, the Preuß–Günther family stands out by combining a fully named *Mittelstand* household, a documented civil‑service position, a clearly described destruction of paper savings, and a later life course that can be traced at least into the early Federal Republic, making it the strongest candidate for the role you need.
