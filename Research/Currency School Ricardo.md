# Currency School and Ricardo on Their Own Terms: A Steelman (1810–1850)

## Executive Overview

Between roughly 1810 and 1850, the so‑called Currency School—building on bullionist arguments and Ricardo’s monetary writings—advanced a program for restructuring the British monetary system around a strict “currency principle.” In their own terms, the central problem was how to prevent the abuses of inconvertible paper, arbitrary debasement, and politically convenient inflation that had characterized both the Bank Restriction period (1797–1819) and notorious continental episodes such as the French assignats. They believed that the stability of all long‑term contracts, the distribution of wealth between debtors and creditors, and the integrity of the British state’s finances were at stake.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

The Currency School’s answer, developed by figures such as David Ricardo, Samuel Jones Loyd (Lord Overstone), and Robert Torrens, was that the note issue must be forced by law to behave exactly as a purely metallic currency would behave under the gold standard. They argued that the empirical record of the Restriction period showed a close connection between excessive note issue, a rise in the market price of gold above the mint price, and depreciation of the exchange—evidence, in their reading, for a strict quantity‑theoretic relation between the stock of paper and the price level. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 attempted to legislate this “currency principle” into the institutional structure of the Bank of England.[^3][^6][^7][^8][^9]

From a modern perspective, the Currency School was right to insist that credible convertibility and constraints on the monetary base matter for long‑run price stability, and that leaving the issue of inconvertible paper entirely to the discretion of a profit‑seeking issuer and a fiscally stressed government is dangerous. Where their model failed, however—and where this failure became increasingly undeniable after 1847—was in assuming that controlling the note issue alone was sufficient to control the money supply and prevent crises. The development of deposit banking and credit pyramiding, and the sequence of crises in 1847, 1857, and 1866, forced a shift toward a central‑banking view centered on lender‑of‑last‑resort responsibilities, most famously articulated by Walter Bagehot in *Lombard Street*.[^10][^11][^12][^13][^14][^15][^16]

What follows reconstructs the Currency School’s case as sympathetically as possible, using the sources they themselves invoked and the later historical literature that has interpreted their program.


## 1. The Problem They Thought They Were Solving

### 1.1 Restriction‑era inflation and the assignat trauma

The starting point for the Currency School was the experience of the Bank Restriction, when cash payments were suspended in 1797 and the Bank of England’s notes became inconvertible. During the wars with Revolutionary and Napoleonic France, the government’s fiscal needs were enormous, and the temptation to rely on inconvertible paper rather than tax increases or explicit loans was obvious. Bullionist writers—Ricardo among them—argued that the subsequent rise in prices, the premium on bullion, and the depreciation of the exchange were the predictable monetary consequences of an over‑issue of paper not constrained by convertibility.[^4][^17][^8][^9][^1][^3]

Continental experience appeared to confirm these dangers. The French assignat experiment, culminating in hyperinflation and repudiation in the 1790s, loomed large as a cautionary tale about the collapse of a paper regime unconstrained by metallic convertibility or clear rules. Currency theorists drew from this the lesson that once a state and its privileged bank had access to unconstrained paper finance, political pressures would always push toward excess. Control over the quantity of paper money therefore could not be left to discretion.[^6][^3]

### 1.2 Arbitrary debasement and the costs of inconvertibility

The Currency School interpreted inconvertibility and debasement not as isolated technical flaws but as systematic threats to contract and property. Ricardo, in *Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency* and in the relevant chapters of his *Principles*, insisted that a “perfect” currency must be “absolutely invariable in value,” at least as far as human institutions could approximate such invariance. An inconvertible paper, subject to discretionary enlargement by the issuer, violated this ideal by redistributing wealth whenever its quantity changed.[^12][^7][^9][^6]

More concretely, they believed that each episode of inflation induced by over‑issue—measured in their framework by a premium on bullion and a depreciation of the exchange—unjustly benefited debtors (including the state) at the expense of creditors and fixed‑income claimants. This was not merely a matter of short‑run misallocation; it signaled that the state could not be trusted to respect nominal contracts. For figures like Overstone, whose correspondence repeatedly stresses the importance of an “equable” and “stable” standard, the very legitimacy of the legal and commercial order depended on preventing such hidden taxation via monetary manipulation.[^8][^3][^6][^12]

### 1.3 The stakes: standard of value, state credibility, and systemic stability

In Currency School rhetoric, what was ultimately at stake was the integrity of the standard of value and, with it, the credibility of the British state in domestic and international finance. The gold standard, as restored after 1821, was not simply a technical rule for note redemption; it was a public commitment device binding the government and the Bank of England to a non‑inflationary path. If that commitment were undermined—whether by outright suspension or by surreptitious over‑issue of notes—the entire system of long‑term contracting, domestic saving, and foreign investment could be destabilized.[^2][^11][^6][^10]

In this vision, discretionary management of an inconvertible paper by the same institution that stood to profit from over‑issue was a fundamental conflict of interest. The problem required an institutional solution: a structure in which the issue of paper claims to money was rigidly linked to gold holdings, so that neither the Bank nor the government could expand the currency without acquiring additional bullion.[^9][^8]


## 2. The Strict Quantity Theory’s Empirical Case, 1820–1840

### 2.1 Ricardo’s monetary framework and the bullion evidence

Ricardo’s quantity‑theoretic framework, developed in *The High Price of Bullion* and carried into *Proposals* and *Principles*, is central to the Currency School’s empirical case. Building on a labor‑theory account of the long‑run value of gold, Ricardo assumed that, for a given real demand for transactions, the value of money varied inversely with its quantity. Under a metallic standard, arbitrage via imports and exports of bullion would automatically adjust the stock of money: excess gold would flow out when prices were too high, and inflows would accompany an undervalued domestic currency.[^7][^2][^8][^9]

The Restriction period provided, in his eyes, a natural experiment: with convertibility suspended, the metallic feedback mechanism was disabled, and the quantity of notes could increase without immediate loss of bullion. The observed combination of a rising market price of gold over the mint price and a depreciating exchange was evidence that the paper pound had fallen in value due to over‑issue. Ricardo’s conclusion—that “a currency, to be perfect, should be absolutely invariable in value” and that inconvertible notes had plainly failed this test—became a canonical Currency School premise.[^3][^8][^9]

### 2.2 Overstone, Torrens, and the codification of the currency principle

Overstone and Torrens transformed Ricardo’s largely theoretical argument into a policy program for the 1840s. Their “currency principle” held that the paper circulation must be made to behave “precisely as a metallic circulation would have done,” which, in practice, meant that the stock of notes should contract when gold left the country and expand only when gold flowed in.[^5][^2][^6][^10][^12]

Empirically, they pointed to several stylized facts from the post‑1821 period:

- When the Bank of England allowed its note circulation to expand without regard to its bullion reserve, the exchange tended to weaken and the bullion reserve to fall, presaging potential crises.
- When the Bank tightened—whether deliberately or as a consequence of gold outflows—prices and activity slowed, but the exchange improved and the bullion reserve recovered.
- Over longer spans, they read the relative stability of the price level under the restored gold standard as vindication of the link between gold, note circulation, and prices.[^10][^12]

On this reading, episodes of strain in the 1820s and 1830s were less indictments of the gold standard than of the Bank’s failure to follow the currency principle systematically. The remedy, therefore, was to legislate a rigid linkage between notes and bullion.

### 2.3 Statistical and historical evidence in the debates

By the 1840s, the parliamentary debates over the Bank Charter Act saw Currency School advocates present tables of Bank note circulation, bullion reserves, exchange rates, and price indices (often based on Tooke’s price histories, though Tooke himself opposed their conclusions) to argue that deviations from the metallic rule were associated with monetary instability. In Hansard’s record of the 1844 debates, Peel and his Currency School allies repeatedly emphasized that under a purely metallic system the entire circulation would automatically adjust to gold flows, whereas under the existing Bank regime paper could temporarily take the place of gold, masking and amplifying external imbalances.[^18][^19]

Their empirical case, in short, was that the combination of the Restriction experience and subsequent episodes of reserve loss demonstrated a systematic relationship between note issue, bullion reserves, and prices, and that the only reliable way to preserve long‑run price stability was to bind note issue mechanically to gold.


## 3. What the Currency School Model Got Right

### 3.1 Convertibility as a credibility device

From a modern standpoint, one of the Currency School’s strongest insights is the recognition that convertibility rules can serve as credibility mechanisms for price stability. By committing the Bank of England to redeem notes in gold at a fixed parity, Parliament constrained the ultimate inflationary options of both the Bank and the Treasury. This institutional constraint effectively imported the long‑run stability properties of a commodity standard into a mixed metallic‑paper system.[^11][^10]

Goodhart’s account of central‑bank evolution emphasizes that commitment to convertibility—and the associated willingness to adjust interest rates and credit conditions to defend the parity—served as the central objective of early central banks. In this respect, the Currency School correctly identified that monetary institutions needed a clear rule anchored in an external standard to prevent political manipulation of the money supply.[^20][^10]

### 3.2 The focus on the monetary base and long‑run price stability

The Currency School’s insistence that control over the monetary base—here, the sum of bullion and notes—was key to long‑run price stability anticipated later quantity‑theory and monetarist perspectives. Anna Schwartz’s survey of the Currency and Banking School debate notes that Currency theorists were early exponents of a rule‑based approach to monetary policy, arguing that if the aggregate quantity of money were kept aligned with the “needs of trade” as constrained by gold, inflation and deflation would be minimized over the cycle.[^21][^22][^23][^24][^12]

Empirically, they took the relatively modest secular movements in British prices after the resumption of convertibility in 1821—especially compared to the volatile Restriction period—as evidence that a gold‑anchored base produced a more stable price level. While modern data revisions complicate this narrative, there is a genuine contrast between the extreme wartime inflation under inconvertibility and the more contained price movements under the restored gold standard.[^11][^10]

### 3.3 The institutional separation of issue and banking

The Bank Charter Act’s separation of the Bank of England into an Issue Department, holding bullion and authorized to issue a fixed fiduciary note component, and a Banking Department, conducting ordinary discount and deposit business, embodied the Currency School’s conviction that note issue should be divorced from ordinary banking operations. This structural move correctly identified that the power to create high‑powered money carried different systemic implications than the provision of credit per se.[^25][^26][^6]

Although subsequent practice eroded the rigidity of the separation, the idea that the central bank’s balance sheet carried public responsibilities distinct from those of commercial banks foreshadowed later understandings of central‑bank independence and balance‑sheet management.[^27][^20]


## 4. Where the Model Failed and How This Became Apparent

### 4.1 Deposit money and the limits of note control

The Achilles’ heel of the Currency School model, from the standpoint of later criticism, was its neglect of bank deposits and the broader credit system as sources of monetary expansion. Even some contemporaries recognized that controlling the quantity of notes alone did not control the totality of purchasing power. William Hawes, in the 1844 Hansard debates, argued that the evidence did not justify the proposed interference with note‑issuing banks and that the Act did not address the expansion of credit through deposits and bills.[^13][^21][^18]

James William Gilbart, an influential practitioner and theorist of joint‑stock banking, likewise emphasized that banks extended credit and created purchasing power through book entries in deposit accounts, not merely through note issue. If deposits could expand independently of the note circulation, then a legal ceiling on notes did not guarantee that the effective money supply—notes plus deposits—would track gold flows as the Currency School intended.[^28]

In more analytical terms, the Currency principle conflated control of the monetary base with control of broader monetary aggregates in an environment where fractional‑reserve banks could pyramid deposits on a limited note and bullion base. This was the core of the Banking School’s critique, and subsequent crises appeared to confirm it.[^16][^21]

### 4.2 The crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866 and suspensions of the Act

The financial crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866 exposed the fragility of an inflexible note‑issue rule in the face of sudden surges in the demand for liquidity. In each case, heavy demands on the Bank of England’s reserves and acute pressures in the money market brought the Banking Department close to exhaustion of its legal note‑issuing capacity under the Act.[^20][^12][^16]

In 1847, as in the later crises, the government ultimately authorized a suspension of the Bank Charter Act’s note‑issue limitations, allowing the Bank to issue notes beyond the statutory limit in order to stem the panic. The very possibility—and repeated use—of such suspensions revealed a deep inconsistency: the Currency School had insisted during the 1844 debates that any contingency clause would render the rule “a nullity,” yet practical necessity forced just such ad hoc relaxations.[^29][^19]

By 1866, the collapse of Overend, Gurney and Company and the ensuing panic made it unmistakable that systemic crises could arise even under a regime that had maintained convertibility and, in broad terms, price‑level stability. The problem was not primarily one of long‑run inflation, but of short‑run liquidity shortages and contagious runs in a credit system dominated by deposits and bills.[^14][^15]

### 4.3 Bagehot’s *Lombard Street* as implicit concession

Walter Bagehot’s *Lombard Street* (1873) is often read as the final intellectual step away from the pure Currency principle toward a lender‑of‑last‑resort conception of central banking. Bagehot accepted the gold standard and the importance of maintaining convertibility, but he argued that given the historical evolution of the British banking system—with a single dominant reserve‑holding institution—the Bank of England had an unavoidable responsibility to support the market in crises.[^30][^15][^14]

Bagehot’s famous rule—that in a panic the central bank should lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral—was in tension with a rigid note‑issue rule that allowed no expansion in response to an extraordinary increase in the demand for base money. Implicitly, *Lombard Street* conceded that discretionary interventions were necessary to prevent solvent banks from failing due to illiquidity, even if such interventions temporarily violated the strict currency principle.[^14][^29]

From a Currency School standpoint, this was a retreat forced by circumstances: the ideal of a self‑regulating currency had run up against the realities of a complex credit system, and the Bank’s micro‑prudential responsibilities as “bankers’ bank” could not be reconciled with an inflexible rule.[^20][^10]


## 5. Steelman Conclusion: What They Got Right, and Where the Case Fails

### 5.1 Enduring insights a fair‑minded modern reader must concede

A fair reconstruction of the Currency School from 1810–1850 must credit them with several enduring insights:

1. **The dangers of unconstrained inconvertible paper.** The Restriction period and the assignat collapse did demonstrate that, absent an external constraint, the combination of fiscal pressure and discretionary note issue can produce substantial inflation and exchange‑rate instability. The Currency School was right to insist that such a regime is inherently vulnerable to abuse.[^1][^4][^3]

2. **Convertibility and rules as commitment devices.** Their insistence on convertibility at a fixed gold parity, and on binding rules for note issue, anticipated later understandings of the role of rules in anchoring expectations and constraining time‑inconsistent policymakers. In this respect, they were early advocates of what would later become the rule‑based monetary policy tradition.[^2][^12][^10]

3. **The centrality of the monetary base.** By focusing attention on the relationship between the monetary base (gold plus notes) and the price level, they foreshadowed monetarist concerns with base control and the long‑run neutrality of money. Their reading of the post‑1821 experience as relatively more stable than the Restriction period captures a genuine difference in regime performance.[^22][^23][^24]

4. **The need to distinguish currency from credit.** Even if their institutional solution was flawed, their intuition that the power to issue currency differed in kind from ordinary credit intermediation laid groundwork for later distinctions between central banks and commercial banks, and between high‑powered money and bank liabilities.[^26][^6][^20]

In short, the Currency School correctly identified that monetary institutions and legal constraints on base money issuance matter profoundly for inflation, contracts, and the credibility of the state’s financial commitments.

### 5.2 The precise point of failure

Where, then, did their case fail? From a steelman perspective, the failure is not in their diagnosis of the Restriction‑era problems or in their belief that rule‑based constraints on the monetary authority are desirable. It lies, rather, in a crucial mis‑specification of the monetary mechanism and an underestimation of the lender‑of‑last‑resort problem.

1. **Mis‑specification of the money supply process.** By equating control over the note issue with control over the money supply, the Currency School ignored the capacity of banks to create deposit money and credit on top of a given base. This oversight meant that their preferred rule, even if perfectly enforced, could not guarantee that the aggregate of means of payment would mimic a pure metallic system.[^13][^21][^28]

2. **Neglect of liquidity crises and systemic risk.** Their focus on preventing long‑run inflation left them ill‑equipped to deal with short‑run liquidity crises in an interconnected banking system. The crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866 showed that severe panics could occur without prior over‑issue of notes relative to gold, and that in such situations strict adherence to the Act endangered solvent institutions and, by extension, convertibility itself.[^19][^29][^10][^20]

3. **Inconsistency between rule and practice.** The repeated suspension of the Bank Charter Act’s provisions under government letter revealed an inherent inconsistency between the Currency School’s insistence on an inviolable rule and the political‑economic reality that no government would allow the banking system to collapse when a temporary relaxation of the rule could avert disaster.[^29][^16]

4. **Failure to integrate Thornton’s insights.** Henry Thornton’s earlier analysis had combined a strong commitment to convertibility with an explicit lender‑of‑last‑resort role for the Bank of England in crises, recognizing that in a panic the Bank might need to expand its balance sheet to satisfy an extraordinary demand for liquidity while still preserving the long‑run gold commitment. The Currency School effectively adopted Thornton’s concern for convertibility but not his nuanced view of crisis management.[^31][^4][^3]

Bagehot’s *Lombard Street* can therefore be read as a partial reconciliation: it accepts the Currency School’s commitment to convertibility and price‑level stability, but insists—on Thornton’s precedent—that these goals cannot be achieved without discretionary lending in crises.[^15][^30][^14]

### 5.3 A balanced assessment

From the vantage point of modern monetary economics and central‑bank history, the Currency School appears as a necessary but incomplete stage in the evolution of monetary institutions. Their program crystallized the idea that the creation of base money must be constrained by credible rules and that the long‑run behavior of the price level is a monetary phenomenon. They were, however, overly confident that a simple mechanical linkage between notes and bullion could, by itself, deliver both price stability and financial stability.[^12][^10][^11]

The development of deposit banking, the experience of mid‑nineteenth‑century crises, and the subsequent emergence of lender‑of‑last‑resort doctrine revealed that monetary rules cannot be specified solely in terms of the note issue, and that central banks have dual responsibilities: to maintain the nominal anchor (whether gold or, later, low inflation) and to preserve systemic stability through crisis lending.[^10][^14][^20]

A fair‑minded modern reader, steelmanning the Currency School, would conclude that they were fundamentally right about the dangers of unconstrained paper and the need for a rule‑based anchor, but that their particular rule—“make paper behave as if it were gold by regulating only notes”—failed to account for the full architecture of the credit system. The resulting institutional design proved too rigid in crises and too narrow in its conception of money, paving the way for the more flexible, but still rule‑constrained, central‑banking regimes that followed.[^16][^11][^10]
