# Human Anchor Cases for Crypto Collapse and 2021–22 Inflation

## Overview

This report identifies concrete human “anchor” stories for two themes in macro-finance and political economy writing:

- An individual FTX customer whose loss in the November 2022 crypto collapse is well-documented with name, timing, approximate loss, and personal detail.
- Households and small-business operators whose 2021–22 experience illustrates inflation as a collision between money creation and real capacity limits, including real wages lagging prices and supply-chain bottlenecks transmitting costs.

All examples are drawn from mainstream reporting and primary documents where possible and are suitable to be novelized at the Backwell / Todd / Hagedorn / McKenzie standard: named protagonists, dated events, specific financial magnitudes, and vivid human details.[^1][^2][^3]

***

## R9: Crypto-Collapse Human Anchor – FTX Customer in November 2022

### Candidate: Terri Smith, FTX Customer and Architect near Seattle

NPR and related public radio transcripts covered the collapse of FTX by profiling everyday investors, including Terri Smith, an architect in the Seattle area who held a substantial part of her savings on the platform.[^4][^5][^1]

Key factual elements:

- **Name and identity:** Terri Smith is described as an architect living in the Seattle area, semi‑retired and working part‑time. This gives you class position, geography, and profession.[^6]
- **Timing and event:** She became more serious about crypto investing in summer 2021, allocating a significant slice of her portfolio to digital assets held across exchanges, with a large portion ultimately on FTX.[^6]
- **Collapse date:** FTX filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on November 11, 2022, after a rapid liquidity crisis and run on customer deposits.[^7][^8]
- **Specific financial loss:** Smith told NPR she “may have lost about 30,000 dollars” on FTX and described it as “a huge chunk of money” for her.[^5][^1]
- **Emotional and moral valence:** She characterized the experience as feeling like “someone stealing your money” and “like theft,” language that foregrounds betrayal rather than market risk.[^5]

### Narrative-Ready Details for a Scene

From the reporting you can reconstruct a tight, scene‑driven arc for November 2022:

- **Pre‑collapse setup (summer 2021 – mid‑2022):**
  - Smith “got serious” about crypto in mid‑2021, studying the technology and distributing funds across several exchanges; FTX’s interface and speed made it her favored venue.[^6]
  - At one point she saw her crypto investments double in roughly five months, giving her the sense of participating in a transformative new asset class rather than a casino.[^6]

- **Onset of crisis (early November 2022):**
  - As reports emerged that FTX might be insolvent, customers rushed to withdraw funds; FTX responded by freezing accounts, and then rapidly filed for bankruptcy.[^8][^1][^5]
  - Smith, who was out of town when she first heard that FTX was halting some withdrawals, managed to pull out part of her assets but remained stuck with roughly 30,000 dollars trapped on the exchange.[^5][^6]

- **Aftermath and loss frame:**
  - NPR’s account emphasizes that Smith viewed this 30,000‑dollar exposure as a “huge chunk of money,” not a marginal speculative flutter.[^1]
  - Her quote “I was devastated really” and her repeated likening of the episode to theft encapsulate a shift from techno‑optimism to moral injury.[^1][^5]

### How This Meets Your Standard

- **Named individual:** Terri Smith, architect in the Seattle area.[^5][^6]
- **Dated event:** FTX’s collapse and bankruptcy in November 2022, with accounts freezing during the second week of that month.[^7][^8]
- **Specific financial loss:** Approximately 30,000 dollars trapped on FTX, described as a large share of her savings.[^1][^5]
- **Human detail:** Semi‑retired architect, living near Seattle, who fell in love with the technology, watched early gains double, tried to diversify across exchanges, was traveling when the collapse hit, and now talks about the experience explicitly as theft rather than bad luck.[^6][^5]

This story can be cross‑cut with more extreme but anonymized accounts, such as FTX customers in Spain who reported losing 12,000 euros, having “a large part” of their savings in FTX, working on minimum wage, supporting a child as a separated parent, and “thinking about suicide” after the bankruptcy; however those individuals remain unnamed in reporting and so are better used as atmospheric color than primary anchors.[^7]

## R11: 2021–22 Inflation Human Anchors

For the inflation case you need characters showing (a) household budgets broken by rising prices despite nominal wage gains, and (b) operators on the supply side of capacity bottlenecks who watch their costs and lead times explode. The best‑documented examples from 2021–22 fit into three buckets: a front‑desk worker whose raise is erased by higher living costs, a small game publisher snarled in port congestion and freight price shocks, and households whose grocery, fuel, and rent bills jump faster than their income.

### Household Wage vs. Inflation: Ty Stehlik in Milwaukee

The Washington Post’s January 22, 2022 story, “That raise meant nothing: Inflation is wiping out pay increases for most Americans,” profiles Ty Stehlik, a front‑desk worker at a Milwaukee hotel.[^2][^9]

Key factual elements:

- **Name and job:** Ty Stehlik works the front desk at a hotel in Milwaukee and is described as 23 years old in early 2022.[^9]
- **Nominal wage gain:** After pressing for a raise throughout the pandemic, Stehlik received an extra 1 dollar per hour in fall 2021, bringing their wage to 15 dollars per hour – roughly a 7 percent nominal increase.[^9]
- **Inflation context:** Average hourly wages rose 4.7 percent in 2021, but after adjusting for inflation, real wages fell 2.4 percent on average for all workers, according to Labor Department data cited in the same article.[^2][^9]
- **Household budget stress:** Stehlik reports that higher prices for rent and food “completely negated” the raise and says, “That raise meant nothing.” They still rely on family support to cover rent and groceries.[^9]
- **Debt and precarity:** The article notes that Stehlik has student loans, their roommate (who also works at the hotel) has medical debt, and many coworkers juggle two or three jobs yet still struggle to make ends meet.[^9]

Narratively, this gives you a tight micro‑case of nominal wages rising into a macro backdrop where the Consumer Price Index is increasing at its fastest pace since the early 1980s: annual CPI inflation reached 6.8 percent in November 2021 and 7 percent in December 2021, far outstripping typical wage gains.[^10][^11]

### Small Business on the Supply‑Side Bottleneck: John Nephew and Atlas Games

NPR’s June 21, 2021 piece “Cargo Is Piling Up Everywhere, And It’s Making Inflation Worse” uses a small game publisher, Atlas Games of Duluth, Minnesota, as a concrete example of supply‑chain congestion feeding into inflation.[^12][^3]

Key factual elements:

- **Business and protagonist:** Atlas Games is a small publisher of board and card games based in Duluth, Minnesota, founded and run by John Nephew.[^13][^14][^15]
- **Order timing and expectations:** In December (2020), Nephew ordered a 40‑foot shipping container of games from a manufacturer in Shanghai, expecting delivery in about six weeks – a standard just‑in‑time cadence for his business.[^3]
- **Actual lead time:** Due to port congestion and overwhelmed terminals on the U.S. West Coast, the shipment took nearly six months to arrive, an order‑of‑magnitude increase in lead time that left him short of inventory.[^12][^3]
- **Cost shock:** The final shipping cost for the container was around 12,000 dollars, at least 50 percent more than Atlas Games had budgeted when the order was placed.[^3]
- **Macro context:** The Port of Los Angeles recorded its busiest month ever in May 2021, handling more than 1 million containers, over half loaded with imports from Asia. Port officials described terminals “overwhelmed” by volume, with vessels waiting up to five days just to dock and an additional 10 days for containers to be put on trains.[^12]

This gives you a small‑business owner whose cost structure and delivery promises are overrun by global capacity constraints on ships, terminals, trucks, warehouses, and railroads – a direct channel from macro logistics bottlenecks into retail prices and availability.[^16][^12]

### Households Squeezed by Grocery, Fuel, and Rent Prices

To round out the inflation case, mainstream coverage in 2022 chronicles how routine living costs jumped in a way that forced households to change behavior.

The New York Times reported in June 2022 that overall inflation reached 8.6 percent year‑over‑year, a four‑decade high, with grocery prices up 12 percent compared to the prior year. Within that:[^17]

- The typical price of a dozen large eggs rose from about 1.60 dollars to 2.80 dollars over one year, a roughly 75 percent increase.[^17]
- Ground beef prices rose 13 percent per pound, and a gallon of whole milk cost about 20 percent more than a year earlier.[^17]
- Median rent in the U.S. reached nearly 1,850 dollars per month in May 2022, about 26 percent higher than in 2019.[^17]

While the article focuses on aggregate trends rather than a single named family, it emphasizes that Americans were “balking at the rising cost of everything,” cutting back on discretionary purchases, trading down in grocery choices, and delaying big‑ticket spending as food, fuel, and rent crowded out the rest of their budgets.[^17]

You can juxtapose these aggregate price moves with Stehlik’s story: a 7 percent nominal pay bump at the hotel front desk is swallowed by double‑digit food inflation, steep rent increases, and higher gasoline costs, leaving real consumption possibilities lower than before the raise.[^9][^17]

### Macro backdrop and policy framing

The inflation anchor cases sit against a macro backdrop documented by repeated CPI releases:

- CPI inflation reached 6.8 percent year‑over‑year in November 2021 and 7 percent in December 2021, the fastest pace since 1982.[^11][^10]
- Federal Reserve officials initially characterized the spike as transitory but by late 2021 were acknowledging that price increases had lasted longer than expected and were spreading beyond the sectors directly affected by the pandemic.[^10][^11]
- Central bank commentary and coverage in outlets like NPR tied a significant share of the price surge to “bottleneck effects” – record import volumes overwhelming ports, rail yards, and warehouses, leading to long delays and higher freight costs that businesses either absorbed or passed on.[^18][^16][^12]

These anchors therefore allow the narrative to show how money‑and‑credit expansion during the pandemic collided with real‑world capacity limits in logistics, housing, and energy, producing a period where raises did not feel like progress and ordinary business models were broken by shipping costs and unpredictability.
