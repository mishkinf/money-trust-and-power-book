## Part 1 – Trade Architecture, Keen, Keynes–Bancor, Modern Revival (Scaffold)
### 1.1 Keynes’s 1944 Bancor Proposal — Core Primary Sources
Key primary and near‑primary materials already identified:

- **Keynes, J. M., “Proposals for an International Clearing Union”**, in *The Collected Writings of John Maynard Keynes*, vol. XXV: *Activities 1940–1944: Shaping the Post‑War World: The Clearing Union* (ed. D. Moggridge). This is the main primary text; several papers summarize its key passages and provide page references.[^1][^2][^3]
- IMF archival republication of Keynes’s draft plan: “The Keynes Plan” in an IMF volume on the origins of the IMF reproduces the February 1942 draft and discusses its evolution.[^2]
- Secondary summaries of the **surplus‑charge mechanism** and quota rules, which quote directly from Keynes:
  - CEPR/Academic summaries of the proposal note that any country with an “excess surplus or debit” of more than one‑quarter of its Clearing Union quota would be charged 1 percent per year, with an additional 1 percent on balances above half the quota, and escalating pressure when credits or debits exceeded three‑quarters and then the full quota.[^4]
  - Popularizations accurately reflecting the technical rule: e.g., Time / Monbiot style summaries emphasize that “Any country with a bancor credit balance that was more than half the size of its overdraft facility would be charged interest, at a rate of 10% … If, by the end of the year, its credit balance exceeded the total value of its permitted overdraft, the surplus would be confiscated,” stressing the *use‑it‑or‑lose‑it* pressure on surplus countries.[^5][^6]
- **Skidelsky, Robert, “Keynes, Global Imbalances, and International Monetary Reform Today”** (2010 lecture/article) gives a concise explanation of the clearing‑account mechanics, quotas set at half of average pre‑war trade, and the objective of preventing asymmetric adjustment that forces only deficit countries to contract.[^7]

Key points to be developed in full memo text (with direct Keynes quotations added at writing stage):

- **Institutional design**: The International Clearing Union (ICU) as a supranational bank clearing all residual international transactions via bancor‑denominated accounts held by member central banks; bancor fixed to gold but not convertible, with the long‑term aim of demonetizing gold and making bancor the final reserve asset.[^8][^7]
- **Quotas and overdrafts**: Each member assigned an “index quota” equal to roughly half the average value of its external trade over the previous five years; permitted overdraft (deficit) and credit (surplus) positions defined as percentages of that quota, with automatic creation and extinction of bancor deposits as deficits and surpluses arise and are settled.[^4][^7]
- **Symmetric pressure**: Both persistent deficit and persistent surplus positions attracted charges and policy obligations; deficit countries faced interest charges, exchange‑rate adjustment, and capital controls, but surplus countries also faced interest on excessive credits, pressure to revalue, liberalize imports and capital exports, and ultimately confiscation of extreme surpluses.[^6][^7][^4]
- **Asymmetric Bretton Woods outcome**: Secondary literature (Steil, Helleiner, Mehrling) emphasizes that Harry Dexter White’s plan for a Stabilization Fund prevailed, creating the IMF and World Bank structure without Keynes’s strong obligations on creditor countries, and with the dollar (convertible into gold at 35 per ounce) at the center instead of a neutral unit of account.[^9][^10][^11]

Contested vs. settled points to flag later:

- Degree to which Keynes’s scheme was primarily about domestic policy autonomy vs. global rebalancing.
- Whether the surplus‑tax/“confiscation” provisions would have been politically sustainable.
- How directly the plan can be transplanted into today’s multipolar, largely floating‑rate system.
### 1.2 Steve Keen on Trade, Private Debt, and MMT (Discovery Notes)
Identified source types so far:

- **Books** (to be consulted directly for precise quotations and page references):
  - *Debunking Economics* (rev. and expanded ed., 2011) — general critique of neoclassical trade theory and treatment of money and debt; needs to be searched for explicit discussion of trade deficits and external imbalances.[^12][^13]
  - *Can We Avoid Another Financial Crisis?* (2017) — focuses on private‑debt‑driven crises; interviews and secondary discussions emphasize “walking dead of debt,” the role of rapidly growing private credit in causing crises, and the notion that all modern financial crises are driven by excessive private debt.[^14][^15]
  - *The New Economics: A Manifesto* (2022) — manifesto for monetary, ecological and complexity‑based macro; secondary reviews note its emphasis on endogenous money and debt, but the trade‑architecture content must still be extracted.[^16][^17][^18]
- **Keen interviews and transcripts**:
  - MacroVoices interview “The Real Risk is in Private Credit” (2018) where Keen stresses that “it’s not sovereign debt that matters … it’s private debt” and that all crises are caused by excessive private debt; he notes that countries running trade deficits tend to develop private‑debt problems as credit growth supports demand in deficit economies.[^15]
  - Debunking Economics podcast episodes on trade deficits (“Is a trade deficit a bad thing?”, “Surviving with a negative trade balance”) where he argues the importance of keeping an economy in surplus and pushes back on views that trade deficits are straightforwardly benign.[^19][^20]
- **Direct critique of MMT on trade**:
  - Blog and debate references indicate Keen has criticized the MMT slogan “exports are a cost, imports are a benefit” when interpreted as policy advice to pursue trade deficits, arguing this ignores monetary/credit dynamics and the risk of private‑debt buildups that accompany persistent deficits.[^21][^22]

Hypothesized synthesis to be confirmed and quoted directly from Keen texts:

- Keen accepts core MMT claims about sovereign‑currency governments not being financially constrained in their own currency, but insists that **private debt dynamics and balance‑of‑payments structures** impose real constraints that MMT understates.
- On trade, Keen appears to argue that:
  - While imports can be real benefits, persistent deficits financed by private borrowing create vulnerability; the accumulation and eventual deleveraging of private debt in deficit countries is macro‑destabilizing.
  - Trade‑surplus strategies (e.g., Germany, China) increase money‑supply growth via net exports, enabling higher investment and demand, which MMT underplays when it emphasizes the utility of deficits for the deficit nation.[^23][^15]
  - Therefore, there is a genuine architecture problem at the global level, not just a domestic policy question; he is closer to Keynes/Triffin on this than to Mosler.

### 1.3 Mosler, Kelton, Wray, Tcherneva, Mitchell on Trade
Key canonical statements already located:

- **Warren Mosler**
  - Repeated, canonical formulation: “Exports are real costs, imports are real benefits. US real wealth goes up with real domestic output and real imports, down with real exports.”[^24][^25][^26]
  - He adds that “a trade deficit gives that nation more fiscal space” and is “a productivity increase,” provided fiscal policy maintains full employment.[^24]
  - In the context of Trump and China, Mosler writes: “Remind him exports = real costs, imports = real benefits, it’s about real terms of trade, + jobs are about fiscal,” stressing that job loss is a fiscal‑policy failure rather than a trade‑deficit problem.[^24]
- **L. Randall Wray**
  - In an NEP post “MMT and external constraints,” Wray states explicitly: “MMT emphasizes that in ‘real’ terms, imports are a benefit and exports are a cost. Floating the currency and relaxing capital controls allows a nation to enjoy more ‘benefits’ (imports) and fewer ‘costs’ (exports).”[^27]
  - He presents the sectoral balances identity and argues that a current‑account deficit corresponds to rest‑of‑world accumulation of financial claims in the importer’s currency; this is sustainable for monetarily sovereign nations so long as policy keeps domestic demand at full employment and the exchange rate adjusts.[^28][^27]
  - In lecture materials, Wray emphasizes that a trade deficit “results from [rest of world] desire to net save dollar‑denominated assets,” that the US trade deficit “finances ROW saving,” and that net imports can be associated with rising private indebtedness if financed by private borrowing rather than fiscal policy, in which case the deficit can be a burden through rising consumer debt.[^28]
- **Stephanie Kelton**
  - In secondary summaries of *The Deficit Myth* chapter on trade, Kelton is reported as arguing that trade deficits should be understood as a “stuff surplus”: “China gets US Treasury bonds, and the US gets Apple computers and other ‘stuff’,” and that imports represent real benefits while exports are real costs.[^29][^30]
  - The book stresses that fears about dependence on overseas creditors are misplaced for a sovereign‑currency issuer; the concern should be about job dislocation and distribution, not solvency.[^30]
  - Direct quotations on trade deficits from the book itself still need to be pulled from the primary text for accuracy.
- **Bill Mitchell**
  - In a 2019 blog essay rebutting claims of an “internal MMT rift on trade,” Mitchell writes: “A trade deficit is a sign that the real terms of trade are working in favour of the deficit nation. That is standard MMT and, in my view, unassailable,” provided the country maintains full employment and manages exchange‑rate and external constraints.[^31]
  - He acknowledges that for nations with limited productive capacity and high import dependence, especially on essentials like fuel and food, external deficits can deplete foreign‑exchange reserves and force painful adjustment; in such cases urgent measures (renegotiation, donor assistance, or default) may be required.[^31]
  - This is a key bridge to the author’s concern about architecture and Triffin‑style asymmetries.
- **Pavlina Tcherneva**
  - Tcherneva’s work on the Job Guarantee mostly focuses on domestic stabilization and price anchors; she argues that a Job Guarantee can provide a nominal anchor and stabilize inflation without relying on unemployment.[^32][^33]
  - In the context of open economies, she and other MMT authors note that a Job Guarantee can help manage demand and labor‑market slack, but she does not centrally address global reserve architecture or Triffin‑type concerns in the core JG literature.

Gaps and contested points that will have to be surfaced clearly in the full memo:

- Canonical MMT is explicit about real‑terms‑of‑trade logic and about monetary sovereignty under floating exchange rates, but comparatively **quiet on global‑order stability questions** and on the asymmetric burdens of the dollar standard.
- Wray, Mitchell, and periphery‑MMT critiques (e.g., Bonizzi et al.; Brazilian and Turkish authors) have increasingly emphasized **external constraints for developing countries**, but this is more about policy space for periphery states than about reforming the global reserve architecture itself.[^34][^35][^36]
- Mosler’s writing and blogging largely treat reserve‑currency status as operationally incidental (the same logic applies to any sovereign issuer), rather than as a structural source of Triffin‑style tension; this asymmetry is where the author’s divergence will sit and needs to be made explicit by juxtaposing Mosler/Wray/Kelton with Keen/Keynes/Stiglitz.
### 1.4 Modern Bancor‑Revival and Adjacent Architecture Proposals
Key clusters of post‑2008 literature located so far:

- **Stiglitz, Greenwald, and the Stiglitz Commission**
  - Stiglitz and Greenwald’s paper “Towards a New Global Reserve System” in *Journal of Globalization and Development* (2010) surveys the costs of the current dollar‑based system: excess reserve accumulation, depressed global aggregate demand, and vulnerability to sudden stops.[^37][^38]
  - They argue that the demand for reserves has contributed to global demand weakness and call for a new global reserve system, with expanded use of IMF Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) as a candidate; they explicitly reference Triffin and earlier advocacy for a non‑national reserve asset.[^38]
  - Related policy work and the UN Commission of Experts on Reforms of the International Monetary and Financial System (Stiglitz Commission) echo this, advocating larger, more regular SDR allocations and movement toward a “global reserve currency.”[^37]
- **Zhou Xiaochuan (PBoC) 2009 speech**
  - In “Reform the International Monetary System,” Zhou writes that the “acceptance of credit-based national currencies as major international reserve currencies … is a rare special case in history” and calls for “an international reserve currency that is disconnected from individual nations and is able to remain stable in the long run,” explicitly invoking the idea of a “super-sovereign reserve currency.”[^39][^40]
  - He suggests that SDRs could evolve into such a currency, with rule‑based issuance and a mandate to address global imbalances, thereby reducing the systemic risks of the current dollar‑based order.[^40][^41]
- **Mehrling, “Beyond Bancor” and Helleiner’s reinterpretation of Bretton Woods**
  - Mehrling’s “Beyond Bancor” paper reviews Keynes’s plan and subsequent debates, arguing that modern key‑currency and dealer‑of‑last‑resort arrangements have, in practice, implemented a different sort of hierarchy; he suggests that proposals for global reserve reform must reckon with the reality of the dollar‑centered system’s dealer structure.[^10][^8]
  - Helleiner’s *Forgotten Foundations of Bretton Woods* emphasizes the North–South dialogues and development concerns at Bretton Woods; later commentary notes that reform debates today reprise many of Keynes’s concerns about reconciling liberal order with development aspirations.[^42]
- **SDR and multipolar reserve debates**
  - IMF Fiscal Monitor and WEO chapters, and policy briefs around the 2021 SDR allocation of about 650 billion, discuss SDRs as a way to ease global liquidity shortages without relying solely on the dollar; some authors explicitly frame this as a step toward a more “Bancor‑like” system, while others present it as a limited technical fix.[^41][^37]
- **Varoufakis and “Plan B” / parallel payments**
  - Varoufakis’s “Plan B” for Greece involved creating a parallel payments system within the euro area and, if necessary, re‑denominating balances; some commentators connect this to ideas of a euro‑area internal clearing mechanism, but Varoufakis’s published work focuses more on political strategy and democratic control than on a fully fledged global clearing‑union design.[^43][^44]
- **Hans‑Werner Sinn on TARGET2**
  - Sinn’s work on TARGET2 imbalances argues that the Eurosystem’s internal settlement mechanism has effectively created large claims of northern central banks on southern central banks, analogous to balance‑of‑payments financing.[^45][^46]
  - Critics warn that proposals to cap TARGET2 balances would undermine the euro’s nature as a single currency and re‑introduce something like a gold‑standard constraint; nonetheless, the debate shows that a regional quasi‑clearing‑union with asymmetric adjustment burdens can generate political tension very similar to the global Triffin problem.[^46]

- Systematic classification of proposals into:
  - **True Bancor revivals** (super‑sovereign unit of account, explicit symmetric charges on surpluses and deficits, automatic clearing via a global institution).
  - **SDR‑based incremental reforms** (expanded SDR use, but no full symmetric charge system).
  - **Multipolar and regional systems** (e.g., key‑currency competition, regional reserve pools, or euro‑area internal mechanisms).
- Extracting explicit passages where modern authors reference **Keynes’s Bancor or Triffin** and assess whether the “dilemma” remains live.
### 1.5 Synthesis – Scaffold for 1,000‑Word Section
To be written in full prose later; key argumentative joints identified now:

- **Convergence**:
  - Canonical MMT, Keen, and Keynes/Stiglitz all treat **money as an institutional arrangement**, not a natural commodity standard, and stress real resource constraints over financial ones.[^38][^27][^47]
  - MMT and Keen agree that sovereign‑currency governments are not operationally constrained by foreign creditors in their own currency, and that unemployment is an institutional rather than natural outcome.[^15][^27][^47]
- **Divergence with canonical MMT on trade architecture**:
  - Canonical MMT emphasizes that under floating exchange rates and monetary sovereignty, **trade deficits are real benefits** and do not impose solvency constraints; external imbalances matter mainly through exchange‑rate and distributional channels.[^24][^27][^28]
  - Keen and the Bancor/Triffin revival literature stress that **persistent imbalances have systemic consequences**: they redistribute demand and credit creation globally, create debt overhangs in deficit countries, and, under a dollar standard, load adjustment pressure asymmetrically onto deficit states.[^45][^38][^15]
  - Keynes’s original ICU proposal and modern calls for a super‑sovereign reserve asset aim to **socialize adjustment between surplus and deficit countries**, whereas MMT’s standard treatment largely abstracts from this level of architecture.
- **Defensible authorial position**:
  - “MMT‑adjacent on domestic monetary sovereignty”: accept that within a sovereign‑currency, floating‑rate regime, the binding constraint on public spending is real productive capacity and inflation, not revenue or reserves; unemployment is a policy choice.[^27][^47]
  - “Keen/Keynes‑bancor on global architecture”: insist that, at the level of the global system, the asymmetries of a dollar standard and persistent surplus/deficit positions matter for *order stability*, and that a clearing‑union‑type architecture with symmetric pressure is a more coherent long‑run design.[^40][^7][^38]
  - Explicitly diverge from Mosler’s tendency to treat external deficits as essentially unproblematic so long as domestic policy sustains full employment, by embedding his real‑terms‑of‑trade logic inside a Keynesian recognition of Triffin‑style system risk.

A full 1,000‑word synthesis can be drafted by expanding these bullet points into prose, weaving in direct quotations from Keynes, Mosler, Wray, Kelton, Keen, and Stiglitz/Zhou, and adding discursive notes per the project standard.

## Part 2 – Interest‑Rate Positions within MMT (Scaffold)
### 2.1 Mosler’s Permanent‑ZIRP Argument (Initial Evidence)
Key texts and formulations identified:

- **“The natural rate of interest is zero”**
  - Mosler in *The 7 Deadly Innocent Frauds of Economic Policy* is quoted (secondary but specific) as writing: “There is no such thing as a natural rate of interest other than zero… Government sets the interest rate it pays on its own liabilities. A positive rate of interest is a policy decision, not a market outcome.”[^48][^49]
  - Bill Mitchell’s blog post “The natural rate of interest is zero!” explains and endorses this as a core MMT maxim, arguing that with net government spending and no support rate, competitive interbank dynamics drive the overnight rate to zero, making zero the “natural” outcome in a fiat system absent central‑bank intervention.[^50]
- **Interest payments as “basic income for people who already have money”**
  - In interviews and commentary, Mosler argues that higher interest rates are “a basic income for people who already have money,” i.e., an income subsidy to bondholders and holders of government liabilities, making higher rates fiscally expansionary and regressive.[^51][^52]
- **Rejection of interest‑rate policy as stabilizer**
  - MMT expositions summarize Mosler’s view as: the interest rate should be set at zero permanently, with fiscal policy (including a Job Guarantee) used to manage demand and employment; raising rates is at best ineffective and at worst destabilizing, because it increases government interest payments and can stoke inflation via higher income flows to the private sector.[^53][^54][^47]
  - Technical work with Armstrong on central‑bank interest‑rate policy argues that central banks are price‑setters for the overnight rate, not quantity‑setters of reserves, and that excess reserves without interest would naturally drive rates to zero.[^55]

### 2.2 Wray on Interest Rates
Evidence located so far (pending direct inspection of the book’s rate chapters):

- Wray’s textbook *Modern Money Theory: A Primer on Macroeconomics for Sovereign Monetary Systems* (2nd ed., 2015) is the standard presentation; reviews emphasize that it treats monetary policy as secondary to fiscal policy and skeptical about the efficacy of interest‑rate adjustments as a stabilizer.[^57][^58]
- In “MMT and external constraints” and other NEP posts, Wray argues that the central bank is a price‑setter of the overnight rate, that interest‑rate changes have uncertain and often perverse effects on demand and inflation, and that job‑guarantee‑centered fiscal policy is the primary stabilization tool.[^27][^28]
- MMT critics summarizing Wray and Mosler note that the “corollary proposition” of MMT is that the interest rate should be set at its “natural or normal rate of zero,” with bond operations used only to maintain the overnight rate at that level.[^53]

Hypothesis to be verified in the primary book:

- Wray probably endorses **zero as the preferred benchmark** for the policy rate in the long run, but allows that central banks can and do vary rates, and that inflation targeting with rate changes is not reliable.
- He is likely less absolutist than Mosler in insisting on permanent zero regardless of context, instead emphasizing the primacy of fiscal policy and a Job Guarantee while treating monetary policy as relatively weak.
### 2.3 Kelton on Interest Rates
So far, the evidence is mostly indirect and must be supplemented by direct text from *The Deficit Myth* and later writings:

- Kelton explicitly centers **fiscal policy and real resource constraints**; she emphasizes that inflation, not solvency, is the binding constraint for a sovereign‑currency issuer and criticizes reliance on interest‑rate policy and NAIRU frameworks.[^30][^47][^59]
- Summaries of the book note that she treats cutting interest rates as “ineffective in a slump” because firms will not invest without expected profits, regardless of low rates.[^47][^59]
- Kelton does not appear to make a **strong, canonical claim** that the policy rate must be fixed at zero forever; rather, she downplays its importance relative to targeted fiscal measures and a Job Guarantee.

### 2.4 Mitchell, Tcherneva, Tymoigne and the Bench
Current evidence:

- **Bill Mitchell**
  - In “The interest rate should be set at zero” (2009), Mitchell argues explicitly that “it would be far better for the government to set the short-term interest rate at zero and achieve full employment through appropriate levels of net spending (fiscal deficits),” rejecting monetary policy as an effective counter‑stabilization tool.[^60]
  - He reiterates that only the overnight policy rate is set at zero; risk premia and term spreads remain market‑determined.[^61][^60]
- **Pavlina Tcherneva**
  - Her work emphasizes the Job Guarantee as a price anchor and automatic stabilizer; interest rates appear as a secondary or background variable rather than a primary tool.[^32][^33][^62]
- **Eric Tymoigne**
  - In his Journal of Economic Issues article on Treasury–central bank interrelations, Tymoigne presents taxes and bond issuance as reserve‑draining devices to maintain price and interest‑rate stability, consistent with the MMT view that fiscal operations and interest‑rate setting are about managing reserves, not funding spending.[^63]
  - In later work, he notes that some MMT authors show that a permanent zero rate yields “the highest rate of growth and the lowest rate of inflation,” reinforcing the Mosler/Mitchell position while acknowledging that mainstream fears about zero rates causing instability are empirically unsupported.[^54][^64]
### 2.5 Synthesis – Scaffold for 500‑Word Section
The synthesis to be written later will need to:

- **Map the internal MMT spread**:
  - Strong permanent‑ZIRP camp: Mosler, Mitchell, parts of Tymoigne’s analysis explicitly treat zero as the natural and normatively preferred rate, arguing for fiscal dominance and a Job Guarantee as the stabilizer.[^50][^54][^60]
  - More flexible camp: Wray and Kelton treat interest‑rate policy as weak and potentially counterproductive, but do not always insist on literal permanent zero; they emphasize that the key is using fiscal tools and a Job Guarantee to manage demand and inflation, while recognizing that central banks currently target rates and can adjust them within an inflation‑anchoring framework.[^27][^47][^57]
- **Position the author’s view**:
  - The desired stance—“Fed dual mandate biased toward employment within an inflation‑anchoring frame” with interest rates available but not central—is **MMT‑adjacent** in the sense that it rejects solvency fears and centers real constraints and employment, but does **not** sign onto Mosler/Mitchell’s normative claim that the policy rate should be locked at zero.
  - The author can cite Wray and Kelton as the closest canonical anchors, while explicitly noting that Mosler and Mitchell argue for permanent ZIRP and that the book diverges from this, preferring a reformed dual‑mandate regime with a Job Guarantee and fiscal primacy.[^57][^60][^27]
