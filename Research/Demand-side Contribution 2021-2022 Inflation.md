# Demand-side contribution to 2021–2022 inflation

Task: The manuscript currently attributes ~60% of above-trend 2021–22 inflation to supply-chain disruptions and treats MMT as vindicated. The serious empirical literature is more divided. Produce a fair,
citation-grade account of where the demand-side share is estimated at, by whom, with what method, and what this implies for the productive-capacity framework the book defends.

Address in order:

1. Bernanke and Blanchard, "What Caused the U.S. Pandemic-Era Inflation?" (Brookings/Hutchins Center, 2023; revised 2024). Method, findings, demand-vs-supply decomposition.
2. Adam Shapiro, "How Much Do Supply and Demand Drive Inflation?" (Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Economic Letter 2022-15). Method and findings.
3. Joseph Gagnon (Peterson Institute) on the role of fiscal stimulus in 2021–22 inflation.
4. The IMF *World Economic Outlook* October 2022 chapter on inflation drivers.
5. Olivier Blanchard's pre-COVID warnings (e.g., "In Defense of Concerns Over the \$1.9 Trillion Relief Plan," PIIE, Feb 18 2021) and his post-hoc verdict.
6. Lawrence Summers' contemporaneous warnings (Washington Post op-ed Feb 4 2021) and post-hoc record.
7. The fair verdict: what share of 2021–22 inflation is best attributed to demand vs. supply, and how does this update the productive-capacity framework? (Hint: it doesn't refute it; it sharpens it.)

Required sources as named above, plus FRED data for the inflation series, the BLS PCE breakdown, and any subsequent peer-reviewed update to Bernanke-Blanchard.

Length: 2,000–2,500 words.

A fair reading of the serious empirical work is that 2021–22 U.S. inflation was driven by both adverse supply shocks *and* unusually strong demand, with demand accounting for roughly one‑third to perhaps one‑half of the above‑trend surge depending on method, horizon, and definition of “excess” inflation.  This is compatible with a productive‑capacity framework, but cuts against any claim that the U.S. episode was “mostly supply” in a way that vindicates unconstrained fiscal expansion à la popularized MMT.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8][^9]

## The 2021–22 U.S. inflation episode in data

Headline CPI inflation rose to 9.1 percent year‑over‑year in June 2022, the largest 12‑month increase since 1981.  Headline PCE inflation (the Fed’s preferred gauge) climbed from around 1.4 percent year‑over‑year in January 2021 to about 5.8 percent by December 2021 and roughly 6.8 percent by June 2022, far above the pre‑pandemic PCE inflation rates near 2 percent.[^10][^11][^12][^13][^14]

Goods inflation in the PCE index averaged about 9.2 percent from March 2021 to July 2022 versus 0.4 percent in the two decades before the pandemic, while services PCE inflation averaged 5.2 percent over that period versus 2.5 percent pre‑COVID, indicating that what began as goods‑heavy inflation broadened markedly into services.  This broadening is exactly what one would expect when initial sector‑specific supply shocks are interacting with aggregate demand that has pushed spending above economy‑wide capacity.[^3][^15][^16][^9]

## Bernanke–Blanchard: model and decomposition

### Method and framework

Bernanke and Blanchard specify and estimate a dynamic model of prices, wages, and short‑ and long‑run inflation expectations, with separate shocks from energy prices, food prices, an index of “sectoral shortages,” and labor‑market tightness (measured by the vacancy‑to‑unemployment ratio).  They estimate the model on pre‑COVID data and then feed through the realized shocks of 2020–23 to decompose the contribution of each to inflation over time, allowing for expectations and wage‑setting dynamics.[^17][^18][^19]

The core conceptual distinction in their work is not “supply vs demand” per se but “price shocks given wages” (energy, food, and sector‑specific bottlenecks) vs shocks operating through an overheated labor market and wage growth.  They also treat commodity price shocks as partly reflecting strong aggregate demand but operationalize them as exogenous price shocks in the model’s price equation.[^18][^17]

### Quantitative findings for the U.S.

They conclude that “most of the inflation surge that began in 2021 was the result of shocks to prices given wages,” including sharp increases in commodity prices and sectoral price spikes driven by changes in the composition of demand and constraints on sectoral supply.  Labor‑market tightness made at most a modest contribution to inflation in 2021–22 but has a more persistent effect on wage and price dynamics, so bringing inflation back to target ultimately requires easing labor‑market overheating.[^20][^19][^17][^18]

In later write‑ups and presentations, they emphasize that headline inflation during the pandemic era was “dominated by price shocks,” but “behind the scenes” an overheated labor market has led to a sustained increase in underlying wage–price inflation that becomes more important as relative price shocks fade.  Their Brookings and PIIE summaries likewise stress that supply disruptions and energy/food shocks explain much of the global inflation surge but that strong aggregate demand—fueled by fiscal and monetary support—helped set the stage.[^21][^22][^19]

### Peer‑reviewed and cross‑country updates

The American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics article (2025) formalizes essentially the same model and message in a peer‑reviewed format: most of the surge reflected price shocks given wages, while labor‑market tightness has more persistent effects that matter for the disinflation phase.  In a follow‑on project with 11 central banks, Bernanke and Blanchard apply and adapt the framework to other advanced economies and conclude that, globally, pandemic‑era inflation was due “primarily to supply disruptions and sharp increases in the prices of food and energy,” with demand playing a varying but significant supporting role across countries.[^23][^22][^21]

Taken at face value, the Bernanke–Blanchard work pushes back against a story in which overheated labor demand is the *primary* proximate driver of 2021–22 inflation, but it is not a clean “60 percent supply” estimate in Shapiro’s sense; commodity and sectoral shocks are themselves partly the result of elevated demand colliding with constrained supply.[^19][^17][^18]

## Shapiro’s PCE decomposition: method and findings

### Sectoral classification of PCE inflation

Adam Shapiro uses sectoral PCE price and quantity data to classify each component as supply‑ or demand‑driven in a given month, based on whether prices and quantities move in the same direction (demand‑driven) or in opposite directions (supply‑driven), and relative to aggregate patterns.  He then aggregates inflation across sectors to construct time series of “supply‑driven” and “demand‑driven” contributions to headline PCE inflation, with a residual “ambiguous” category.[^24][^25][^1]

This approach yields series for the contribution of supply and demand factors to the *gap* between current 12‑month PCE inflation and its pre‑pandemic level, allowing a direct statement about “shares” of excess inflation.[^1][^24]

### 2022 Economic Letter results

In his June 2022 San Francisco Fed Economic Letter, Shapiro reports that supply factors explain “about half” of the gap between current 12‑month PCE inflation and its pre‑pandemic level, while demand factors explain “about a third,” with the remainder due to categories that cannot be clearly assigned.  At that time, he finds that supply‑driven inflation had become more important over 2021–22, while demand‑driven contributions, though initially large in early 2021, were diminishing.[^24][^1]

He quantifies this by noting that supply‑driven inflation was contributing roughly 2.4 percentage points more to headline PCE inflation than pre‑pandemic, while demand‑driven inflation was contributing about 1.4 percentage points more, yielding a 4.8‑percentage‑point gap relative to pre‑COVID norms.  Put differently, in this framework demand accounts for roughly one‑third of the overshoot, supply for about half, and residual factors for the rest.[^13][^1][^24]

### Later refinements and cross‑checks

A subsequent working paper (2024) generalizes this decomposition and confirms that demand‑driven inflation tends to fall in recessions and respond strongly to monetary policy, while supply‑driven inflation tracks oil and global supply‑chain shocks.  An IMF working paper applies Shapiro‑style sectoral decompositions to 32 countries and finds that in the United States and much of Asia, demand‑driven and supply‑driven inflation made roughly equal contributions to the post‑2021 surge, whereas inflation in Europe was more heavily supply‑driven.[^2][^26][^27][^25][^9]

Taken together, Shapiro’s Letter and the IMF cross‑country work suggest that it is reasonable to say supply factors explain on the order of one‑half (not all) of U.S. excess inflation in 2021–22, with demand explaining a sizable share—around one‑third in the original Letter and closer to parity with supply in some cross‑country decompositions that use quarterly data and a broader horizon.[^26][^9][^2][^1]

## Joseph Gagnon and fiscal stimulus

### Gagnon’s qualitative assessment

Joseph Gagnon at PIIE has emphasized that while supply shocks were the “main culprit” for the 2021–23 inflation surge, demand—especially in the United States—“played a supporting role,” making higher policy rates appropriate to cool demand.  He notes that aggressive fiscal support in 2020–21, combined with household savings built up during lockdowns, generated a significant, though not exclusive, demand contribution to inflation.[^7][^28]

A later PIIE briefing on the “worst inflation outbreak in 40 years,” coauthored with other economists, stresses the “centrality of a broad‑based demand shock” in the U.S. and highlights that the American Rescue Plan (ARP) added roughly 8 percent of 2021 GDP in additional fiscal support even as PCE inflation and trimmed‑mean PCE were already moving well above their pre‑pandemic ranges.  That briefing argues that price increases became unusually broad‑based across sectors, consistent with a large aggregate demand impulse interacting with constrained supply.[^8]

### Quantitative work on fiscal contributions

Several papers closely aligned with Gagnon’s perspective quantify fiscal contributions more explicitly. A New York Fed staff report (“Quantifying the Inflationary Impact of Fiscal Stimulus Under Supply Constraints”) finds that, in a calibrated multi‑sector model for December 2019–June 2022, aggregate demand shocks explain roughly two‑thirds of model‑based inflation, and that fiscal stimulus accounts for about half or more of this demand effect.  In this framework, fiscal policy alone is responsible for on the order of one‑third of total inflation over that period, though the authors stress model dependence and uncertainty.[^5][^6]

An Every‑Institute working paper on “Fighting the Pandemic Inflation Surge of 2021–2022” develops a “fiscal quantity theory” in which, under constrained supply, larger deficits translate into higher inflation; using CBO data on the major U.S. pandemic relief programs and their spending paths, the author estimates that between one‑third and two‑thirds of the pandemic inflation shock resulted from excess demand generated by relief expenditures.  A Federal Reserve Board note using cross‑country data finds that U.S. fiscal stimulus likely added about 2.5 percentage points to U.S. inflation through February 2022, with larger fiscal packages associated with larger “excess inflation” across advanced economies.[^29][^3]

Together, this literature—much of it cited and interpreted by Gagnon—suggests that while supply shocks and energy prices were crucial, fiscal stimulus in the U.S. plausibly contributed on the order of 2–3 percentage points to the inflation overshoot, representing perhaps one‑third of the total deviation from target over 2020–22 in model‑based decompositions.[^6][^3][^5][^29][^8]

## IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2022

### Narrative on inflation drivers

The October 2022 WEO describes the global inflation surge as arising from a “mix of supply‑ and demand‑side drivers” as economies recovered from COVID‑19, pointing to pandemic‑related supply chain disruptions, commodity price shocks (especially energy and food), expansive fiscal and monetary support, accumulated savings, and shifts in consumption from services to goods.  The IMF notes that global inflation rose from 4.7 percent in 2021 to an expected 8.8 percent in 2022 before gradually declining, with upside surprises especially pronounced in advanced economies.[^30][^31][^16]

A specific analytical chapter on wage dynamics concludes that “capacity shocks” (supply constraints) primarily drove wages, while “private saving and pent‑up demand” were important for prices, and stresses that both supply shocks and strong demand played roles in pushing inflation to multidecade highs.  The chapter also highlights the risk that prolonged inflation could de‑anchor expectations and argues for tight monetary policy combined with targeted fiscal measures to protect vulnerable households.[^16][^30]

### Quantitative decomposition work

The IMF’s later working paper “Demand vs. Supply Decomposition of Inflation” uses Shapiro‑style sectoral PCE data for 32 countries and finds that after an initial pandemic‑era decline in demand‑driven inflation in 2020, both demand and supply contributions rose sharply from early 2021.  The paper notes that in the United States and Asia, demand‑driven and supply‑driven inflation contributed roughly equally to headline inflation during the surge, whereas in Europe the rise was mostly supply‑driven, reflecting energy dependency and the war in Ukraine.[^9][^2][^26]

That cross‑country perspective reinforces the view that the U.S. was something of an outlier on the demand side—more demand‑driven than Europe, especially because of discretionary fiscal policy—even as global supply shocks set the background.[^2][^26][^16]

## Blanchard’s pre‑emptive warnings and post‑hoc view

### 2021 concerns about ARP size

In his February 18, 2021 PIIE blog “In Defense of Concerns over the \$1.9 Trillion Relief Plan,” Olivier Blanchard argued that the ARP was very large relative to a realistic output gap and pandemic‑constrained potential.  Using Congressional Budget Office estimates, he calculated that the nominal output gap in late 2020 was roughly 900 billion dollars (about 4.2 percent of GDP) and that, after allowing for COVID‑related supply restrictions, the gap that could be safely filled by additional demand in 2021 was smaller still.[^32][^4]

Blanchard stressed that the ARP’s size—about 1.9 trillion dollars, on top of earlier packages—risked “overheating,” meaning demand rising above productive capacity, and warned that this could generate inflation and force the Fed into a sharper tightening cycle than anticipated.  He was careful to say that some overshoot might be acceptable but worried that the magnitude of the package relative to the gap increased the risk that inflation would not be easily reversible.[^4][^33]

### Post‑hoc verdict and link to Bernanke–Blanchard

In his later work with Bernanke, Blanchard effectively revisits these concerns in a structural framework and concludes that the pessimists and optimists were both partly right: there *was* a substantial inflation episode, but its main proximate source was not a classic wage–price spiral; rather, it was a sequence of price shocks layered on an increasingly overheated economy.  His slides summarizing that work note that “headline inflation [was] dominated by price shocks” while “overheating has led to a sustained increase in wage/price inflation,” implying that the ARP‑induced demand impulse probably contributed more to persistence than to the initial spike.[^22][^18][^19]

The Bernanke–Blanchard working paper explicitly cites earlier IMF post‑mortems as highlighting both increased aggregate demand and pandemic‑induced supply constraints, while criticizing some for underplaying the dynamics of commodity shocks and expectations.  Although they do not pin a single percentage of 2021–22 inflation on the ARP, the combination of Blanchard’s 2021 ex ante analysis and the later model‑based decompositions supports the view that U.S. fiscal overkill materially amplified inflation, even if global supply shocks were the dominant spark.[^17][^4][^21][^18]

## Lawrence Summers’s warnings and later record

### February 2021 Washington Post op‑ed

In his February 4, 2021 Washington Post op‑ed, Summers described President Biden’s 1.9 trillion dollar plan—on top of the December 2020 package—as “the boldest act of macroeconomic stabilization policy in U.S. history,” but argued that its scale raised serious risks of inflation and financial instability.  He emphasized that the proposed package was several times larger than estimates of the output gap and that, unlike in 2009, the risk now was not doing too little but doing too much.[^34][^35]

Summers warned that the program could trigger “inflationary pressures of a kind we have not seen in a generation,” force the Fed into sharp tightening, and potentially undermine the dollar’s role if inflation became entrenched.  In subsequent interviews he labeled the policy “the least responsible macroeconomic policy we’ve had in the last 40 years,” precisely because it ignored the possibility that demand would run far ahead of constrained supply.[^36][^37][^34]

### Ex post evaluation

By mid‑2022, many commentators noted that Summers’s inflation warnings had been borne out in qualitative terms: inflation had indeed surged, and the Fed was engaging in rapid tightening.  However, subsequent research, including Bernanke–Blanchard and the IMF work, makes clear that global supply shocks—energy prices, supply chains, and the war in Ukraine—were crucial, and that the euro area saw a comparable or larger inflation surge without anything like the U.S. ARP.[^37][^38][^15][^14][^26][^30][^2]

A fair reading is that Summers correctly anticipated that the ARP’s scale relative to the output gap would create significant demand‑side inflation risk, but he underplayed the central role of global supply shocks in the timing and composition of the 2021–22 inflation surge.  The empirical work suggests that U.S. discretionary fiscal policy meaningfully raised the level and persistence of inflation but did so in an environment where supply shocks would have generated a serious inflation problem even with more modest stimulus.[^35][^26][^3][^5][^34][^6][^29][^21][^2]

## A fair verdict on demand vs. supply and implications for a productive‑capacity framework

### What share is demand vs supply?

Across the key empirical studies, the range of estimates for the demand‑side share of U.S. excess inflation in 2021–22 looks roughly as follows:

- Shapiro’s 2022 Letter: supply ≈ 50 percent, demand ≈ 33 percent, residual ≈ 17 percent of the PCE inflation gap relative to pre‑pandemic norms as of early 2022.[^1][^24]
- IMF’s Shapiro‑style cross‑country work: in the U.S., demand‑ and supply‑driven inflation made “roughly equal” contributions to the post‑2021 surge, implying demand on the order of 50 percent of the excess.[^26][^9][^2]
- Model‑based fiscal studies (New York Fed, EII): aggregate demand shocks explain around two‑thirds of model‑based inflation over December 2019–June 2022, with fiscal stimulus responsible for half or more of that demand effect; this implies fiscal policy alone accounts for perhaps one‑third of total inflation in those models.[^5][^6][^29]
- Fed Board cross‑country note: U.S. fiscal policy contributed about 2.5 percentage points to U.S. inflation through February 2022, which is roughly half of the 4–5 percentage‑point PCE overshoot over that period.[^3][^13]

Bernanke–Blanchard do not give a single “demand share,” but by locating most 2021–22 inflation in price shocks (energy, food, sectoral shortages) rather than labor‑market overheating, they implicitly tilt toward a more “supply‑heavy” reading—while acknowledging that those price shocks themselves partly reflect strong demand hitting constrained supply.  Gagnon and the PIIE authors synthesize this into the view that supply shocks were the main culprit, but demand “probably played a supporting role, especially in the United States.”[^7][^8][^18][^19][^17]

Given the methodological differences, the most defensible summary is a *range*, not a point estimate:

- For **headline U.S. inflation over 2021–22**, supply‑side shocks (energy, food, supply chains, sectoral bottlenecks) likely account for **around half, perhaps slightly more**, of the overshoot relative to a 2 percent PCE target.[^15][^30][^2][^26][^1]
- Demand‑side forces (discretionary fiscal stimulus, monetary accommodation, savings drawdowns, rotation into goods, and subsequent broadening into services) plausibly account for **roughly one‑third to one‑half** of the overshoot, depending on whether one focuses on 2021 alone, the full 2020–22 period, or the evolution into more persistent core inflation.[^27][^6][^29][^2][^26][^3][^5][^1]

A claim that “about 60 percent” of above‑trend inflation is supply‑driven is broadly consistent with Shapiro’s early‑2022 decomposition and with the fact that Europe experienced a largely supply‑driven inflation surge.  But treating that as a precise, universally accepted figure—especially for the U.S.—understates the substantial demand‑side contributions highlighted by the IMF, Fed system research, and fiscal modeling work.[^38][^6][^29][^9][^2][^26][^24][^3][^5][^1]

### Implications for a productive‑capacity (real‑resource) framework

For a book that defends a productive‑capacity, real‑resource view of inflation, the empirical record reinforces the framework but demands sharper specification:

- **Capacity is sectoral and state‑contingent.** The pandemic showed that when supply capacity is constrained and reallocated (e.g., from services to goods) and global supply chains are bottlenecked, the inflationary impact of a given increment of demand is much larger in some sectors than others.  A productive‑capacity framework has to be more granular than a single output gap.[^15][^16][^9]
- **Large fiscal expansions when supply is constrained can be inflationary even with slack in some aggregates.** Blanchard’s 2021 analysis of the ARP, the Fed’s cross‑country note, and the New York Fed and EII modeling all point to the fact that big deficit‑financed transfers in a supply‑constrained environment translate disproportionately into prices.  This is exactly the kind of interaction a real‑resource framework should highlight.[^4][^6][^29][^3][^5]
- **Demand matters for persistence even when supply shocks dominate the initial spike.** Bernanke–Blanchard, the IMF, and Shapiro all find that supply shocks explain much of the initial 2021 jump, but that an overheated labor market and strong demand help explain why inflation became broader and more persistent.[^18][^19][^16][^2][^26][^17][^1]

In that sense, the evidence does *not* vindicate a naïve version of MMT in which large fiscal expansions are costless as long as unemployment is not obviously below a conventional NAIRU. Strong demand played a meaningful role in elevating and prolonging inflation, and several mainstream economists—Blanchard, Summers, Gagnon, and others—flagged this ex ante.[^34][^35][^8][^4][^7]

What the episode *does* vindicate is the importance of starting from real productive capacity and then asking how both demand management and supply shocks move the economy relative to that capacity. The challenge for a modernised productive‑capacity framework is to:

- Incorporate sectoral capacity constraints and supply shocks explicitly (energy, tradables bottlenecks, pandemic‑specific disruptions).[^16][^9][^15]
- Treat large discretionary fiscal packages as powerful shifts in demand whose inflationary impact depends on the contemporaneous state of supply, including health‑related constraints, global input shortages, and energy prices.[^6][^29][^3][^4][^5]
- Recognize that once expectations and wage dynamics adjust, demand and labor‑market tightness become central to bringing inflation back down, as Bernanke–Blanchard and the IMF stress.[^23][^9][^17][^16]

For revising the manuscript, that means:

- Presenting the 60‑percent‑supply claim explicitly as one plausible early‑2022 estimate (Shapiro), not as the settled literature, and juxtaposing it with the IMF’s roughly equal demand–supply contributions for the U.S. and with fiscal modeling that assigns roughly one‑third of the overshoot to fiscal stimulus alone.[^29][^2][^26][^3][^5][^6][^1]
- Acknowledging that while supply shocks were the dominant *spark* globally, U.S. policy choices made the resulting inflation significantly larger and more persistent than it needed to be.[^35][^8][^3][^4][^34][^7]

On that more nuanced reading, the productive‑capacity framework is sharpened, not refuted: it must confront the fact that both pandemic‑era supply constraints and deliberate, unusually large demand injections pushed spending well beyond feasible output, and both sides of that ledger matter for explaining 2021–22.[^9][^2][^26][^17][^3][^5][^6][^16]
