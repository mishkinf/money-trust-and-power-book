The public-choice critique of MMT and discretionary fiscal policy is that once you accept “the government can always print,” the binding constraint is no longer solvency but political will to stop at real capacity—and democratic institutions give you strong, structural reasons to expect systematic overshooting, especially in election years.[^1][^2][^3]

Below is a strongest-form reconstruction of that critique, organized around the authors you specified and then brought to bear directly on MMT.

***

## 1. Buchanan \& Wagner: Democracy in Deficit

### Central claim

Buchanan and Wagner’s core thesis in *Democracy in Deficit* is that the Keynesian revolution destroyed an informal “fiscal constitution” of balanced budgets in normal times and debt only for wars and major capital projects, and replaced it with a technocratic doctrine of “functional finance” that is politically non‑robust.[^4][^3]

Historically, they argue, elected officials operated under a moral and institutional norm that peacetime deficits were exceptional and to be retired quickly; Keynesianism instead taught them that deficits are a legitimate tool to manage demand, promising deficits in recessions and offsetting surpluses in booms.[^2][^4]

The key political-economy claim is that in a democracy with short electoral horizons, you will systematically get the easy half of Keynes and not the hard half: deficits in bad times are politically attractive, but surpluses in good times are not, so the new doctrine biases the system toward chronic deficits, rising debt, and inflation.[^3][^5][^2]

### Institutional mechanism: why discretionary fiscal authority drifts to over‑issuance

Buchanan and Wagner embed this in a simple but powerful institutional story.[^6][^2][^3]

- **Voters and fiscal illusion.** Voters tend to misperceive the cost of deficit finance, underestimating the future tax burden and the inflation tax; deficit spending therefore creates an illusion of “free” benefits relative to current tax-financed spending.[^7][^8][^3]
- **Politicians’ time horizon.** Elected officials maximize electoral prospects over short horizons; they discount costs that fall on future voters (and future officeholders) relative to benefits that materialize before the next election.[^5][^3]
- **Asymmetry of policy.** Tax cuts and spending increases have concentrated, salient beneficiaries; tax increases and spending cuts impose diffuse, politically painful losses. In a Keynesian framework that blesses deficits as “scientific,” the path of least resistance is to run deficits in recessions and merely *avoid* offsetting surpluses in booms, not to actively engineer them.[^4][^2][^5]
- **Intergenerational externalization.** Deficit finance allows the current electorate to shift some of the burden of today’s spending onto future taxpayers who do not yet vote, undermining the intergenerational ethic that had previously supported balanced budgets.[^1][^3][^4]

From this, “money‑financed deficits in political democracy” (their phrase) are not a neutral technical choice but an institutional hazard: once politicians internalize that they need not balance budgets year‑by‑year, the electoral calculus produces deficits larger and more persistent than a benevolent‑planner Keynesian model would prescribe.[^2][^3]

In election years specifically, the logic is ruthless: discretionary fiscal power plus electoral competition and fiscal illusion give strong incentives to pull forward spending and postpone consolidation, especially if monetary financing is available to dull the immediate tax signal.[^9][^1]

## 2. Buchanan on monetary rules vs discretion

### Constitutional perspective: rules as political, not just technical

In his Nobel lecture “The Constitution of Economic Policy,” Buchanan argues that economists must stop giving advice as if they served a benevolent despot and instead analyze policy within realistic political institutions, modeling policymakers as self‑interested “homo economicus” at the constitutional level.[^10][^11]

Constitutional economics, in his sense, studies the *choice of rules*—tax and monetary constitutions that constrain day‑to‑day political actors—not the optimal one‑shot policy of a mythical social planner.[^12][^13]

From this vantage point:

- **Discretionary policy** is dangerous because it invites time‑inconsistent behavior: once expectations are formed, policymakers face strong temptations to renege on prior commitments (e.g., use surprise inflation or deficits to extract resources).[^14][^15]
- **Rules**—whether balanced-budget norms, a gold standard, or Friedman’s $k$-percent money-growth rule—are justified less by their theoretical optimality in a frictionless model and more by their ability to bind self‑interested officials to predictable behavior and remove temptation.[^16][^17][^14]

Buchanan and collaborators explicitly defend Friedman-style monetary rules or even commodity standards on *political-economy* grounds: discretionary central banking allows governments to generate seigniorage and off‑budget transfers in a way that is too tempting for real-world politicians and too opaque for voters, so you should constitutionally pre‑commit to a non‑manipulable rule even if a flexible discretionary regime could in principle do better.[^15][^12][^14]

In short, the case for rules is: assume the worst about political incentives, then design monetary and fiscal constitutions that minimize the damage; do not assume a wise technocrat will “just stop printing” when inflation risks arise.[^10][^3]

## 3. Niskanen, Tullock, and public-choice fiscal politics

### Niskanen’s bureaucracy: systematic upward pressure on spending

William Niskanen’s *Bureaucracy and Representative Government* supplies the micro-foundations of an overspending bias on the administrative side.[^18]

His “budget-maximizing bureaucrat” model posits that:

- Senior bureaucrats derive utility from larger budgets because these correlate with salary, staff, prestige, and influence.[^19][^18]
- Agencies enjoy **information asymmetry**: they know their production technologies and costs better than legislators, and can strategically overstate benefits or understate costs to secure larger appropriations.[^20][^21][^18]
- Given this, bureaus tend to be “too large, grow too fast, use too much capital, and exploit their sponsor,” leading to spending above the level that a fully informed legislature would choose.[^22][^21][^18]

Empirical work has softened the starkness of Niskanen’s original model, but the core insight—that bureaucratic incentives plus information asymmetry create persistent expansionary pressure on budgets—remains central in public-choice treatments of the state.[^22][^19]

### Tullock, logrolling, and electoral incentives

Gordon Tullock and Buchanan in *The Calculus of Consent* and related work add electoral and legislative mechanisms:

- **Logrolling and pork-barrel politics.** Legislators trade votes on localized spending projects whose benefits are concentrated while costs are spread thinly over the national tax base; this produces a level of aggregate spending and deficit finance higher than any individual voter would choose under full information.[^16][^3]
- **Rational ignorance.** Because each individual’s share of aggregate fiscal costs is small, voters rationally underinvest in monitoring complex budgetary choices, enabling politicians to use deficits and monetary finance without triggering effective electoral penalties.[^3][^16]

In this framework, an election-year government with access to central-bank financing has multiple reinforcing incentives to “print past capacity”:

- It can fund tax cuts and spending programs that boost short‑term incomes of pivotal constituencies, with inflationary costs arriving later and being hard to attribute.[^4][^2]
- Bureaucracies will not internalize the macro constraint; they push for their own budgetary expansions regardless of aggregate capacity, trusting macro authorities to manage inflation ex post.[^21][^18]

The overall public-choice narrative is thus a multi-level pressure toward deficits and, when money creation is available, toward over‑issuance: voters, politicians, and bureaucrats each have reasons to favor more spending now and to underweight the inflation and tax consequences later.

## 4. Modern restatements: Sargent–Wallace and Cochrane

### Sargent \& Wallace: unpleasant monetarist arithmetic

“Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic” (1981) formalizes the intuition that monetary policy cannot independently control inflation if fiscal policy is exogenous and deficit-biased.[^23][^24]

In their model:

- The **fiscal authority** chooses an exogenous path for real primary deficits and finances them by issuing interest-bearing debt and by seigniorage (money creation).[^25][^23]
- The **monetary authority** controls the split between debt and money, but the consolidated government is subject to an intertemporal budget constraint: the present value of future primary surpluses plus seigniorage must equal the real value of outstanding debt.[^24][^23][^25]

From this simple arithmetic they derive two key results:

1. If the fiscal authority runs persistent primary deficits with no credible future adjustment, the central bank cannot forever restrain money growth; attempting “tighter money now” (less current seigniorage) simply forces **more seigniorage later**, yielding higher future inflation—the “unpleasant” conclusion that tighter money today can mean higher inflation eventually under fiscal dominance.[^23][^25][^24]
2. In such a regime, the **long-run rate of inflation is pinned down by fiscal policy**, not by the monetary authority’s desire; central bank independence is largely illusory without a supportive fiscal regime.[^26][^27][^25]

This is essentially Buchanan–Wagner plus accounting: if democratic politics produces an exogenous, deficit-biased fiscal path, monetary restraint cannot ultimately prevent inflationary erosion of debt; the only way out is to change the fiscal regime itself.[^25][^1]

### Cochrane: fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL)

Cochrane’s *The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* restates and extends this logic in the language of asset pricing and DSGE macro.[^28][^29]

The central FTPL equation is:

- The **real value of nominal government debt** today equals the present value of expected future primary surpluses (discounted by appropriate real discount rates). If expected surpluses fall short, the price level must jump or trend upward so that the real value of outstanding liabilities adjusts down.[^29][^28]

Key implications Cochrane emphasizes:

- **Deficits do not mechanically cause inflation**, but large deficits unbacked by credible future surpluses must be offset either by explicit default or by inflation, which is de facto a real default.[^30][^28][^29]
- Monetary policy (interest-rate rules, QE, etc.) can control inflation only in regimes where fiscal authorities adjust surpluses to validate the chosen nominal path. Without such backing, attempts to raise interest rates can even be inflationary—essentially a fiscal analogue of Sargent–Wallace’s unpleasant arithmetic.[^31][^29][^25]
- He explicitly connects his framework to Sargent–Wallace’s “unpleasant arithmetic” and to debates over fiscal dominance, arguing that post‑2008 and Covid-era large deficits make fiscal backing central for understanding inflation outcomes.[^32][^31][^29]

From a public-choice perspective, FTPL is the formalization of the Buchanan worry: if your political institutions are such that expected primary surpluses will *not* rise to match rising debt—because voters and politicians resist tax hikes and spending cuts—then the constraint operates through the price level instead. The more credible it becomes that the political system will not generate future surpluses, the more today’s price level must adjust.[^28][^29][^25]

An MMT world, where bond markets and solvency are officially declared non‑constraints and deficits are normalized, is—on Cochrane’s reading—a regime that makes the FTPL channel more salient: inflation becomes the main equilibrating variable when expectations of future primary surpluses are politically implausible.

## 5. Kelton’s *Deficit Myth* and the missing public-choice response

### What Kelton argues

Kelton’s *The Deficit Myth* sets out the popular MMT case: for a monetarily sovereign government (like the U.S.), **financial constraints do not bind** in the way household analogies suggest, because such a government cannot run out of its own currency.[^33][^34][^35]

Core propositions include:

- The federal government is a **currency issuer**, not a user; it spends currency into existence and taxes it back; taxes are not needed to “fund” spending but to create demand for the currency and manage inflation.[^34][^33]
- Budget deficits are not proof of overspending; they are the mirror image of private-sector surpluses and can be economically beneficial.[^35][^33]
- The **true constraint** on spending is not solvency but real resources and inflation: “inflation is evidence of overspending,” whereas the mere size of the deficit is not.[^36][^33][^34]
- Accordingly, questions like “How will we pay for it?” misframe debates over public investment; the right question is whether there is enough real capacity (labor, materials, technology) to absorb the spending without inflation.[^37][^33][^36]

On inflation control, she argues that governments can and should use **tax policy and other tools** to withdraw spending power if inflation emerges, for example by raising taxes on the wealthy, cutting certain outlays, or using targeted measures, while continuing to maintain full employment via programs like a Job Guarantee.[^38][^33][^36]

Politically, Kelton spends considerable time attacking what she views as the “austerity myth”—the idea that deficits are inherently dangerous and that public spending must be tightly constrained—which in her telling has led governments to underinvest and embrace premature austerity even in deep recessions.[^39][^40][^37]

### What she does not engage

Two points are critical for the public-choice critique:

1. **No engagement with Buchanan/Wagner or public choice.** The Deficit Myth is written as a popular macro-fiscal polemic; in the book text and surrounding discussion there is no substantive engagement with Buchanan, Wagner, Niskanen, or the public-choice literature on deficit bias and electoral incentives.[^41][^7][^33]

Her discussion of politics focuses mainly on how “deficit myths” and partisan obstruction (e.g. Mitch McConnell’s opposition to further stimulus in 2010) prevented adequate deficit-financed stimulus, not on how election-year incentives might push spending *beyond* capacity.[^37][^39]
2. **Strong institutional assumptions, largely undefended.** Kelton’s normative program presupposes institutional properties that public-choice theorists regard as highly non‑robust:
    - That Congress and the executive will be willing to **raise taxes or cut popular programs promptly** when inflationary pressures appear, even in election years and even when those measures hurt powerful constituencies.[^33][^38]
    - That policymakers can reliably **diagnose slack vs. capacity** and distinguish “good” (non‑inflationary) deficits from “bad” (inflationary) ones in real time, despite uncertainty and data lags.[^34][^33]
    - That bureaucracies administering expanded programs (e.g. a Job Guarantee) will not exhibit Niskanen-style budget-maximizing behavior that ratchets spending upward regardless of macro conditions.[^18][^21][^19]
    - That the electorate will tolerate being told that inflation control requires higher taxes or reduced nominal income growth, rather than blaming central banks or “greedy corporations,” and will not punish the first government that tries to implement such measures.[^36][^38]

Where critics explicitly raise these issues, MMT responses often emphasize that inflation is the real constraint and that governments must be “responsible” in using their fiscal capacity, but they rarely propose concrete constitutional or incentive-compatible mechanisms to ensure that responsibility in the face of electoral pressure.[^42][^9][^38]

From a Buchanan perspective, this is precisely the missing step: MMT replaces a *false* financial constraint (solvency fear) with a *true* real-resource constraint (inflation), but then simply asserts that democratic politics will respect that real constraint through wise discretionary management—reverting to the benevolent-planner model Buchanan’s constitutional economics was designed to reject.[^13][^10][^1]

## 6. Fair verdict and a credible architecture for fiscal flexibility

### What chartalism/MMT get right—and owe to public choice

Chartalist/MMT insights that should be taken seriously—even by their critics—include:

- The recognition that a sovereign government’s **nominal budget constraint is different** from that of households or currency users; solvency in its own currency is not the immediate constraint.[^38][^33][^34]
- The emphasis that **real resources and inflation**, not arbitrary deficit targets, should anchor fiscal debates, and that the “household budget” metaphor has been used ideologically to justify austerity that is neither necessary nor efficient.[^40][^33]
- The focus on the **state’s taxing power** and legal structures as central to the value of money, which resonates with both Adam Smith’s remarks and with modern FTPL’s emphasis on the present value of primary surpluses.[^29][^28][^33]

From a public-choice standpoint, these are important corrections to naive bond‑market fatalism and to narratives that treat debt ratios as exogenous destiny. But they raise, rather than resolve, Buchanan’s central question: if you remove the solvency constraint and normalize monetary finance, what *political* mechanism enforces the real-resource constraint?

In that sense, MMT “owes” the public-choice tradition a serious institutional theory:

- It must explain why democratic actors facing short horizons, fiscal illusion, bureaucratic budget-maximization, and logrolling will voluntarily stop spending or raise taxes at the point where inflation risk becomes material.[^18][^1][^3]
- It must reconcile its call for expansive, permanent programs with FTPL’s requirement that expectations of future primary surpluses be credible if inflation is to remain low and stable.[^28][^29][^25]

Without that, public-choice theorists will reasonably infer that an MMT-informed polity is one in which the unpleasant arithmetic and fiscal-theory channels are more, not less, likely to bite.

### Toward a “Buchanan-compatible” fiscal-flexibility regime

If you start from Buchanan and Niskanen but accept that some degree of active fiscal stabilization and monetary sovereignty is desirable, what kind of institutional architecture would make discretionary fiscal power least dangerous?

A strong version might combine:

1. **Constitutional fiscal rules with built-in symmetry.**
    - Embed a rule that allows automatic deficits in downturns and automatic surpluses in booms, keyed to observable indicators (e.g., unemployment rate, output gap), so that the Keynesian prescription is symmetric rather than one‑sided.[^43][^1][^2]
    - Make these rules *constitutional* or quasi-constitutional, as Buchanan advocated, to reduce the scope for ad hoc election-year relaxation.[^5][^10][^3]
2. **An independent fiscal council defining “fiscal space.”**
    - Create an institution analogous to an independent central bank—a fiscal council that estimates sustainable non‑inflationary fiscal space, given real capacity and FTPL constraints, and publishes binding or at least politically focal “fiscal envelopes” for discretionary spending.[^15][^29][^28]
    - Make it costly for politicians to exceed these envelopes (e.g., through automatic triggers or supermajority requirements).
3. **Automatic inflation-contingent tax and transfer adjustments.**
    - Pre‑legislate tax and transfer schedules that automatically tighten when inflation exceeds a target—e.g., temporary VAT surcharges, phaseouts of transfers, or indexed excises—so that inflation control does not depend on discretionary tax hikes that are politically suicidal in election years.[^26][^25]
    - This is the fiscal analogue of Kydland–Prescott “rules vs discretion,” transforming the inflation constraint into a rule-based response rather than a heroic assumption about political will.[^14][^15]
4. **Bureaucratic reforms to blunt budget-maximization.**
    - Introduce competing service providers, sunset clauses, and performance-based budgeting to reduce Niskanen-style bias toward ever-larger agencies whose budgets are treated as baseline entitlements.[^21][^19][^18]
    - Make program continuation periodically conditional on independent evaluation of effectiveness and macro‑consistency.
5. **Explicit recognition of FTPL constraints in debt management.**
    - Require that any substantial, persistent rise in the debt-to-GDP ratio be accompanied by an explicit primary-surplus plan (e.g., phased-in tax measures) that is credible enough to anchor expectations, rather than assuming markets will always roll over debt at low rates.[^29][^25][^28]
    - Treat inflation as a politically unacceptable form of default, not an unmentioned backstop.

Under such an architecture, one could imagine a “fiscal-flexibility” position that uses MMT’s descriptive insights—sovereign currency, ability to run deficits in downturns, rejection of arbitrary deficit ceilings—but acknowledges and tries to neutralize Buchanan–Niskanen incentives via rules, automatic stabilizers, and independent institutions.

This would answer the core question in your Chapter 13 form: yes, governments can print; what stops them from printing past capacity in election years is *not* the goodwill or wisdom of current office‑holders, but a prior constitutional and institutional design that makes such overshooting both difficult and politically costly.

On that standard, *The Deficit Myth* is incomplete: it powerfully dismantles bad metaphors about deficits and highlights the real-resource constraint, but it largely sidesteps the public-choice problem of how actual democratic institutions behave when given the discretionary power to “just print,” and thus does not yet offer the institutional architecture that a fully worked‑out chartalist/MMT framework would need to be politically credible.[^39][^33][^1]
