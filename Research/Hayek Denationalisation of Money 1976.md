# 1. Hayek, *Denationalisation of Money* (1976)

## Core critique of discretionary central banking

- “The root and source of all monetary evil is the government’s monopoly on money.” (Hayek, *Denationalisation of Money – The Argument Refined*, Hobart Paper Special 70, IEA, often cited from later editions as p. 18 or p. 29 depending on pagination).[web:39][web:41]
- Hayek argues that government control over currency has been “regularly used to exploit and defraud” the public, and that the long history of inflation under political control contrasts with the relative stability under the classical gold standard.[web:28][web:34][web:51]

## Proposed alternative: competitive private currency issuance (summary)

Hayek proposes removing the state’s monopoly over money issuance and allowing private institutions (primarily banks) to issue their own redeemable currencies in open competition, with users free to choose which currencies to hold and transact in; issuers whose currencies lose purchasing power would see those currencies rejected, while those who maintain stable value would gain market share, creating a rule-like discipline through competition instead of discretionary central bank management.[web:19][web:20][web:24]

## Metallic constraints and their failure by the 1970s

- Hayek characterizes the gold standard as a useful but imperfect discipline: “Though gold is an anchor — and any anchor is better than a money left to the discretion of government — it is a very wobbly anchor.” (typically cited as *Denationalisation of Money*, p. 106 in later IEA/Cato printings).[web:45][web:49]
- He argues that once the classical gold standard was abandoned and convertibility suspended, metallic constraints no longer provided an effective check, leaving price-level stability entirely to discretionary policy which repeatedly failed to prevent inflation and cyclical instability.[web:35][web:48]


# 2. Ammous, *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018)

## Hard money and time preference – key quotations

- Ammous defines hard money via stock-to-flow and links it directly to intertemporal choice: “The relative difficulty of producing new monetary units determines the hardness of money: money whose supply is hard to increase is known as hard money, while easy money is money whose supply is amenable to large increases.” (Ch. 1, “Money”, around p. 5 in the Wiley hardback).[web:58]
- On the civilizational role of hard money and time preference: “Sound money allows people to think about the long term and to save and invest more for the future. Saving and investing for the long run are the key to capital accumulation and the advance of human civilization.” (Prologue / Part II introduction, often cited around pp. xvii–xviii).[web:58][web:59]

## Historical claim about gold-standard-era flourishing (summary)

Ammous argues that the classical gold standard era, particularly the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, was characterized by low and stable prices, strong capital accumulation, rapid industrial and technological progress, and flourishing arts and architecture; he labels this period, especially the “Belle Époque”, as perhaps “the greatest period for human flourishing,” contending that the hardness of gold-based money lowered time preference and enabled long-horizon investment and cultural achievement.[web:59][web:65][web:79]

## Bitcoin’s monetary properties and deflationary case (summary)

In the later chapters on digital money, Ammous presents Bitcoin as “digital hard money” whose supply is capped at 21 million coins by protocol, with a pre-programmed halving schedule that makes it the first instance of absolute, credibly enforced scarcity in monetary history; he claims this fixed cap prevents political inflation, aligns Bitcoin with and even surpasses gold on hardness, and argues that a mildly deflationary money with reliably increasing purchasing power over time will lower time preference, encourage saving and capital accumulation, and thus improve economic and social outcomes.[web:68][web:70][web:71][web:75][web:76]


# 3. Selgin, *Less Than Zero* (1997)

## Productivity-norm quote

- Selgin’s core statement of the productivity norm: under a properly designed monetary rule, “the price level should be allowed to vary to reflect changes in goods’ unit costs of production,” so that improvements in productivity show up as gently falling prices rather than being offset by deliberate monetary expansion.[page:1]
- In later summaries he emphasizes that a productivity norm implies “a falling price level, at least when the economy is growing,” because real output rising faster than the money stock should generate benign deflation rather than being sterilized.[web:3][web:6][page:1]

## Good versus bad deflation (summary)

Selgin distinguishes “good” deflation, arising when real output or productivity grows faster than the money stock so that prices fall while real wages and profits are maintained or rising, from “bad” deflation, which results from shocks to money demand or supply that cause nominal spending to contract and idle resources.[web:81][web:90][web:91] Under a productivity norm or nominal income targeting regime, monetary policy should accommodate changes in money demand to prevent demand-driven deflation, while allowing price-level declines that mirror productivity gains, arguing that only the latter conveys useful information and avoids unnecessary unemployment.[web:81][web:86][page:1]


# 4. Bitcoin empirical record

## Volatility levels

- Long-run measures typically place Bitcoin’s annualized volatility in a very high range relative to traditional assets: one decade-long analysis finds average annual volatility around 46–70% for Bitcoin, versus roughly 15–20% for major equity indices such as the S&P 500, and below 10% for gold.[web:83][web:87] Even in the more recent, somewhat “matured” period, estimates for Bitcoin’s annualized volatility around 2024–2025 remain in the mid-30% range, several times that of broad equity or FX benchmarks.[web:83][web:87]

## Major drawdowns: 2013, 2017–18, 2021–22

- 2013–2015 cycle: Bitcoin peaked in late 2013 and subsequently lost on the order of 80–90% of its value, with estimates of the peak-to-trough drawdown in this cycle around −87%.[web:84][web:88]
- 2017–2018 cycle: After the December 2017 all‑time high, Bitcoin fell roughly −80 to −83% over about a year, with one systematic drawdown table listing an −83% decline over 360 days from December 19, 2017 to the market bottom.[web:92][web:93]
- 2021–2022 cycle: From the November 10, 2021 peak, Bitcoin fell approximately −75 to −77%, with multiple sources converging on a −77% peak‑to‑trough drawdown over roughly one year.[web:84][web:88][web:92]

## Use as transaction medium vs. speculative/portfolio asset

- Surveys of central banks in 2022 found that major cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ethereum were “hardly used by anyone” for day‑to‑day consumer or business payments, being classified as “trivial/no use” or at most used by small niche groups, with usage concentrated in remittances rather than retail transactions.[web:96]
- A 2026 user survey across more than 5,700 Bitcoin holders reported that only around 12–18% of respondents used cryptocurrency for everyday or weekly payments, with most users rarely or never spending their holdings; barriers cited included merchant acceptance, fees, volatility, and fraud risk.[web:103]

(For on‑chain breakdowns between merchant payments and exchange/speculative flows, current public data tend to show centralized exchanges and trading venues receiving the majority of Bitcoin volume, with merchant‑service and payment processors handling a far smaller share, though precise splits vary by methodology and period.)[web:109]

## El Salvador’s Bitcoin legal‑tender experiment

- El Salvador adopted Bitcoin as legal tender in September 2021, launching the state-backed Chivo wallet and offering incentives; subsequent nationally representative survey and blockchain‑based analysis found that Bitcoin usage as a medium of exchange remained very limited, with adoption concentrated among younger, banked men and low ongoing use by firms and households after initial incentives expired.[web:89][web:85]
- As of 2024–2025, the U.S. dollar continues to dominate daily transactions (on the order of 85–90% of activity), while Bitcoin plays niche roles: estimates suggest around 1–5% of remittances are routed through crypto, Chivo processes perhaps $7–10 million per month, and nationwide only roughly 15% of businesses accept Bitcoin, with much higher acceptance in tourism hotspots such as El Zonte.[web:94]
- Government holdings: public trackers estimate that El Salvador’s government has accumulated roughly 6,100–6,200 BTC, corresponding to hundreds of millions of dollars at recent prices, and continues small, steady purchases (e.g., “1 bitcoin per day”) despite pausing large headline buys to accommodate IMF negotiations.[web:94]


# 5. Crypto failure cases (brief notes)

- **Mt. Gox (2014).** Once the largest Bitcoin exchange, Mt. Gox suspended withdrawals and filed for bankruptcy in early 2014 after disclosing that roughly 850,000 bitcoins (around 7% of all coins then outstanding), worth about $470–500 million at the time, had gone missing, most likely stolen gradually from its hot wallet over several years.[web:97][web:104]
- **Terra/Luna (May 2022).** The TerraUSD (UST) algorithmic stablecoin attempted to maintain its dollar peg via a burn‑and‑mint arbitrage mechanism with the LUNA token and subsidized demand through the Anchor protocol’s high yields; a loss of confidence and large redemptions in May 2022 triggered a classic “death spiral” in which UST lost its peg and hyper‑inflationary LUNA issuance drove both tokens toward zero, wiping out on the order of $40–60 billion in market value.[web:98][web:105]
- **Celsius and Voyager (mid‑2022).** Celsius Network, a large centralized crypto lender advertising double‑digit yields, froze withdrawals in June 2022 amid liquidity stress and subsequently filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, leaving customers’ deposits locked inside the estate; Voyager Digital, a crypto broker with concentrated exposure to failing counterparties, likewise halted withdrawals and entered bankruptcy proceedings in July 2022.[web:99][web:106]
- **FTX (Nov. 2022).** The FTX group of exchanges collapsed in November 2022 after a run on deposits exposed massive misuse of customer assets; internal estimates and subsequent court filings indicated an $8–9 billion shortfall in customer funds, with affiliated trading firm Alameda Research having borrowed and lost much of the customer money.[web:100][web:107]
- **Silvergate and Signature (March 2023).** Silvergate Bank, a California institution heavily focused on crypto firms, saw roughly 68–70% of its deposits (about $8 billion) run off in late 2022 after the broader crypto downturn and FTX collapse, leading to voluntary liquidation in March 2023; Signature Bank, another major bank with substantial crypto‑related deposits, experienced a rapid run of more than $10 billion and was closed by regulators on March 12, 2023, with its crypto deposit franchise wound down in receivership.[web:101][web:108]


# 6. Institutionalist parallel: rule‑bound vs. discretionary regimes

## Hayek / Buchanan on rules vs. discretion

- Hayek’s monetary constitutionalism stresses that the problem with modern central banking is not merely technical miscalculation but the lack of binding rules: he argues that inflation is “quite simple: excessive growth in the supply of money,” and that relying on the discretionary judgment of monetary authorities invites political pressures and recurrent abuse.[web:48][web:51]
- In *Choice in Currency* and *Denationalisation of Money*, Hayek argues that a regime of competing private currencies or at least strict rule‑based constraints on money issuance would better align incentives than discretionary central bank management, framing the issue explicitly as one of institutional design rather than policy fine‑tuning.[web:34][web:51][web:49]

## Selgin on a “monetary constitution”

- Selgin’s advocacy of a productivity norm and, in some writings, free banking, is explicitly framed as a call for a monetary constitution: a set of general, predictable rules (such as nominal income targeting implemented through automatic mechanisms or decentralized note issue) that constrain authorities ex ante, rather than discretionary attempts to stabilize short‑run macro variables.[page:1][web:91]
- Later summaries of his work emphasize that the point is not deflation per se but rule‑bound monetary arrangements under which benign, productivity‑driven price declines are permitted while demand‑driven collapses in spending are offset, thus aligning modern central banking with an institutional framework closer to the classical gold standard’s rule‑like discipline.[web:3][web:86][web:91]
