BANK OF ENGLAND BALANCE SHEET — Before Loan to Government

### Figure 5.2 — The Money-Creation Cycle, 1694

**Placement:** After the section "How Money Multiplied," before "Why It Worked Domestically: Taxation Creates Demand."

**Specification (flow diagram, 7 stages):**

1. **Government needs £1.2 million** for war finance.
2. **Bank of England creates the loan** by booking matched entries: Asset (Loan to Government £1.2M) | Liability (Notes Issued £1.2M).
3. **Government spends the notes** into the economy: soldiers' wages, military supplies, diplomatic payments, naval construction.
4. **Notes circulate** privately: merchants → workers → landlords → shopkeepers → farmers.
5. **Taxation creates demand**: everyone needs notes to discharge land tax, customs, window tax, excise.
6. **Taxes return notes** to the government; government pays the Bank interest on the loan.
7. **Cycle repeats**: government spends notes back into the economy.

**Caption:** "Money was created by spending, not by collecting. Taxation did not fund the cycle — it sustained demand for the money the cycle had already created."

### Figure 5.3 — Two-Tier Banking: Bank of England and the Country Banks

**Placement:** In the section "The Two-Tier System: Base Money and Private Money," replacing the inline text diagram.

**Specification:**

- Top tier: **Bank of England** — issues base money; only its notes are accepted by the Exchequer for tax payment.
- Middle tier: **Country banks** (Somerset, Birmingham, Manchester) — issue their own notes, convertible on demand into Bank of England notes.
- Bottom tier: **Gold reserves** held by the Bank of England against its own notes.

Lines connect each country bank up to the Bank of England, labeled "convertibility on demand." A separate line connects the Bank of England down to gold reserves, labeled "redeemable in coin."

**Caption:** "Country bank notes circulated because they could be converted into Bank of England notes — the only paper accepted at the Exchequer for taxes. When country banks lost the ability to convert, their notes collapsed in value."

## Chapter 6: Newton's Accident

### Figure 6.1 — The Global Spread of the Gold Standard, 1717–1900
**Placement:** After "The unintended consequence" section.
**Specification:** World map with adoption-date color coding: dark blue (Britain 1717–1821), medium blue (Germany 1871, U.S. 1873, Scandinavia 1873–1875), light blue (Russia, Japan, Austria-Hungary, India 1890s), gray (China and parts of Latin America, never fully adopted). Arrows show influence radiating outward from London. Annotations call out 1871 Germany cascade, the U.S. "Crime of 1873," and Japan/Russia 1897.
**Caption:** "Adoption radiated outward from London because access to British capital required gold backing. By 1900 nearly every major economy except China was on it, treating an accidental English standard as natural law."

### Figure 6.2 — The Gold Points Mechanism
**Placement:** In the "gold points mechanism" section.
**Specification:** Vertical band chart with the official $4.8665/£ parity in the middle, gold export point at $4.90 above (annotated "ship gold to London → arbitrage profitable"), gold import point at $4.83 below (annotated "ship gold from London → arbitrage profitable"). Side notes: shipping + insurance roughly 0.5–1 percent; transit time several weeks by steamship.
**Caption:** "Shipping costs created narrow bands around the official parity. Within those bands the exchange rate floated; beyond them, gold moved. The 'automatic' adjustment depended on private arbitrage, stable politics, and reliable shipping — and required active central bank management whenever any of those broke."

## Chapter 7: Paper Proves Itself

### Figure 7.1 — Wheat Prices and General Price Level, 1790–1824
**Placement:** In the section that originally held the inline price-data block.
**Specification:** Two-panel chart. Top panel: wheat shillings/quarter, 1790–1824, with shaded region 1797–1821 marked "Bank Restriction (paper money)." Key data points: 1790 = 54s; 1795 = 75.2s; 1797 = 53.9s; 1800 = 113.1s; 1813 = 109.9s; 1815 = 65.7s; 1821 = 56.1s; 1822 = 44.7s; 1824 = 63.9s. Bottom panel: decadal price index, 1790s = 100, with 1800s = 134, 1810s = 150, 1820s = 123.
**Caption:** "Cumulative inflation across the 24-year Bank Restriction was only 22.3 percent (about 1.2 percent annually). The deflation that followed gold resumption in 1821 was sharper than the wartime inflation it was meant to reverse."
**Sources:** Clark (2005); O'Brien & Palma (2020).

## Chapter 8: The Great Forgetting

### Figure 8.1 — Notes vs. Deposits: Functionally Similar Money
**Placement:** In the "Peel's Tragic Error" section.
**Specification:** Two-column comparison. Left column: "Bank Note (visible)" — physical paper, promise to pay on demand, bearer transferable, accepted in settlement, created when a bank lends, **regulated by the 1844 Act**. Right column: "Bank Deposit (less visible)" — ledger entry, promise to pay on demand, transferable by check, accepted in settlement, created when a bank lends, **not directly constrained by the note-issue rule**. Footnote: by 1900, English payments by value were overwhelmingly settled through deposits and checks rather than notes; exact denominator pending final source check.
**Caption:** "The 1844 Act meticulously regulated the visible form of bank money while the less visible form came to dominate. The legal form changed; the monetary function endured."

## Chapter 9: The Interwar Catastrophe

### Figure 9.1 — Gold-Standard Exit and Recovery, 1929–1937
**Placement:** After the "Roosevelt's revolution" section.
**Specification:** Line graph, real GDP indexed to 1929 = 100, six countries, 1929–1937. Vertical markers showing each country's date of leaving gold. Color coding: green for early leavers (Britain, Sweden, Japan in 1931), yellow for mid leavers (U.S. March 1933), red for late leavers (France September 1936). Key endpoints by 1937: Britain +16.4%, U.S. +5.3%, Germany +17%, France still below 1929. Annotation: "Each year of delay cost roughly 5–7% of growth."
**Caption:** "Countries that abandoned gold earlier recovered faster. The gold standard transmitted the Depression by forcing adjustment through prices, wages, and output."
**Sources:** Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters* (1992).

## Chapter 12: The Age of Pure Fiat

### Figure 12.1 — Federal Reserve Balance Sheet, 2007–2022
**Placement:** Replaces the inline ASCII chart in the QE section.
**Specification:** Area chart, Fed total assets in trillions of dollars, 2007–2022. Key inflection points: Dec 2007 = $0.9T; Dec 2008 = $2.2T (Bear/Lehman/TARP); Dec 2014 = $4.5T (QE3 ends); Dec 2019 = $4.2T (pre-COVID); Dec 2020 = $7.4T (COVID response); Jun 2022 = $9.0T (peak); Dec 2022 = $8.6T (QT begins). Secondary axis: % of GDP (6.05% pre-crisis → 34.67% peak). Shaded bands show QE1, QE2, QE3, COVID response. Inflation overlay: 2010–2014 average = 1.4% (low); 2022 peak = 9.1%.
**Caption:** "The same monetary tool, opposite results. The 2008–2014 expansion ($3.6T) caused minimal inflation because capacity was idle. The 2020–2022 expansion ($4.7T) hit binding supply constraints. The constraint is real resources, not money itself."
**Sources:** FRED series WALCL; Federal Reserve H.4.1; BEA GDP series.

### Figure 12.2 — Money vs. Real Resources: Three Scenarios
**Placement:** In the "Real Constraint" section.
**Specification:** Three-panel illustration. Panel 1: "Below Capacity" — concert hall with 100 seats, 70 people, 30 empty seats; government adds 30 tickets, fills hall, no inflation. Panel 2: "At Full Capacity" — 100 seats, 100 people; 50 extra tickets bid up seat prices; inflation. Panel 3: "Capacity Destroyed" — burning concert hall, only 20 seats remain, 100 tickets outstanding; hyperinflation. Below panels, contrast: "Gold standard = arbitrary ticket limit unrelated to seats. Fiat = tickets adjustable to match available seats."
**Caption:** "Money is the ticket. Real resources are the seats. The constraint is always seats — not tickets."
